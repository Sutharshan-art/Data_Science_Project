**Territories of Poland Annexed by the Soviet Union**

**Historical Background**
In September 1939, Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union executed the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, a non-aggression treaty containing a secret protocol that divided Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence. Under this arrangement, on 17 September 1939, Soviet forces invaded eastern Poland—then the Second Polish Republic—pursuant to claims of protecting Ukrainian and Belarusian minorities. This invasion followed Germany’s attack from the west on 1 September, effectively partitioning Poland between the two powers.

**1939 Annexations**
By early October 1939, the Red Army had occupied roughly 200,000 square kilometers of eastern Polish territory. On 2 November 1939, the Soviet government formally incorporated these areas into the Ukrainian and Byelorussian Soviet Socialist Republics (SSR). The annexed regions included five Polish voivodeships in full or in part: Wilno (Vilnius), Nowogródek, Polesie, Lwów (Lviv), and Stanisławów (Ivano-Frankivsk). Major cities absorbed into the USSR included Lwów, Wilno, Grodno, and Białystok, alongside vast rural districts.

**Administrative and Political Integration**
Following annexation, Soviet authorities instituted rapid Sovietization. Pre-existing Polish political institutions were dissolved, and Communist Party structures were extended. Land reform abolished private estates, redistributing agricultural holdings under collective or state farms. Polish registrations of land and property were invalidated; Soviet legal codes, currency, and education systems replaced Polish laws and curricula. The Soviet secret police (NKVD) conducted mass arrests, deportations, and executions targeting former Polish officials, military officers, intelligentsia, landowners, and perceived “class enemies.”

**World War II Interregnum and German Occupation**
Between June 1941 and July 1944, Nazi Germany’s Operation Barbarossa overran Soviet-occupied Polish lands, subjecting them to German military and civilian administration. During this period, both Polish and Soviet citizens suffered atrocities, including mass shootings and the Holocaust. In summer 1944, the Red Army recaptured the territories, re-establishing Soviet control and reaffirming the 1939 borders.

**Post-War Border Settlement**
At the Tehran (1943), Yalta (February 1945), and Potsdam (July–August 1945) conferences, the Allies—particularly Joseph Stalin, Franklin D. Roosevelt (and later Harry S. Truman), and Winston Churchill—agreed to shift Poland’s eastern frontier westward to what became known as the Curzon Line (with minor adjustments). Consequently, the pre-1939 Polish territories east of this demarcation remained within the Ukrainian and Belarusian SSRs, while Poland received former German territories to the west (including Silesia, Pomerania, and southern East Prussia).

**Demographic and Humanitarian Consequences**
The transfer of territory triggered one of the largest forced population movements in European history. Between 1944 and 1946, approximately 1.5 million Poles were expelled or fled from the annexed eastern regions to new Polish territories. Simultaneously, some 460,000 Ukrainians and Belarusians displaced from Poland were “repatriated” to the USSR or resettled in Poland’s western lands. Soviet and Polish authorities orchestrated population exchanges and, in some cases, coerced relocations—particularly under Operation Vistula (1947), which targeted Ukrainian minorities in southeastern Poland.

**Economic and Cultural Impact**
Under Soviet administration, the former Polish eastern provinces underwent industrial and agrarian reorganization integrating them into centralized Soviet planning. Urban reconstruction in war-ravaged cities such as Lwów (Lviv) followed Stalinist architectural patterns. Polish cultural heritage sites were often neglected or repurposed, while local Ukrainian and Belarusian cultural institutions were promoted. Education and public life mobilized around Soviet ideology, with the Russian language and history stressed in official discourse.

**Legacy and Contemporary Context**
After the USSR’s dissolution in 1991, the annexed territories became integral parts of independent Ukraine and Belarus. Cities that had once served as centers of Polish political and cultural life—most prominently Lviv and Vilnius—now function as regional capitals and cultural hubs in their respective nations. The memory of border changes and population displacements continues to influence Polish-Ukrainian and Polish-Belarusian relations, interwoven with issues such as minority rights, historical commemoration, and cross-border cooperation.

**Conclusion**
The Soviet annexation of eastern Polish territories from 1939 to 1945 reshaped Central and Eastern Europe’s political map, creating enduring demographic shifts and geopolitical realignments. Rooted in the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and solidified by Allied wartime diplomacy, these territorial changes underpinned post-war Soviet influence and laid the groundwork for the modern borders of Poland, Ukraine, and Belarus. The human consequences of enforced migrations and cultural transformations remain an important chapter in the region’s complex history.
