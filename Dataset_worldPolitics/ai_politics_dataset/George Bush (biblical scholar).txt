**George Bush** (April 3, 1796 – September 26, 1859) was an American Presbyterian minister, theologian, and pioneer of biblical scholarship in the United States. As the first professor of Hebrew at the newly founded New Brunswick Theological Seminary (later Rutgers College) and author of one of the earliest Hebrew grammars published in America, Bush played a key role in shaping theological education during a period of intense religious revival and political transformation in the young republic.

**Early Life and Education**
Born in Juniata County, Pennsylvania, Bush was raised in a devout Presbyterian family of Scots-Irish descent. He entered Jefferson College (now Washington & Jefferson College) in 1814 and graduated in 1818. He then studied theology under Dr. John M. Mason at the newly established Princeton Theological Seminary, whose rigorous curriculum combined biblical languages with Reformed doctrine. Ordained in 1821, Bush initially served congregations in New York State, where he became known for his eloquent preaching and proficiency in Hebrew.

**Academic Career and Hebrew Scholarship**
In 1828 Bush accepted appointment as Professor of Hebrew and Oriental Literature at the Theological Seminary of the Dutch Reformed Church in New Brunswick, New Jersey. There he delivered systematic instruction in Hebrew grammar and biblical exegesis, filling a void in American theological training, which until then had relied heavily on European imports. His 1833 text, *Grammar of the Hebrew Language*, was the first original work of its kind by an American scholar. Bush’s grammar emphasized reasoned analysis of Semitic roots over rote memorization, reflecting Enlightenment-inspired pedagogical reforms then gaining traction in American higher education.

**Publications and Theological Influence**
Bush authored numerous biblical commentaries and lectures, including volumes on the Pentateuch and the prophets, which combined philological rigor with conservative Reformed orthodoxy. He edited journals that reviewed contemporary German and British scholarship, guiding a generation of American ministers through debates over Higher Criticism and emerging Unitarian theology. By defending traditional views of Mosaic authorship and the inerrancy of Scripture, Bush fortified the conservative wing of the Presbyterian Church against liberalizing currents—a stance with clear political overtones in an era when public education, moral reform, and national identity were hotly contested.

**Role in Religious and Political Context**
Bush’s career coincided with the Second Great Awakening (circa 1790–1840), a nationwide revival movement that reshaped American politics and society. His advocacy for temperance, Sabbath observance, and abolition of dueling linked religious convictions to public morality campaigns that influenced state legislatures and local governance. Bush supported colonization efforts—backed by figures such as President James Monroe—to repatriate freed African Americans to Liberia, reflecting the complex intersection of antislavery sentiment and racial attitudes in antebellum politics. Although he opposed slavery on theological grounds, Bush’s gradualist position aligned him with mainstream Northern Presbyterians rather than radical abolitionists.

**Institutional Leadership and Legacy**
In 1840 Bush became president of the American Oriental Society and later helped establish the Eclectic Society of biblical scholars, forums that fostered interaction between clergy and lay intellectuals. Under his leadership, Rutgers College expanded its classical and scientific curricula, positioning it among the first American colleges to integrate modern languages and sciences alongside theology. Bush’s students—many of whom entered politics as well as the ministry—carried his philological methods into civic life, influencing the way the Bible was taught in public schools and debated in legislative halls.

**Death and Posthumous Recognition**
Bush retired from active teaching in 1857 and died two years later in New Brunswick. His obituary in religious and academic journals lauded him as “the father of Hebrew learning in America.” Although later overshadowed by advances in critical scholarship, his grammars and commentaries remained in use well into the late 19th century. Modern historians of American religion acknowledge Bush’s pivotal role in professionalizing theological education and embedding biblical scholarship within broader cultural and political debates over morality, national identity, and the role of religion in public life.
