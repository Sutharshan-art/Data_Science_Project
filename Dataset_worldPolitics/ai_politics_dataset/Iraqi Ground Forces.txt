**Iraqi Ground Forces**

The Iraqi Ground Forces—commonly known as the Iraqi Army—are the principal land warfare component of the Iraqi Armed Forces. Since their foundation under the British mandate in the 1920s, they have reflected Iraq’s shifting political regimes: from monarchy, through republican and Ba‘th Party rule, to the post-2003 democratic state. Their development has been shaped by domestic power struggles, regional conflicts, and external intervention.

### Origins under the Monarchy (1921–1958)

Following the collapse of the Ottoman Empire and British occupation of Mesopotamia during World War I, the League of Nations granted Britain a mandate over the territory that would become Iraq. In 1921, Emir Faisal I established the Kingdom of Iraq, and with British military advisers formed the Royal Iraqi Army. Initially composed of former Ottoman soldiers and tribal levies, its early roles included suppressing Shi‘a and Kurdish revolts and defending the nascent state’s borders. Under British direction, the army was structured on British lines, equipped with small arms and Vickers and Mark IV tanks, and trained by expatriate officers.

### Republican Upheaval and Military Coups (1958–1968)

The 14 July 1958 coup d’état, led by Army officers including Abd al-Karim Qasim, overthrew the monarchy and established a republic. The new regime sought to “Iraqize” the officer corps, reducing British influence and promoting Arab nationalist ideology. Over the next decade, factional rivalries among pan-Arabists, communists, and Ba‘thists led to frequent coups and counter-coups. During this period, the Ground Forces expanded with Soviet assistance, receiving T-34 tanks and MiG-15-trained crews. Political purges weakened cohesion, as each ruling clique trusted only officers loyal to its faction.

### Ba‘th Party Consolidation and Expansion (1968–1980)

After the Ba‘th Party seized power in July 1968, Saddam Hussein emerged as a dominant figure. Between 1968 and 1980, oil revenues financed major military buildups. The army grew to over a quarter-million troops organized into armored, mechanized, and infantry divisions. Western and Soviet arms imports—including British Chieftain tanks, Soviet T-55 and T-72 tanks, BMP-1 infantry fighting vehicles, and artillery—modernized the force. Parallel to quantitative growth, Ba‘thist political officers ensured party control within units, embedding the army in Saddam’s system of patronage and surveillance.

### Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988)

On 22 September 1980, Iraqi forces invaded Iran, initiating an eight-year war. Initially, large armored formations advanced into Khuzestan province, backed by artillery and supported by air strikes. After early gains, Iraq’s offensive stalled amid fierce Iranian resistance and devastating trench warfare reminiscent of World War I. Both sides suffered heavy losses; chemical weapons use by Iraq amplified casualties. By 1988, the Ground Forces had absorbed hundreds of thousands of casualties but maintained enough strength—through massive mobilization and external arms supplies—to secure a UN-brokered ceasefire without territorial change.

### Gulf War and Sanctions (1990–2003)

Iraq’s August 1990 invasion of Kuwait prompted a U.S.-led coalition response in Operation Desert Storm (January–February 1991). Coalition air superiority and precision strikes destroyed much of the Iraqi Army’s armor, command centers, and logistics. By ground operations’ end, only a fraction of Saddam’s divisions remained combat effective. Harsh UN sanctions following the war deprived the Ground Forces of spare parts and new equipment, forcing reliance on cannibalized stocks. No-fly zones imposed over northern and southern Iraq further constrained Iraqi military movements.

### Disbandment and Insurgency (2003–2006)

The U.S.-led invasion of March 2003 swiftly toppled Saddam’s regime. Coalition Provisional Authority Order 2 formally disbanded the Iraqi Army, eliminating command structures and retiring hundreds of thousands of soldiers. The dissolution created a security vacuum, feeding the subsequent Sunni-led insurgency. Former officers joined anti-coalition militias or sectarian militias, and widespread looting of depots fueled armed groups. Efforts to recruit new forces began in mid-2003 but progressed slowly amid violence and mistrust.

### Reformation under the New Republic (2006–2014)

By 2006, Coalition and Iraqi leaders had reestablished the Ground Forces as a volunteer institution under the Ministry of Defense. New infantry brigades and mechanized units were trained by U.S. and Coalition advisers at bases such as Besmaya and Al-Kindi. Emphasis was placed on counter-insurgency tactics, human rights training, and unit cohesion across ethnic and sectarian lines. Despite political efforts to integrate Sunnis and Kurds, recruitment remained dominated by Shia communities, complicating national unity.

### Counter-Islamic State Campaign (2014–2017)

The rise of the Islamic State of Iraq and al-Sham (ISIS) in 2014 overran Iraqi security forces in Mosul and other provinces. Under Prime Minister Haider al-Abadi, the Ground Forces regrouped with critical support from U.S. airpower, Iranian advisers, and Popular Mobilization Forces. Iraqi divisions—particularly the 1st and 16th armored divisions—conducted large-scale offensives to retake Tikrit, Ramadi, Fallujah, and finally Mosul in 2017. While demonstrating improved operational capability, the Ground Forces continued to face challenges in logistics, command-and-control, and interservice coordination.

### Contemporary Structure and Challenges

Today, the Iraqi Ground Forces comprise approximately 170,000 active-duty soldiers organized into four corps covering northern, central, southern, and western sectors. Forces include armored, mechanized, infantry, special operations, and counter-terrorism brigades. Persistent challenges include equipment shortages, corruption in procurement, political interference, and the need to integrate remnants of militia groups into a unified command structure. Ongoing programs aim to modernize heavy armor with U.S. M1A1 Abrams tanks, enhance intelligence and reconnaissance capabilities, and professionalize the officer corps through expanded military education.

### Political Significance

Throughout their history, the Iraqi Ground Forces have been both instruments of state power and barometers of Iraq’s political health. Under the monarchy and early republic, they upheld fragile regimes amid external pressures. During the Ba‘thist era, they buttressed authoritarian rule and pursued aggressive regional policies. Their dissolution and rebirth after 2003 illustrated the perils and promises of security-sector reform in post-conflict states. As Iraq navigates reconstruction, sectarian reconciliation, and regional rivalries, the future effectiveness and cohesion of its Ground Forces remain crucial to national stability and sovereignty.
