\==Early Life and Education==
Nancy Davis Reagan was born Anne Frances Robbins on July 6, 1921, in New York City. After her parents’ divorce in 1929, she was raised primarily by her maternal grandmother in Chicago, adopting the surname of her stepfather, Loyal Davis, a prominent neurosurgeon. She attended Girls’ Latin School in Chicago and subsequently enrolled at Smith College in Northampton, Massachusetts, in 1939. At Smith, she studied drama but left in 1943 before completing her degree to pursue an acting career in Hollywood.

\==Acting Career and Marriage==
Upon moving to California in the mid-1940s, Nancy Davis signed a contract with Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer and appeared in a series of minor film roles, including parts in *Don’t Give Up the Ship* (1949) and *The Next Voice You Hear…* (1950). Her acting career, however, was secondary to her role as social companion and later spouse to Ronald Reagan, a rising figure in the Screen Actors Guild. The two married on March 4, 1952, in a ceremony at the Little Brown Church in Studio City, California. Nancy Reagan subsequently retired from acting to support her husband’s political and business endeavors.

\==First Lady of California==
When Ronald Reagan was elected Governor of California in 1966, Nancy took on the role of First Lady of California from January 2, 1967, to January 6, 1975. She oversaw the restoration of the governor’s mansion and championed causes such as the arts and historic preservation. Though she maintained a lower public profile than some First Ladies, she was regarded as a calming presence and a skilled hostess, hosting cultural events and dignitaries at the mansion.

\==First Lady of the United States==
As First Lady of the United States from January 20, 1981, to January 20, 1989, Nancy Reagan played an influential role in her husband’s administration. Her style was marked by elegance and traditionalism, and she sought to bring a sense of dignity to White House events. She established the White House Millennium Council to plan the celebration of the nation’s upcoming bicentennial in 1976—a project later repurposed to mark the end of the 20th century.

One of her most notable contributions was the “Just Say No” anti-drug campaign, launched in 1982 in partnership with the National Federation of Parents for Drug-Free Youth. The initiative aimed to educate young people about the dangers of illicit drug use and promote refusal skills. While it became a widely recognized slogan, its long-term efficacy has been the subject of debate among scholars and public health experts.

\==Political Influence and Controversies==
Nancy Reagan’s influence extended beyond traditional First Lady duties. She was known to have direct access to the President, often voicing opinions on Cabinet appointments and policy decisions. This close advisory role earned her both praise and criticism. Critics argued that her involvement in personnel decisions, especially during discussions surrounding the nomination of Sandra Day O’Connor to the Supreme Court, amounted to undue influence. Supporters contended that her counsel was a natural extension of the partnership between a President and his spouse.

During her husband’s administration, Nancy Reagan oversaw a controversial refurbishment of the White House living quarters in 1983. The project cost approximately \$1.5 million, much of which was privately funded, but it sparked controversy over perceived extravagance at a time of economic recession. The episode underscored broader debates about the role and funding of the First Lady’s initiatives.

\==Health Advocacy==
In 1987, Nancy Reagan was diagnosed with breast cancer and underwent a mastectomy. Her public disclosure of the diagnosis and subsequent recovery helped raise awareness about breast cancer detection and treatment. She used her platform to encourage regular screenings for women, reinforcing the importance of early diagnosis.

\==Later Life and Legacy==
Following the end of Ronald Reagan’s presidency in 1989, Nancy Reagan continued her advocacy work, particularly in areas of drug prevention and cancer awareness. She established the Nancy Reagan Foundation to preserve her husband’s legacy and to support charitable causes. After Ronald Reagan’s Alzheimer's disease diagnosis in 1994, she became a private caregiver, withdrawing from public life to focus on her husband’s health until his death on June 5, 2004.

In her later years, Nancy Reagan faced criticism for her initial support of certain experimental Alzheimer’s treatments and for contentious decisions over her husband’s medical care. Nevertheless, historians generally regard her as one of the most politically active and influential First Ladies of the 20th century. She died on March 6, 2016, at the age of 94, and was interred alongside her husband at the Ronald Reagan Presidential Library in Simi Valley, California.

\==Historical and Political Context==
Nancy Reagan’s tenure as First Lady occurred during a period of significant ideological realignment in American politics. The Reagan administration emphasized free-market principles, a robust national defense posture, and a conservative social agenda. Within this framework, Nancy Reagan’s ceremonial leadership and advocacy for drug prevention complemented broader Republican efforts to project strength and moral clarity. Her public persona helped shape the image of the presidency in the 1980s, reinforcing themes of tradition, family values, and celebrity-driven political culture.

Her “Just Say No” campaign must also be understood in the context of the 1980s “War on Drugs,” a series of federal policies aimed at reducing drug use through law enforcement and prevention programs. While the campaign succeeded in elevating public awareness, critics point to rising incarceration rates and limited attention to addiction treatment as unintended consequences of the era’s punitive approaches.

Nancy Reagan’s story illustrates the evolving role of the First Lady in American politics, transitioning from largely symbolic duties to active participation in policy and public advocacy. Her legacy continues to inform contemporary debates about the influence of presidential spouses, the use of private funds in public office, and the intersection of celebrity culture and political power.
