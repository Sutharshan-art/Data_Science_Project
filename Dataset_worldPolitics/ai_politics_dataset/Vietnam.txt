**Vietnam**

**Introduction**
Vietnam, officially the Socialist Republic of Vietnam, is a country in Southeast Asia occupying the eastern margin of the Indochinese Peninsula. Bounded by China to the north, Laos and Cambodia to the west, and the South China Sea to the east and south, Vietnam has a population of over 100 million. Its capital is Hanoi, while Ho Chi Minh City is the largest urban center and economic hub. Vietnam’s history is marked by millennia of indigenous state formation, external domination, revolutionary struggle, and rapid economic transformation under a one-party socialist system.

**Early History**
Archaeological evidence indicates that rice cultivation and bronze-working cultures flourished in the Red River Delta by at least 2000 BC. The first recorded polity, Văn Lang ruled by the Hồng Bàng dynasty, emerged in the first millennium BC. Over subsequent centuries, Vietnamese rulers consolidated control, pushing southward in a process known as Nam Tiến (“southern advance”), assimilating Champa and Khmer territories. Imperial dynasties such as Lý (1009–1225), Trần (1225–1400), and Lê (1428–1789) developed Confucian administration, maintained tributary ties with China, and oversaw periods of cultural flourishing and territorial expansion.

**French Colonial Period and Independence Struggle**
In the mid-19th century, France colonized Vietnam, incorporating it into French Indochina alongside Laos and Cambodia. Colonial rule brought infrastructural development and integration into the global economy but also imposed harsh taxes, land loss, and social disruption. Nationalist movements emerged in the early 20th century, most notably the Indochinese Communist Party led by Hồ Chí Minh. Following Japanese occupation during World War II, Hồ’s Việt Minh declared independence in September 1945, but colonial conflict resumed. The First Indochina War (1946–1954) ended with Vietnamese victory at Điện Biên Phủ and the Geneva Accords, dividing the country at the 17th parallel.

**Division and the Vietnam War**
The division established a communist government in North Vietnam under Hồ Chí Minh and an anti-communist regime in South Vietnam supported by the United States and other allies. Escalating Cold War tensions led to large-scale U.S. military intervention beginning in 1965. The Vietnam War featured guerrilla warfare in the South and extensive bombing in the North. The 1968 Tet Offensive dealt a psychological blow to American resolve, and growing antiwar sentiment in the U.S. contributed to a gradual drawdown of forces. The 1973 Paris Peace Accords facilitated U.S. withdrawal, but fighting continued until the Fall of Saigon on April 30, 1975, when North Vietnamese forces captured Saigon, reunifying the country by force.

**Reunification and Socialist Rule**
After reunification, the Communist Party of Vietnam (CPV) established a centrally planned economy and instituted land collectivization. Postwar reconstruction faced challenges including economic isolation, war-related devastation, and social dislocation. In 1978–1979, Vietnam intervened in Cambodia to overthrow the Khmer Rouge, leading to international sanctions and conflict with China. By the mid-1980s, economic stagnation prompted leadership to introduce Đổi Mới (Renovation) reforms, transitioning toward a “socialist-oriented market economy” while retaining one-party rule.

**Political System**
Vietnam operates under the constitution of 2013 as a socialist republic led by the CPV, which exercises political leadership through its Politburo and Central Committee. The President serves as head of state, and the Prime Minister heads the government. The unicameral National Assembly enacts legislation, approves the budget, and oversees government agencies. Local People’s Councils and People’s Committees govern provinces and municipalities. While elections are held, all candidates must be approved by the CPV and its affiliated organizations, ensuring party dominance and limited political pluralism.

**Foreign Relations and Regional Role**
Vietnam’s foreign policy emphasizes independence, self-reliance, multilateralism, and diversification of partnerships. A founding member of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) since 1995, Vietnam has pursued closer ties with former adversaries, including the United States, which normalized relations in 1995. It maintains strategic partnerships with China, Russia, Japan, and the European Union. Vietnam balances cooperation with great powers against territorial disputes in the South China Sea, asserting sovereignty while engaging in dialogue through mechanisms such as the ASEAN Regional Forum.

**Economy and Development**
Since Đổi Mới, Vietnam has experienced sustained economic growth, transforming from one of the world’s poorest countries into a lower-middle-income economy. Key sectors include manufacturing, agriculture, fisheries, and increasingly services and information technology. Export-driven growth has benefited from trade agreements such as the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) and the EU–Vietnam Free Trade Agreement (EVFTA). Challenges remain in income inequality, environmental degradation, and state-owned enterprise reform.

**Contemporary Issues and Outlook**
Vietnam faces contemporary challenges of managing rapid urbanization, environmental sustainability, and demographic shifts. Internet penetration and a young population have fostered vibrant digital economies and social discourse, though restrictions on political freedoms persist. Recent constitutional amendments and anti-corruption campaigns reflect ongoing efforts to strengthen governance credibility. Vietnam’s strategic location and growing economic integration position it as a pivotal actor in regional affairs, navigating complex dynamics between development goals and one-party governance principles.
