**Virginia Dell Cassidy Clinton Kelley**

**Introduction**
Virginia Dell Cassidy Clinton Kelley (June 6, 1923 – January 6, 1994) was an American nurse and midwife, best known as the mother of President Bill Clinton. Though she never held elected office, her life and work intersected with key social and political currents of mid-20th-century America. A committed healthcare professional and community volunteer, she influenced the early development and character of her son, shaping his views on public service, compassion, and social responsibility.

**Early Life and Education**
Born Virginia Dell Cassidy on June 6, 1923, in Bodcaw, Nevada County, Arkansas, she was one of four children in a family of modest means. Her father, James Eldridge Cassidy, was a blacksmith and a Republican precinct committeeman, while her mother, Edith Grisham Cassidy, was a seamstress. Virginia grew up during the Great Depression, attending segregated schools and helping on the family farm. Inspired by a desire to serve, she pursued nursing at the Presbyterian–St. Luke’s School of Nursing in Little Rock, graduating in 1944. Her education coincided with World War II, when nurse shortages and public health concerns made the profession both challenging and vital.

**Nursing Career and Midwifery**
After earning her nursing degree, Virginia Cassidy worked at Little Rock’s St. Luke’s Hospital and later with the United States Public Health Service. Trained as a nurse-midwife, she delivered dozens of babies in rural Arkansas and neighboring communities, often making house calls under difficult conditions. Her work exposed her to the health disparities faced by low-income and African American families, reinforcing her belief in accessible, community-based care. She supplemented her income through part-time work and government assignments, embodying the wartime expansion of women’s roles in professional fields.

**First Marriage and Family Life**
In 1943, Virginia Cassidy married William Jefferson Blythe Jr., a traveling salesman. Widowed in a car accident three months later, she returned to her nursing career. In 1950, she married a local automobile dealer, Roger Clinton Sr., and bore a son, William Jefferson “Billy” Clinton, on August 19, 1946, later known simply as Bill Clinton. When her marriage to Clinton Sr. became troubled, she divorced in 1962, raising her son as a single mother while continuing her nursing practice. In 1969, she married Jeff Dwire, a carpenter and veteran, with whom she remained until his death in 1974. Her series of personal losses and responsibilities forged resilience and empathy—qualities she instilled in her son.

**Community Engagement and Volunteerism**
Throughout the 1960s and 1970s, Virginia Clinton Kelley was active in local civic organizations. She volunteered for the American Red Cross, served on hospital auxiliary boards, and participated in her church’s outreach programs. Her bedside manner and advocacy for maternal and child health earned respect among colleagues and neighbors. Though she rarely spoke publicly on political issues, her involvement in community health initiatives mirrored broader national trends toward expanding social services and the emerging women’s movement, which championed greater professional opportunities for women.

**Influence on Bill Clinton’s Political Development**
Virginia’s influence on her son’s political philosophy was profound. Bill Clinton later credited his mother’s warmth, moral courage, and dedication to helping the disadvantaged as formative to his own public-service ethos. Her decision to encourage her son’s education—sending him to boarding school and instilling in him a commitment to scholarship—proved pivotal in his rise from rural Arkansas to the presidency. During Bill Clinton’s gubernatorial and presidential campaigns, Virginia Kelley made occasional appearances but largely stayed out of the political spotlight, preferring to support family and community quietly.

**Later Years and Public Recognition**
In the 1980s and early 1990s, as her son attained national prominence, Virginia Kelley received increasing media attention. She continued her nursing and volunteer work, even as health issues emerged. Diagnosed with breast cancer in 1992, she underwent treatment while attending events in Little Rock to support her son’s 1992 presidential campaign. Her grace under pressure and down-to-earth humility endeared her to both local communities and national audiences, reinforcing the image of a grounded, compassionate First Family matriarch.

**Death and Legacy**
Virginia Clinton Kelley died of complications from breast cancer on January 6, 1994, at age 70. Her funeral in Little Rock drew hundreds of mourners, including Arkansas colleagues from the medical and civic sectors. Her legacy endures in the fields of maternal and child health she championed, and in the narrative of social mobility she embodied. Through her son’s speeches and writings, Bill Clinton often invoked his mother’s example when discussing family values, social justice, and the American Dream. As a nurse, volunteer, and mother of a president, Virginia Kelley’s life story reflects the intersections of gender, health care, and politics in 20th-century America.
