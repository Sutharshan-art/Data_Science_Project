**War in the Gulf**

**Introduction**
The War in the Gulf, commonly known as the First Gulf War or the Persian Gulf War (1990–1991), was a multinational conflict triggered by Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait on August 2, 1990. Orchestrated by Iraqi President Saddam Hussein, the occupation prompted a swift international response led by the United States under United Nations auspices. The ensuing military campaign liberated Kuwait and reshaped regional geopolitics at the close of the Cold War.

**Historical and Regional Context**
In the 1980s, Iraq emerged from the protracted Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988) burdened by heavy debts, particularly to Gulf states such as Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, which had financed Baghdad’s war effort against Iran. Disputes over oil production and pricing, combined with Iraq’s assertion that Kuwait was historically part of its territory, fueled tensions. Saddam Hussein accused Kuwait of overproducing oil below OPEC quotas—thereby depressing global prices—and of slant drilling into Iraq’s Rumaila oil field. These grievances provided the pretext for military action.

**Invasion of Kuwait**
On August 2, 1990, Iraqi forces—numbering over 100,000 troops—crossed the Kuwait–Iraq border in a coordinated invasion that overwhelmed the small Kuwaiti defense forces. Within days, Iraq had occupied major Kuwaiti cities, deposed the ruling Al-Sabah family, and declared Kuwait Iraq’s nineteenth province. The annexation violated the UN Charter’s prohibition on the use of force against territorial integrity, prompting immediate condemnation from the United Nations Security Council.

**International Response and Coalition Building**
The United Nations Security Council passed Resolution 660 on August 2, 1990, demanding Iraq’s immediate and unconditional withdrawal from Kuwait. Over the subsequent weeks, additional resolutions imposed economic sanctions, authorized member states to use “all necessary means” to enforce compliance (Resolution 678, November 29, 1990), and established a naval blockade of Iraqi-controlled waters. The United States assembled a broad coalition—eventually including over 30 nations from five continents—comprising Western European powers, Arab states such as Saudi Arabia, Egypt, and Syria, and contributions from Commonwealth countries. This coalition marked a rare alignment of Arab and Western governments in defense of state sovereignty.

**Operation Desert Shield**
On August 7, 1990, U.S. President George H. W. Bush announced Operation Desert Shield, deploying American troops and air defenses to Saudi Arabia to deter further Iraqi advances toward the kingdom’s oil fields and holy sites. Allied forces reinforced the Saudi military, secured key air and naval bases, and prepared for potential ground operations. Meanwhile, extensive diplomacy sought to maintain coalition unity, with Arab members wary of foreign troops on Muslim soil and of escalation toward regime change in Baghdad.

**Operation Desert Storm**
After Iraq ignored the UN’s January 15, 1991 deadline for withdrawal, the coalition launched Operation Desert Storm on January 17, 1991. A sustained air campaign targeted Iraqi command centers, communication networks, air defenses, and infrastructure. Coalition air superiority allowed precision strikes using smart munitions, degrading Iraqi capabilities and isolating front-line units. On February 24, a ground offensive commenced, involving U.S. VII Corps’ “left-hook” maneuver through the Iraqi desert and combined-arms attacks by British, French, Saudi, and other Arab forces. Within 100 hours, coalition forces liberated Kuwait City and severed Iraqi supply lines.

**Ceasefire and Post-Conflict Arrangements**
On February 28, 1991, President Bush declared a ceasefire. The UN Security Council adopted Resolution 687, requiring Iraq to dismantle its weapons of mass destruction (WMD) programs, submit to inspections, and pay reparations to Kuwait. A security zone in southern Iraq, enforced by no-fly zones and coalition patrols, aimed to protect Kurdish and Shi‘a communities from regime reprisals. Saddam Hussein remained in power, and coalition forces withdrew by April 1991, leaving behind enforcement mechanisms to monitor compliance and deter further aggression.

**Political and Humanitarian Consequences**
The war’s swift conclusion bolstered U.S. leadership in the post–Cold War order and demonstrated the effectiveness of multilateral collective security under UN mandates. For Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states, the conflict underscored vulnerabilities and accelerated defense cooperation, eventually contributing to expanded U.S. basing in the region. Domestically, Iraq faced severe economic hardship from sanctions and infrastructure damage; widespread humanitarian crises emerged, compounded by internal uprisings among Kurdish and Shi‘a populations that were brutally suppressed by Saddam’s forces.

**Legacy**
The War in the Gulf set precedents for coalition warfare, precision air campaigns, and post-conflict arms-control regimes. It reshaped Middle East geopolitics, confirming the strategic importance of Gulf oil supplies and embedding U.S. military presence in Saudi Arabia—a factor that influenced regional perceptions and later anti-American sentiments. The sanctions regime and no-fly zones persisted throughout the 1990s, maintaining tensions that would contribute to the 2003 U.S.-led invasion of Iraq. The conflict remains a defining episode in late 20th-century international relations, illustrating both the capacity for rapid multilateral action and the complexities of achieving lasting political solutions.
