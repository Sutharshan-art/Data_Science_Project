**Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization (1968)**

The **Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization (PATCO)** was a labor union in the United States that represented air traffic controllers from 1968 until its decertification in 1981. Established to advocate for improved working conditions, better wages, and enhanced safety protocols in the aviation industry, PATCO played a pivotal role in labor relations between federal employees and the U.S. government during the late 20th century. The union became nationally prominent following its 1981 strike, which resulted in a decisive and controversial intervention by the Reagan administration.

PATCO's dissolution after the 1981 strike marked a significant moment in American labor history and federal labor policy, signaling a shift in government-labor relations and influencing the labor movement in the United States for decades.

---

### Founding and Early Advocacy

PATCO was founded in **1968** by a group of air traffic controllers dissatisfied with their representation under the broader federal employee unions. Initially formed as an independent organization, PATCO sought exclusive representation for the unique challenges and demands of the air traffic control profession. Air traffic controllers faced long hours, high stress, and significant responsibility, as the aviation industry expanded rapidly during the postwar years.

In its early years, PATCO lobbied for improvements to working conditions, staffing levels, and equipment modernization. The union also campaigned for better pay and benefits, arguing that the complexity and intensity of air traffic control work required recognition beyond standard civil service classifications.

While PATCO operated within the bounds of federal labor regulations, including Executive Order 10988 issued by President John F. Kennedy in 1962 (which allowed federal employees to unionize but not to strike), the union was increasingly frustrated by perceived inaction and inflexibility from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and successive presidential administrations.

---

### Tensions with the FAA and Escalating Demands

By the late 1970s, relations between PATCO and the FAA had deteriorated. The union argued that outdated equipment, understaffing, and excessive work demands were leading to fatigue and safety hazards. Air traffic controllers also cited limited avenues for advancement and a lack of recognition for the critical nature of their work.

In 1980, PATCO endorsed Republican presidential candidate **Ronald Reagan**, who, during his campaign, expressed support for the union’s concerns and pledged to address issues within the FAA. The endorsement came despite PATCO's traditional alignment with Democratic-leaning labor coalitions.

---

### The 1981 Strike and Government Response

Tensions peaked in **August 1981**, when contract negotiations between PATCO and the federal government broke down. On **August 3, 1981**, more than **13,000 air traffic controllers** walked off the job in a nationwide strike, seeking better pay, reduced working hours, and improved working conditions. The strike violated federal law prohibiting strikes by government employees.

President Reagan, citing national security and the rule of law, responded swiftly. He declared the strike illegal and ordered the controllers to return to work within 48 hours. When the majority refused, Reagan followed through on his ultimatum, **firing over 11,000 controllers** and barring them from federal employment for life (a ban later lifted by President Bill Clinton in 1993).

The Reagan administration mobilized military controllers and supervisors to maintain essential air traffic operations. Though airline schedules were disrupted and capacity reduced, the system continued functioning, albeit under strain.

---

### Decertification and Aftermath

Following the mass firings, the **Federal Labor Relations Authority (FLRA)** formally **decertified PATCO** as the exclusive bargaining representative for air traffic controllers on **October 22, 1981**. The union was effectively dismantled, with its leadership discredited and its members blacklisted from federal aviation work.

The strike and its aftermath had profound implications for labor relations in the United States. It marked a turning point in public sector unionism and set a precedent for a more assertive stance by the federal government against illegal strikes. The decertification of PATCO was widely viewed as a weakening of organized labor’s influence, particularly in the public sector.

---

### Legacy

The PATCO strike of 1981 remains a watershed moment in U.S. labor history. It is frequently cited as a symbolic and strategic victory for anti-union conservatives and a blow to organized labor. The outcome emboldened private-sector employers to adopt tougher stances in labor negotiations throughout the 1980s and 1990s.

In 2003, a new union—also called the **Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization (PATCO, AFL–CIO)**—was formed, though it has not regained the influence or membership levels of its predecessor.

PATCO’s story is often discussed in debates over federal labor rights, union strategy, and the limits of collective bargaining in critical national industries. Its rise and fall underscore the complex intersection of labor advocacy, public safety, executive authority, and political ideology in the United States.
