**Poles in the Soviet Union**

**Poles in the Soviet Union** refers to the ethnic Polish population that resided within the borders of the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR)** from its inception in 1922 until its dissolution in 1991. This population included both long-established Polish communities in territories absorbed into the Soviet Union and individuals forcibly relocated or deported during various political upheavals, especially under the rule of **Joseph Stalin**. The experiences of Poles in the USSR were shaped by Soviet nationalities policy, forced migration, political repression, and complex relations between Poland and the Soviet Union throughout the 20th century.

The treatment of Poles in the Soviet Union varied over time, ranging from relative cultural autonomy during early Soviet nationality experiments to widespread persecution, deportations, and executions, particularly during the 1930s and 1940s. The political relationship between Moscow and Warsaw played a critical role in shaping the fate of Polish communities, especially during periods of war, occupation, and ideological confrontation.

---

### Historical Background

Polish communities had lived in regions of what would become the Soviet Union for centuries, particularly in areas such as present-day **Belarus, Ukraine, and Lithuania**, which were historically part of the **Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth**. Following the **partitions of Poland** in the late 18th century, many Poles became subjects of the Russian Empire.

After the **Bolshevik Revolution** and the creation of the Soviet Union in 1922, these communities found themselves within the borders of the new socialist state. The **Treaty of Riga** (1921), which ended the Polish–Soviet War, established new boundaries but left significant Polish populations on both sides of the border.

---

### Early Soviet Period and National Autonomy

In the 1920s, Soviet nationalities policy under **Lenin** promoted limited cultural autonomy for various ethnic groups, including Poles. Polish-language schools, newspapers, and cultural institutions were permitted, and two autonomous Polish districts were established in the 1930s: **Marchlewszczyzna** in Soviet Ukraine and **Dzierżyńszczyzna** in Soviet Belarus.

These districts were part of the USSR’s broader strategy to promote socialist identity through national forms, in line with the policy of **korenizatsiya** (indigenization). However, this period of relative tolerance was short-lived, as Stalin's regime soon reversed course and targeted many national minorities for suspicion and repression.

---

### Stalinist Repression and the Polish Operation

The late 1930s saw a dramatic escalation in Soviet repression of Poles. During the **Great Purge**, Stalin's regime carried out a series of ethnically targeted campaigns against national minorities accused of disloyalty or espionage.

One of the most devastating was the **Polish Operation of the NKVD (1937–1938)**, during which approximately 111,000 ethnic Poles were executed and tens of thousands more were imprisoned or sent to labor camps. The operation was based on NKVD Order No. 00485 and targeted individuals suspected of links to Polish military or political organizations, though most victims were ordinary civilians.

In parallel, the autonomous Polish districts were abolished, and cultural institutions were shut down, signaling the end of official support for Polish identity within the USSR.

---

### World War II and Mass Deportations

The situation worsened with the outbreak of **World War II**. Following the **Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact** of 1939, Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union divided Poland, and the USSR annexed eastern Polish territories. Approximately 13.5 million people, including hundreds of thousands of Poles, fell under Soviet control.

Between 1940 and 1941, the Soviet government deported an estimated **325,000 to 1 million Poles** to Siberia, Kazakhstan, and other remote regions of the USSR. These deportations targeted former landowners, military personnel, intelligentsia, and their families. Many died from harsh conditions, hunger, and disease.

The situation shifted again after **Operation Barbarossa**, when Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941. The **Sikorski–Mayski Agreement** between the Polish government-in-exile and the USSR led to a temporary amnesty for Polish citizens in the USSR and the formation of the **Anders’ Army**, composed of Polish exiles.

---

### Post-War Period

After the war, the Soviet Union retained the eastern Polish territories annexed in 1939, and a communist government was established in Poland under Soviet influence. Many Poles in the USSR were repatriated to postwar Poland in several waves between 1944 and 1947, though some remained in the Soviet republics, particularly in **Lithuania, Belarus, and Ukraine**.

Under the post-Stalin leadership, open repression declined, but Poles continued to face restrictions on cultural expression, religion, and education. In areas with significant Polish populations, efforts at Russification persisted, and religious practices, particularly Catholicism, were closely monitored.

---

### Decline of the USSR and Legacy

In the late Soviet period, Polish cultural and religious life saw modest revival in some areas. The rise of **Solidarity** in Poland and the election of **Pope John Paul II** inspired renewed interest in Polish identity among diaspora communities, despite surveillance and restrictions.

Following the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, newly independent states like Lithuania, Belarus, and Ukraine inherited the remaining Polish populations. Issues such as minority rights, property restitution, and cultural preservation continue to be part of post-Soviet Polish relations.

---

### Conclusion

The history of Poles in the Soviet Union reflects the broader challenges faced by national minorities under authoritarian regimes. From early promises of autonomy to brutal repression and forced deportation, their experience was marked by cycles of inclusion and exclusion shaped by shifting ideological and geopolitical priorities. The legacy of these policies continues to influence Polish memory and relations with successor states of the USSR.
