**Ross Perot**

**Early Life and Education**
Henry Ross Perot was born on June 27, 1930, in Texarkana, Texas, to a family of modest means; his father was a traveling salesman, and his mother taught language and music. Perot excelled academically and athletically at Texarkana High School. He attended Texarkana Junior College before transferring to the United States Naval Academy, graduating in 1953 with a Bachelor of Science in engineering. He served as a naval officer aboard the destroyer USS Robert A. Owens, rising to Lieutenant Junior Grade before resigning his commission in 1957.

**Business Career**
After leaving the Navy, Perot joined IBM as a salesman and rapidly rose in the company’s ranks. In 1962, he founded Electronic Data Systems (EDS) in Dallas, Texas, financing the venture with a \$1,000 loan from his in‐laws. EDS pioneered outsourcing of data processing and information‐technology services, securing contracts with the U.S. government, including a \$3 million management of the Social Security Administration’s data centers. By the 1980s, EDS employed over 50,000 people worldwide and generated revenues exceeding \$3 billion. Following a hostile takeover bid in 1984, Perot sold his controlling stake in 1988 for approximately \$2.4 billion and used the proceeds to fund philanthropic and political activities.

**Entry into Politics and 1992 Presidential Campaign**
Frustrated by growing federal deficits and political gridlock, Perot entered the 1992 presidential race as an independent candidate. He self‐financed his campaign, spending over \$60 million of his own fortune, and utilized infomercials—45‐minute television broadcasts—to outline his policy proposals. Perot emphasized fiscal responsibility, advocating a balanced budget by reducing the federal deficit through spending cuts and a one‐time 19% sales tax on all new goods. He made deficit reduction the centerpiece of his campaign, famously describing the national debt as a “giant sucking sound” drawing jobs away from American workers.

Polling as high as 39% in June 1992, Perot’s support declined after he temporarily withdrew in July, citing concerns over campaign pressures, only to reenter in October. On Election Day, he captured 18.9% of the popular vote—the highest for a third‐party or independent candidate since Theodore Roosevelt in 1912—but won no electoral votes. Exit polls indicated that Perot drew supporters from both major parties, though his presence arguably influenced the tight race between Democrat Bill Clinton and incumbent President George H. W. Bush.

**Formation of the Reform Party and 1996 Campaign**
In February 1995, Perot founded the Reform Party of the United States of America, aiming to institutionalize his platform of campaign‐finance reform, term limits, and balanced budgets. The party achieved ballot access in over 40 states for the 1996 election. Perot secured the Reform Party’s nomination and embarked on a second presidential bid, again self‐financed but more disciplined in campaign structure. His running mate was retired Vice Admiral James Stockdale.

The 1996 campaign was overshadowed by the Clinton‐Gore ticket’s incumbency advantages and the Reform Party’s internal divisions. Perot received 8.4% of the popular vote—far below his 1992 showing—and no electoral votes. The campaign highlighted the challenges of sustaining a third party within the U.S. two‐party system and set the stage for subsequent factional disputes within the Reform Party.

**Political Positions and Influence**
Perot’s policy platform combined fiscal conservatism with socially moderate views. He supported free trade but insisted on labor protections and opposed the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) until labor and environmental standards were enforceable. He championed educational reform, universal work requirements, and a restructured Social Security system. Perot was a strong advocate for electronic government and technology‐driven efficiency, reflecting his business background.

Although never elected to public office, Perot’s campaigns shifted national debate toward deficit reduction and trade issues. In the aftermath of the 1992 election, Congress passed the 1993 Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act, which combined spending cuts and tax increases, a package credited by some analysts as influenced by the urgency Perot created on fiscal matters.

**Philanthropy and Later Years**
After 1996, Perot largely withdrew from active politics but remained an elder statesman and donor. Through the Perot Foundation and his family’s philanthropies, he supported educational initiatives, veterans’ services, and medical research. His daughter, H. Ross Perot Jr., led efforts in economic development and historic preservation in Texas. In 2008, Perot received the Presidential Medal of Freedom for his business innovations and civic contributions.

**Death and Legacy**
Ross Perot died on July 9, 2019, in Dallas, Texas, at age 89. His life story—from small‐town origins to business magnate and political reformer—embodied themes of entrepreneurial success and civic engagement. Perot’s two presidential campaigns remain the most successful third‐party bids of the late 20th century, demonstrating the potential—and limits—of populist reform movements in American politics. His focus on fiscal responsibility and political outsiders foreshadowed later waves of anti‐establishment sentiment, contributing to enduring discussions about party realignment, campaign finance, and voter engagement.
