**Coat of Arms of Bosnia and Herzegovina**

The **coat of arms of Bosnia and Herzegovina** is one of the key national symbols of the country, officially adopted on **May 20, 1998**, following the end of the Bosnian War and the signing of the **Dayton Peace Agreement**. It reflects Bosnia and Herzegovina’s contemporary political structure and international identity, while replacing previous heraldic symbols that had become associated with ethnic divisions during the 1992–1995 conflict.

The current design features a **gold diagonal band (bend)** on a **blue shield**, with **seven five-pointed white stars** arranged along the bend and cut off at the top and bottom edges. The design is simple, geometric, and European in character, echoing the aesthetics of the **flag of Bosnia and Herzegovina**, which was introduced in 1998 under international mediation. The coat of arms represents neutrality and statehood, with no explicit ethnic or religious symbolism, aligning with the country’s postwar emphasis on unity and inclusive governance.

---

### Historical Background

Historically, Bosnia and Herzegovina has used a variety of coats of arms, reflecting its complex political past and multiethnic composition. The earliest heraldic emblem associated with the region was the **coat of arms of the medieval Kingdom of Bosnia**, ruled by the **Kotromanić dynasty**. That medieval symbol featured a blue shield with six golden fleurs-de-lis around a white bend, which came to be identified with the **Bosniak** or **Bosnian Muslim** population in the 20th century.

During the **Austro-Hungarian period** (1878–1918), Bosnia and Herzegovina had different regional coats of arms assigned by imperial decree. In the **Yugoslav era**, the Socialist Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina (one of six constituent republics of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia) used a socialist-style emblem featuring industrial and natural motifs such as a factory and mountains, with red stars symbolizing communism.

Following the dissolution of Yugoslavia in 1992, Bosnia and Herzegovina declared independence and adopted a new coat of arms, based on the medieval Kotromanić emblem. While seen by many Bosniaks as a symbol of historical continuity, it was viewed by many **Serbs** and **Croats** in the country as ethnically exclusive.

This situation changed dramatically after the **Bosnian War** and the establishment of a new constitutional order under the **1995 Dayton Agreement**, which created a highly decentralized state composed of two main entities: the **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina** and the **Republika Srpska**. In 1998, the international community, particularly the **Office of the High Representative (OHR)**, intervened to impose a new coat of arms to serve as a neutral symbol of the central state.

---

### Design and Symbolism

The modern coat of arms features a **blue shield** divided by a **diagonal yellow band**, also known as a bend. The bend is lined with **white five-pointed stars**, partially cut off at the top and bottom to give the appearance of continuity. This design mirrors the **national flag** introduced in 1998, which similarly avoids ethnic identifiers and instead emphasizes the country’s European orientation.

* **Blue and yellow**: These colors are often associated with **European unity** and reflect Bosnia and Herzegovina’s aspiration toward integration with European institutions such as the **European Union**.
* **Diagonal band with stars**: The stars symbolize **Europe**, while their incomplete appearance suggests openness and a forward-looking vision.
* **Shield**: The use of a shield maintains a link with traditional heraldry while simplifying previous historical and ethnic associations.

The current emblem was not chosen by domestic consensus but was imposed by the High Representative as a **provisional solution**. Despite this, it has remained in use for over two decades, becoming a de facto permanent national symbol.

---

### Political and Constitutional Context

Under the **Constitution of Bosnia and Herzegovina**, established by the Dayton Peace Accords, the country is a sovereign state made up of multiple constituent peoples—**Bosniaks**, **Croats**, and **Serbs**—as well as **others**. This framework has complicated the adoption of state symbols, as each group maintains its own cultural and historical identities.

The coat of arms, like the flag and national anthem, is considered **ethnically neutral**, in contrast to the entity-level symbols that reflect particular ethnic majorities. For instance, Republika Srpska and the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina each have their own coats of arms with cultural or religious symbolism relevant to their dominant groups.

Efforts to replace or revise the coat of arms at the national level have been sporadic and unsuccessful, largely due to political gridlock and differing views on representation. As a result, the 1998 emblem continues to function as a **compromise symbol**, acceptable to the international community and minimally offensive to domestic constituencies.

---

### Usage and Public Reception

The coat of arms is used by state institutions including the **Presidency**, **Parliamentary Assembly**, **Council of Ministers**, and **Constitutional Court**. It appears on official documents, buildings, and diplomatic missions.

Public opinion toward the national coat of arms remains mixed. Some see it as a **symbol of unity** and a step toward a shared civic identity, while others view it as an **externally imposed emblem** that lacks cultural resonance. Nonetheless, in the absence of a new agreed-upon symbol, it remains a functional emblem of Bosnia and Herzegovina’s statehood.

---

### Conclusion

The coat of arms of Bosnia and Herzegovina embodies the post-conflict, multiethnic, and internationally guided nature of the state. Designed to transcend ethnic divisions and reflect a common civic identity, it represents a political compromise rather than a national consensus. As such, it continues to play a vital role in symbolizing the country’s sovereignty, unity, and aspirations within the broader context of European integration and postwar recovery.
