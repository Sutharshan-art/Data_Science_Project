**Soviet Union–United States Relations**

**Soviet Union–United States relations** encompassed a complex and evolving diplomatic, ideological, and military rivalry that shaped much of the 20th century. These bilateral relations formally began with U.S. recognition of the Soviet Union in 1933 and ended with the USSR’s dissolution in 1991. Characterized by alternating periods of cooperation and intense hostility, the relationship was defined most prominently by the **Cold War**, during which the two countries stood as opposing superpowers on the global stage.

Though the United States and the Soviet Union were allies during **World War II**, their relationship quickly deteriorated afterward due to conflicting visions for the postwar international order, ideological opposition between capitalism and communism, and mutual suspicion. The resulting Cold War profoundly influenced international politics, economics, and military alignments.

---

### Early Relations and Recognition

Initial American relations with Bolshevik Russia were hostile following the **Russian Revolution of 1917**. The U.S. government opposed the Bolshevik seizure of power and supported anti-communist forces during the **Russian Civil War**. For over a decade, the United States refused to recognize the Soviet government, citing ideological differences, the Soviet repudiation of Tsarist debts, and concerns about communist subversion.

Formal diplomatic recognition occurred in **1933**, under President **Franklin D. Roosevelt**, motivated by hopes of mutual economic benefit and the need for strategic partners amid growing global instability. Despite the establishment of embassies, trust remained low, and numerous tensions persisted through the 1930s.

---

### World War II Alliance

The Nazi invasion of the Soviet Union in **June 1941** brought the USSR into an alliance with the United States and the United Kingdom. Following the Japanese attack on **Pearl Harbor**, the U.S. entered World War II and extended significant material support to the USSR through the **Lend-Lease program**. Together, the Allied powers coordinated military efforts against the Axis.

Despite military cooperation, underlying ideological and strategic tensions remained. Disagreements emerged over the timing of the Western Front invasion and the future of Eastern Europe. The wartime alliance was thus one of convenience rather than shared values, and suspicion persisted between leaders **Joseph Stalin**, **Franklin D. Roosevelt**, and later **Harry Truman**.

---

### Origins of the Cold War

Following Germany’s defeat in 1945, the alliance fractured. The United States and the Soviet Union pursued divergent postwar goals. While the U.S. promoted democratic institutions and open markets, the USSR sought security through the establishment of pro-Soviet regimes in **Eastern Europe**. The **Iron Curtain**, a term coined by Winston Churchill in 1946, symbolized the division of Europe.

The **Truman Doctrine** (1947) and **Marshall Plan** were interpreted by the USSR as efforts to contain communism and extend American influence. The Soviet response included the consolidation of **satellite states**, the formation of **COMECON**, and later, the **Warsaw Pact**.

Key events that solidified the Cold War dynamic included the **Berlin Blockade** (1948–1949), the Soviet acquisition of nuclear weapons (1949), and the outbreak of the **Korean War** (1950–1953), where the two powers supported opposing sides.

---

### Height of the Cold War

From the 1950s to the early 1980s, U.S.–Soviet relations were dominated by nuclear arms competition, proxy wars, and ideological confrontation. The **Cuban Missile Crisis** in 1962 brought the two countries to the brink of nuclear war but also marked the beginning of cautious crisis management mechanisms such as the **Moscow–Washington hotline**.

While Cold War confrontations played out globally—in Vietnam, Afghanistan, Latin America, and Africa—both nations engaged in arms control negotiations, including the **Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I and II)** and the **Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty**. The policy of **détente** in the 1970s, under Presidents **Richard Nixon** and **Leonid Brezhnev**, temporarily reduced tensions and facilitated economic and cultural exchanges.

However, détente unraveled by the end of the decade. The **Soviet invasion of Afghanistan** in 1979 led to the **U.S. boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics**, and the U.S. increased support for anti-Soviet forces globally, particularly through the **Reagan Doctrine** in the 1980s.

---

### Gorbachev and the End of the Cold War

A major turning point in U.S.–Soviet relations came with the rise of **Mikhail Gorbachev** in 1985. His policies of **glasnost (openness)** and **perestroika (restructuring)** aimed to reform the Soviet political and economic system and improve international relations.

Gorbachev’s willingness to negotiate led to a dramatic easing of Cold War tensions. His meetings with **President Ronald Reagan** and later **George H. W. Bush** resulted in key arms control agreements, including the **Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty** in 1987. Gorbachev also ended Soviet support for Eastern European communist regimes, enabling the peaceful revolutions of 1989.

By **1991**, with internal pressures mounting and the Soviet Union unraveling, U.S.–Soviet relations had shifted from confrontation to cooperation. The two countries collaborated on managing nuclear disarmament and diplomatic normalization. The final summit between Gorbachev and Bush in July 1991 confirmed the transformation of the bilateral relationship.

---

### Legacy

The collapse of the Soviet Union in December 1991 ended nearly six decades of formal U.S.–Soviet relations and marked the emergence of the **United States as the sole superpower**. Many of the structures and dynamics that characterized the Cold War—mutual distrust, arms buildup, and ideological competition—left a lasting imprint on global politics and international institutions.

While the Cold War no longer defines international relations, the U.S.–Soviet experience continues to shape contemporary U.S.–Russia relations, especially in areas such as nuclear policy, NATO expansion, and regional conflicts. The history of Soviet–American interactions remains one of the most studied and consequential in modern international affairs.
