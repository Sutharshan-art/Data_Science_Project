**United States**

**Overview**
The United States of America (USA) is a federal constitutional republic comprising fifty states and a federal district. With a population exceeding 330 million and spanning roughly 9.8 million square kilometers, it is both the world’s third-largest country by area and its third-largest by population. The capital is Washington, D.C., and the country is a founding member of the United Nations, NATO, and other international organizations.

**Historical Background**
The territory that became the United States was first colonized by European powers in the 17th century. Thirteen British colonies along the Atlantic coast declared independence in 1776, leading to the Revolutionary War (1775–1783). The 1781 victory at Yorktown and the 1783 Treaty of Paris secured sovereignty. In 1787, delegates drafted the U.S. Constitution, creating a federal structure with separation of powers; the document took effect with the first ten amendments—the Bill of Rights—in 1791.

Throughout the 19th century, the nation expanded westward under the doctrine of Manifest Destiny, acquiring territories through treaties, purchases (notably the Louisiana Purchase in 1803), and wars (including the Mexican–American War of 1846–1848). Tensions over slavery culminated in the Civil War (1861–1865), after which the abolition of slavery and Reconstruction amendments (13th–15th) reshaped American federalism and civil rights.

**Constitutional Framework and Federalism**
The United States operates under the Constitution’s division of power among three branches:

* **Legislative (Congress):** A bicameral body consisting of the Senate (two senators per state) and the House of Representatives (based on population). Congress enacts legislation, controls federal spending, and holds oversight authority.
* **Executive (President):** The President serves as head of state, head of government, and commander-in-chief of the armed forces. Elected to four-year terms via the Electoral College, the President implements laws, conducts foreign policy, and appoints federal officials—with Senate confirmation.
* **Judicial (Supreme Court and lower federal courts):** Judges interpret laws and review the constitutionality of legislation and executive actions. The Supreme Court’s power of judicial review, established in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), underpins its role as arbiter of constitutional disputes.

Federalism divides sovereignty between the national government and the states, each with its own constitution, legislature, governor, and judiciary. States retain powers not expressly granted to the federal government, including oversight of education, public safety, and local infrastructure.

**Political Parties and Elections**
Since the mid-19th century, U.S. politics have been dominated by a two-party system: the Democratic Party and the Republican Party. Third parties—such as the Libertarian Party and the Green Party—play minor roles but occasionally influence policy debates. Regular free elections, enshrined by amendments extending suffrage (e.g., 15th, 19th, 26th), occur every two years for Congress and every four years for the presidency. Primary elections, party conventions, and an Electoral College system shape candidate selection and final outcomes.

**Major Political Eras**

* **Founding and Early Republic (1789–1824):** Debates over federal versus state power and economic policy defined the Federalist and Jeffersonian Democratic-Republican factions.
* **Jacksonian Democracy and Sectionalism (1828–1860):** Expansion of white male suffrage and the rise of populist politics coincided with growing North–South divisions over slavery.
* **Civil War and Reconstruction (1861–1877):** The conflict preserved the Union and abolished slavery but left unresolved issues of racial equality and federal authority.
* **Gilded Age and Progressive Era (1877–1920):** Rapid industrialization, urbanization, and political corruption prompted regulatory reforms and the women’s suffrage movement.
* **New Deal and World War II (1933–1945):** The Great Depression spurred President Franklin D. Roosevelt’s expansion of federal social programs, while wartime mobilization solidified America’s global leadership.
* **Cold War (1947–1991):** Bipolar confrontation with the Soviet Union shaped domestic politics—anti-communism, civil rights progress, and expansion of the welfare state—and foreign engagements in Korea and Vietnam.
* **Post–Cold War and Globalization (1991–2001):** Unipolar ascendancy, economic liberalization, and technological revolution marked this period, alongside rising partisan polarization.
* **Post-9/11 Era (2001–present):** The September 11 attacks prompted the Global War on Terror, wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, homeland-security expansion, and renewed debates over civil liberties.

**Domestic and Social Issues**
Key ongoing political issues include health care reform, economic inequality, immigration policy, gun rights, and climate change. The civil rights movement of the 1950s–1960s secured legal equality for African Americans, influencing later reforms for women, LGBTQ+ individuals, and people with disabilities.

**Foreign Policy and International Role**
U.S. foreign policy has oscillated between isolationism—prominent in the early 20th century—and active global engagement. The United States has led or joined multilateral institutions (United Nations, NATO, World Bank), negotiated arms-control treaties, and engaged in peace-keeping and nation-building missions. As a permanent U.N. Security Council member and the world’s largest economy and military spender, the U.S. exerts significant diplomatic and economic influence.

**Contemporary Political Climate**
Since the early 21st century, American politics have grown sharply polarized, with deeply divided electorates, contentious Supreme Court confirmation battles, and frequent use of executive actions to bypass congressional gridlock. The rise of social media has transformed political communication, mobilization, and the dissemination of information. Electoral controversies, voter-suppression debates, and questions about the resilience of democratic norms underscore current challenges.

**Legacy and Influence**
The United States’ constitutional model, Bill of Rights, and democratic institutions have served as templates for other nations. Its political evolution—from a small federation of states to a global superpower—reflects ongoing tensions between liberty and order, federal and state authority, and majority rule and minority rights. The U.S. continues to navigate these dynamics as it addresses both domestic imperatives and global responsibilities.
