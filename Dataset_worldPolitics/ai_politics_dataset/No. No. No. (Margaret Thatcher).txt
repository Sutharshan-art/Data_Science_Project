**“No. No. No.” (Margaret Thatcher)**

**“No. No. No.”** is the colloquial title given to a statement delivered by British Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher on 27 October 1990, in which she emphatically rejected proposals to extend European Community (EC) social policy into the United Kingdom. Delivered during her speech to the Conservative Party conference in Blackpool, the phrase came to epitomize Thatcher’s Eurosceptic stance and her determination to preserve national sovereignty against further European integration.

## Historical Context

By 1990, the European Community was moving toward deeper political and economic union, building on the Single European Act of 1986 which had laid the groundwork for a single market. Among the pending measures was the so-called “Social Chapter,” a set of directives on workers’ rights, working hours, and health and safety standards negotiated under the Maastricht Treaty process. These measures, sponsored by the then French President François Mitterrand and German Chancellor Helmut Kohl, aimed to harmonize labour protections across member states.

Thatcher’s government had already secured an opt-out from the Social Chapter in March 1990, arguing that the United Kingdom’s flexible labour market and low unemployment made additional regulation unnecessary and potentially harmful to competitiveness. However, at the Conservative Party’s annual conference in October, she chose to restate that position in unmistakable terms.

## The Speech and the Phrase

On 27 October 1990, delivering her keynote address, Thatcher declared:

> “We have not successfully rolled back the frontiers of the state in Britain, only to see them re-imposed at a European level with all the implications for our economic life, our social life, and our vital civil liberties. And I say to you, no, no, no!”

The triple “no” underscored three separate refusals—to the Social Chapter, to federalist ambitions within the EC, and to further transfer of powers from Westminster to Brussels. The speech closed with sustained applause, reflecting a strong domestic Eurosceptic sentiment within her Conservative base.

## Political Significance

### Domestic Impact

Thatcher’s emphatic rejection reinforced divisions within the Conservative Party between pro- and anti-Europe factions. While her stance rallied many on the right who feared loss of national control, it alienated centrist and pro-European Tories. Within weeks, party dissatisfaction over Europe—coupled with rebellions over domestic policies such as the Community Charge (“poll tax”)—contributed to a leadership challenge. On 22 November 1990, Thatcher resigned as party leader and Prime Minister, succeeded by John Major, who adopted a more conciliatory line toward Europe.

### European Reaction

In Brussels, Thatcher’s speech was met with frustration by EC partners negotiating the Maastricht Treaty. The Social Chapter eventually formed Part III of the Maastricht Treaty text, but the UK secured a permanent protocol—annexed as Protocol No 30 to the Treaty on European Union—exempting it from implementation. This opt-out became a recurring touchstone in Britain’s relationship with the European Union until its withdrawal in 2020.

## Legacy

### Euroscepticism and National Sovereignty

The “No. No. No.” catchphrase entered British political lore as a crystallization of sovereignist opposition to supranational governance. It has been repeatedly cited by Eurosceptic politicians—most notably during debates over the Maastricht Treaty ratification in 1992, the 2016 Brexit referendum, and subsequent Withdrawal Agreement negotiations—to evoke Thatcher’s precedent for resisting deeper integration.

### Cultural Resonance

The phrase has been referenced in political speeches, media commentary, and popular culture as shorthand for firm rejection. It appears in numerous retrospectives on Thatcher’s premiership and is inscribed in the public imagination as a defining moment in late 20th-century British politics.

### Scholarly Assessment

Historians and political scientists view the “No. No. No.” speech as illustrative of the tensions inherent in the post-war European project. Thatcher’s unequivocal stance underscored the difficulty of reconciling national parliamentary sovereignty with supranational institutions. The episode is frequently studied as part of broader analyses of European integration, party politics, and leadership crises.

## Conclusion

Margaret Thatcher’s “No. No. No.” declaration remains one of the most memorable moments of her premiership, symbolizing the fraught relationship between the United Kingdom and the evolving European Union. Its immediate effect—Thatcher’s own political downfall—was eclipsed over time by its enduring role in shaping Eurosceptic discourse and Britain’s eventual decision to leave the EU. As such, the phrase stands as a testament to the enduring potency of national sovereignty in modern political debate.
