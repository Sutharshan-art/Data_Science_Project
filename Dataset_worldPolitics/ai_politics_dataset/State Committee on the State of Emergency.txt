**State Committee on the State of Emergency**

The **State Committee on the State of Emergency** (Russian: *Государственный комитет по чрезвычайному положению*, abbreviated as **GKChP**) was an ad hoc group of hardline Soviet officials who attempted to seize control of the Soviet Union from President **Mikhail Gorbachev** during a coup d'état in **August 1991**. The committee declared a state of emergency on August 19, 1991, citing the need to restore law and order amid rising nationalist movements and perceived chaos associated with Gorbachev’s reform policies, particularly **perestroika** and **glasnost**.

The GKChP coup attempt lasted three days and ultimately failed, but it had significant political consequences. It accelerated the collapse of the Soviet Union, undermined the authority of the Communist Party, and elevated the political standing of **Boris Yeltsin**, then president of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic. The failure of the committee marked the final unraveling of Soviet centralized power and the rapid disintegration of the USSR by the end of the same year.

---

### Background

By the late 1980s, the Soviet Union was experiencing profound political, economic, and social instability. Mikhail Gorbachev’s reforms, introduced after he became General Secretary in 1985, aimed to modernize the economy and increase political transparency. However, **perestroika** (economic restructuring) and **glasnost** (openness) had unintended consequences, including the weakening of central authority, the rise of nationalist movements in Soviet republics, and the economic downturn.

Amidst these changes, conservative elements within the **Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU)**, military, and security services grew increasingly alarmed. They believed the reforms were leading to the disintegration of the Soviet state and the loss of Communist control. Tensions heightened in mid-1991 as Gorbachev prepared to sign a **New Union Treaty**, which would grant greater autonomy to the republics. The treaty was scheduled to be signed on August 20, 1991.

Fearing the dissolution of the Union, a group of high-ranking officials decided to take matters into their own hands to prevent the signing of the treaty and to restore centralized power.

---

### Composition and Objectives

The State Committee on the State of Emergency was composed of eight prominent Soviet officials:

* **Gennady Yanayev**, Vice President of the USSR (acting as chairman of the GKChP)
* **Valentin Pavlov**, Prime Minister of the USSR
* **Boris Pugo**, Minister of Internal Affairs
* **Dmitry Yazov**, Minister of Defense
* **Vladimir Kryuchkov**, Chairman of the KGB
* **Oleg Baklanov**, First Deputy Chairman of the Defense Council
* **Vasily Starodubtsev**, Chairman of the Peasants’ Union
* **Alexander Tizyakov**, President of the Association of State Enterprises

The GKChP claimed that Gorbachev was incapacitated and unable to perform his duties. In reality, Gorbachev was placed under house arrest at his dacha in Crimea, isolated from communications and surrounded by KGB operatives.

On August 19, 1991, the committee announced that a **state of emergency** had been declared across the Soviet Union to restore stability. Tanks and troops were deployed in **Moscow** and other cities, while the committee banned strikes, demonstrations, and independent media outlets.

---

### Public Response and Collapse of the Coup

The GKChP's move was immediately met with resistance, particularly in Moscow. **Boris Yeltsin**, who had been elected President of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic in June 1991, emerged as the leading figure in opposing the coup. He condemned the GKChP as an unconstitutional junta and famously climbed atop a tank outside the **Russian White House** (parliament building) to rally public support.

Massive crowds of civilians surrounded government buildings, defying curfews and engaging in civil disobedience. Units of the military began to defect or refuse orders. Crucially, the elite **Tamanskaya Division**, sent to occupy Moscow, ultimately sided with the resistance.

By August 21, the coup unraveled. The committee members failed to consolidate control, and Gorbachev returned to Moscow. Several GKChP members were arrested, while others, such as Interior Minister Boris Pugo, died by suicide.

---

### Political Aftermath

Though Gorbachev resumed office, his authority was significantly weakened. The failed coup discredited the Communist Party, which was suspended shortly thereafter. Power rapidly shifted to the republics, especially to Yeltsin and the Russian leadership.

Over the following months, republics including **Ukraine**, **Belarus**, and the **Baltic states** declared independence. In December 1991, the **Belavezha Accords**, signed by Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus, formally dissolved the USSR, replacing it with the **Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS)**.

The coup attempt marked the end of Soviet Communist rule and the beginning of a new political era in Eastern Europe. It also served as a textbook example of how elite fragmentation and public mobilization can undermine authoritarian efforts to reverse democratization.

---

### Legacy

The GKChP coup remains a pivotal moment in modern Russian and post-Soviet history. It exposed the deep divisions within Soviet leadership and demonstrated the limits of state coercion in the face of public opposition and evolving political legitimacy.

In Russia, the events of August 1991 are commemorated as both a democratic victory and a moment of lost opportunity. Critics argue that the coup's failure led to political and economic turmoil in the ensuing transition, while supporters highlight it as a decisive rejection of authoritarianism and a catalyst for post-Soviet independence movements.

The GKChP’s brief and unsuccessful rule became symbolic of the final crisis of Soviet governance and the irreversible decline of the Soviet imperial model.
