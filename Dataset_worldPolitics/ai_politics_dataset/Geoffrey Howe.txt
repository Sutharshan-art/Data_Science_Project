**Geoffrey Howe**, Baron Howe of Aberavon (20 December 1926 – 9 October 2015), was a British Conservative politician and statesman whose ministerial career spanned the governments of Edward Heath and Margaret Thatcher. As Chancellor of the Exchequer (1970–1974) he implemented monetarist-inspired fiscal policies amid economic turbulence; under Thatcher he served successively as Leader of the House of Commons, Lord President of the Council and Deputy Prime Minister (1979–1983), and as Foreign Secretary (1983–1989), playing a central role in Britain’s relationship with Europe and in the politics that ultimately led to the end of the Thatcher premiership.

**Early Life and Education**
Born in Port Talbot, Wales, to a railway clerk, Howe was educated at Llandarcy Secondary School and won a scholarship to Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, reading Economics. After graduating in 1948 he served in the Royal Artillery and then read Law at the University of Cambridge, was called to the Bar at Gray’s Inn in 1954, and built a successful legal practice on the South Wales circuit. His early exposure to industrial South Wales shaped his pragmatic approach to economic and social issues.

**Entry into Politics**
Howe joined the Conservative Party at university and stood unsuccessfully for Aberavon in 1955. He entered Parliament at the 1959 general election as Member for Bebington and Ellesmere Port. In opposition he served on the Public Accounts Committee, championing financial probity and earning a reputation for meticulous fiscal expertise.

**Chancellor of the Exchequer (1970–1974)**
When Edward Heath became Prime Minister in June 1970, he appointed the 43-year-old Howe as Chancellor. Facing rising inflation and a balance-of-payments deficit, Howe shifted Tory economic strategy toward control of the money supply, public spending restraint, and a transitional incomes policy to curb wage inflation. He presided over entry negotiations into the European Economic Community (EEC), securing Britain’s first rebates on its budget contributions. However, the 1973 oil crisis and ensuing inflationary pressures forced wage freezes and public pay cuts amid mounting industrial unrest. The Conservative government’s unpopularity on economic grounds contributed to its defeat in the February 1974 election.

**Roles in Opposition (1974–1979)**
Defeated in his constituency in 1974, Howe returned to Parliament in October as MP for Surrey Heath. Under Margaret Thatcher’s leadership, he served on the frontbench in shadow finance and Europe briefs, advocating fiscal discipline and a pro-European stance that often put him at odds with party Eurosceptics. His standing as a sober, detail-oriented operator made him a key ally when Thatcher won the 1979 election.

**Leader of the House, Lord President, and Deputy Prime Minister (1979–1983)**
In Thatcher’s first government, Howe became Leader of the House of Commons and Lord President of the Council. He guided the government’s legislative programme—overhauling trade union laws, privatizing public enterprises, and reforming local government finance—through often hostile parliamentary opposition. In 1983 he was additionally named Deputy Prime Minister, acting as the government’s principal conciliator between ministers and Parliament while also chairing Cabinet sub-committees on the economy.

**Foreign Secretary (1983–1989)**
Promoted to Foreign Secretary in June 1983, Howe negotiated the 1984–86 enlargement of the European Community to include Spain and Portugal and worked to secure the Single European Act, which laid the groundwork for the single market. He also managed sensitive Atlantic Alliance relations, supporting the deployment of nuclear cruise missiles in the United Kingdom while fostering closer ties with the United States under President Ronald Reagan. Howe championed diplomatic pressure to end the Iran–Iraq War, advance Middle East peace talks, and oppose apartheid in South Africa.

**Resignation and Political Aftermath**
By the late 1980s, Howe grew increasingly uneasy with Thatcher’s Eurosceptic turn and her resistance to deeper European integration. On 13 November 1990 he delivered a landmark resignation speech in the House of Commons, critiquing the Prime Minister’s approach to Europe and Cabinet unity. His powerful address is widely credited with undermining Thatcher’s authority, precipitating her resignation ten days later.

**Later Life and Legacy**
Elevated to the House of Lords as Baron Howe of Aberavon, he served as UK Deputy Governor of the Bank of England from 1991 to 1997. He remained an outspoken commentator on European affairs, warning of the risks of British isolation from continental institutions. Howe’s blend of fiscal orthodoxy, legal precision, and pro-European conviction influenced successive Conservative governments and European policy debates. His tenure illustrates the tensions between national sovereignty and supranational cooperation that have shaped late twentieth-century British politics.

**Personal Life**
Howe married Elspeth Shand (later Queen Camilla’s sister) in 1953; they had three sons. Known for his soft-spoken demeanor and analytical mind, he continued to write, lecture, and advise on economic and international matters until his death in London on 9 October 2015, aged 88.
