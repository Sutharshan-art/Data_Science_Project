**Serbia in the Yugoslav Wars**

**Overview**
Serbia, as the largest republic of the former Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (SFRY), played a central role in the Yugoslav Wars (1991–2001), which encompassed conflicts in Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Kosovo. Under the leadership of Slobodan Milošević, Serbia pursued policies of Serbian national unification and protection of Serb minorities outside its borders, contributing to interstate and intrastate warfare, ethnic cleansing, and humanitarian crises. International sanctions, military interventions, and war‐crimes prosecutions profoundly shaped Serbia’s domestic politics and its relations with Europe.

**Historical and Political Context**
Following World War II, the SFRY was held together under Josip Broz Tito’s federal, multiethnic system. After Tito’s death in 1980, rising economic hardship, nationalist tensions, and democratization pressures fractured the federation. In 1989–1990, Milošević consolidated power in Serbia and, in alliance with other Serbian elites, pressured autonomous provinces Kosovo and Vojvodina to cede their autonomy, signaling a shift toward centralization and Serbian predominance.

**Wars in Croatia (1991–1995)**

* **Outbreak**: When Croatia declared independence in June 1991, ethnic Serb leaders in Krajina—backed by Belgrade—rejected secession. The Yugoslav People’s Army (JNA), increasingly controlled by Serbian officers, intervened ostensibly to protect Serb populations.
* **Conflict**: The JNA and local Serb forces (later reorganized as the Army of the Republic of Serb Krajina, ARSK) fought Croatian forces, seizing large swathes of territory, including areas around Vukovar and Dubrovnik.
* **Ethnic Cleansing**: Serbian forces forcibly expelled Croatian civilians in occupied areas. Human Rights Watch and UN investigations documented widespread atrocities.
* **Resolution**: A series of UN‐brokered ceasefires and the deployment of the UN Protection Force (UNPROFOR) stabilized front lines. In August 1995, Croatian offensives (“Operation Storm”) retook Krajina, displacing over 200,000 Serbs.

**War in Bosnia and Herzegovina (1992–1995)**

* **Declaration of Independence**: Bosnia and Herzegovina’s March 1992 referendum triggered conflict among Bosniaks, Croats, and Serbs. Bosnian Serb leaders, supported politically and militarily by Belgrade, established the Republika Srpska.
* **Siege of Sarajevo and Ethnic Cleansing**: The Army of Republika Srpska (VRS) conducted a prolonged siege of Sarajevo (1992–1996) and ethnic‐cleansing campaigns in eastern Bosnia (e.g., Srebrenica, 1995), resulting in the deaths of tens of thousands of Bosniak civilians.
* **International Response**: NATO launched limited air strikes against VRS positions in 1994 and more extensive strikes in August–September 1995, contributing to the Dayton Accords (December 1995), which ended the war and recognized Bosnia and Herzegovina’s territorial integrity.

**Kosovo Conflict (1998–1999)**

* **Escalation**: Rising tensions between Kosovo Albanian separatists (Kosovo Liberation Army, KLA) and Serbian security forces culminated in widespread insurgency in 1998.
* **Repression and Displacement**: Serbian police and paramilitaries conducted operations in Drenica and other regions, leading to accusations of ethnic cleansing against Albanian civilians.
* **NATO Intervention**: After the failure of peace talks in Rambouillet, NATO began an aerial bombing campaign against targets in Serbia and Kosovo on 24 March 1999. The 78‐day campaign ended on 10 June 1999 with the withdrawal of Serbian forces from Kosovo and the establishment of UN administration (UNMIK) and a NATO‐led peacekeeping force (KFOR).

**Domestic Political Developments**
Milošević’s wartime leadership fostered authoritarian rule, media control, and a nationalist narrative portraying Serbia as besieged protector of Serbs. Economic sanctions imposed by the United Nations (1992–1995, 1998–1999) and the destruction of infrastructure during the 1999 bombing precipitated economic collapse and humanitarian hardship. Opposition to Milošević coalesced in the Democratic Opposition of Serbia (DOS), which led mass protests during the “October 5th” revolution in 2000, culminating in Milošević’s ouster.

**War Crimes and Accountability**
The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY), established in 1993, indicted numerous Serbian and Bosnian Serb leaders for genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes. Notable trials included those of Slobodan Milošević (d. 2006), Radovan Karadžić, and Ratko Mladić, reflecting Serbia’s involvement in atrocities. Serbia’s cooperation with the ICTY—initially reluctant—improved after 2001, enabling the handover of multiple indictees.

**Post‐War Reconciliation and European Integration**
In the aftermath, Serbia embarked on democratic and economic reforms under new leadership. The legacy of conflict, refugee returns, and interethnic mistrust posed significant challenges to reconciliation. Serbia’s aspiration to join the European Union—and its candidacy since 2012—has been conditioned on cooperation with the ICTY, normalization of relations with Kosovo, and domestic rule‐of‐law improvements.

**Legacy**
Serbia’s role in the Yugoslav Wars remains a subject of intense introspection and debate. While Serbian society has made strides in confronting its wartime past, nationalist narratives persist. The state’s transformation from 1991 through the post‐Milošević era illustrates the complex interplay between nationalism, international intervention, and democratization in the Western Balkans.
