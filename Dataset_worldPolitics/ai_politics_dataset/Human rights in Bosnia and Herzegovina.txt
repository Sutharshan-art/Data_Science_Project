**Human rights in Bosnia and Herzegovina**

Human rights in Bosnia and Herzegovina are shaped by the legacy of the 1992–1995 war, the Dayton Peace Agreement’s institutional architecture, and ongoing efforts to align domestic law with international standards. While the country has made significant legal and institutional advances, implementation remains uneven, and challenges persist in areas such as minority rights, freedom of expression, and social equality.

---

## Historical and Political Context

The dissolution of Yugoslavia and ensuing conflict in Bosnia and Herzegovina resulted in large‐scale atrocities, ethnic cleansing, and the displacement of over two million people. The 1995 **Dayton Peace Agreement** ended hostilities by establishing a complex federal structure comprising the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (primarily Bosniaks and Croats), Republika Srpska (primarily Serbs), and the Brčko District under joint jurisdiction. Dayton included provisions on human rights protection, refugee returns, and the prosecution of war crimes, laying the groundwork for post-conflict reconstruction.

---

## Constitutional and Legal Framework

The **Constitution of Bosnia and Herzegovina**—annexed to Dayton—guarantees fundamental rights such as equality before the law, freedom of thought and religion, and free expression. However, certain constitutional clauses reserve key offices (e.g., Presidency seats) for members of the three “constituent peoples” (Bosniaks, Croats, Serbs), limiting full political participation by minorities (e.g., Jews, Roma, Others).

Bosnia and Herzegovina is party to major international human‐rights instruments, including the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR). The **Human Rights Chamber**, established under Dayton, was replaced in 2003 by a domestic judicial mechanism and direct access to the European Court of Human Rights; Bosnian citizens have since lodged numerous rulings addressing discrimination and property rights.

---

## Institutional Framework

Human‐rights oversight is divided across state, entity, and district levels:

* The **Human Rights Ombudsman of Bosnia and Herzegovina** investigates complaints of rights violations by state institutions.
* Entity‐level ombudsmen and commissions address local issues.
* Parliamentary committees on human rights exist in both the national Parliamentary Assembly and entity legislatures.
* Civil society organizations, including refugee and women’s groups, complement official bodies and monitor compliance.

Coordination among these institutions is complicated by overlapping competencies and political fragmentation.

---

## Civil and Political Rights

### Freedom of Expression and Media

Constitutional guarantees protect press freedom, but journalists face political pressure, threats, and self-censorship. Public broadcasters reflect entity and ethnic allegiances, contributing to polarized discourse. Defamation remains criminalized in some jurisdictions, and libel suits are occasionally used to intimidate investigative reporting.

### Electoral Rights and Representation

Elections are administered by the **Central Election Commission**, with generally free and fair procedures. However, the “constituent peoples” requirement bars some citizens of non-majority ethnicity from running for certain offices. The European Court of Human Rights ruled this provision discriminatory, prompting calls for constitutional reform.

### Return of Refugees and Property Rights

Post-war returns have been a central human-rights task. International agencies and the **Commission for Real Property Claims of Displaced Persons and Refugees** processed claims, enabling many to reclaim homes. Nonetheless, property restitution is incomplete, and segregated return patterns persist, affecting community reconciliation.

---

## Economic, Social, and Cultural Rights

Unemployment remains high (over 15 percent nationally), exacerbating social exclusion, especially among rural, Roma, and war-affected populations. Access to health care and education is constitutionally guaranteed, but disparities exist between entities and Brčko District. Women’s rights have progressed through laws on gender equality and domestic violence, yet traditional norms and under-representation in leadership roles limit full realization.

---

## Transitional Justice and War Crimes

The **International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY)** prosecuted senior wartime leaders for genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes. Domestic courts continue trials of lower-level perpetrators. The Missing Persons Institute addresses the fate of over 7,000 still-unidentified persons. Public memorials and reparations schemes contribute to victim recognition, though ethnic narratives often hinder shared remembrance.

---

## Minority and LGBTI Rights

Roma remain the most marginalized minority, facing inadequate housing, health care, and education. Recent laws prohibit discrimination on ethnic, religious, and gender grounds, but enforcement is weak. LGBTI individuals have legal protections against discrimination, but societal prejudice and limited recognition of same-sex partnerships constrain rights in practice.

---

## International Monitoring and Reform Prospects

The Council of Europe’s **Group of States against Corruption (GRECO)** and the **Venice Commission** have recommended legal amendments to strengthen human-rights institutions and reduce political interference. European Union accession talks have accelerated reforms in anti-discrimination legislation and judicial independence. Civil society campaigns advocate for constitutional reform to eliminate ethnic quotas and ensure universal political participation.

---

Bosnia and Herzegovina’s human-rights landscape reflects its post-conflict institutional design and the challenge of uniting diverse communities under a common legal framework. Continued reform efforts, international engagement, and civil society activism remain vital to advancing the full spectrum of human rights across the country.
