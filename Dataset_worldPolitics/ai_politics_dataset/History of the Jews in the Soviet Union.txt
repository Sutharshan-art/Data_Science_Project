**History of the Jews in the Soviet Union**

The history of the Jews in the Soviet Union (1917–1991) reflects the complex interplay of revolutionary egalitarianism, state‐sponsored nationalism, wartime exigencies, ideological campaigns, and periods of both official tolerance and severe persecution. Over seven decades, Soviet Jews experienced radical social transformation, cultural experimentation, institutional discrimination, and ultimately mass emigration.

---

## Early Soviet Period (1917–1928)

The October Revolution of 1917 abolished the Tsarist legal restrictions on Jews, granting them formal equality and citizenship. In 1918, the Bolsheviks disbanded the Jewish organizational structures of the old regime and created the **Yevsektsiya**, a Jewish section of the Communist Party tasked with eradicating Jewish religious, Zionist, and bourgeois influences. While Jewish cultural autonomy in Yiddish was initially encouraged—through secular schools, theaters, and publications—the Yevsektsiya suppressed Hebrew and religion as “counter‐revolutionary.”

In 1921, facing the impact of anti‐Bolshevik pogroms during the Civil War, the government instituted the **Jewish National Districts** policy (korenizatsiya), establishing local councils in areas of substantial Jewish population (e.g., in Ukraine and Crimea).

---

## Formation of the Jewish Autonomous Oblast (1934)

As part of Soviet nationality policy, the government founded the **Jewish Autonomous Oblast (JAO)** in 1934, centered on Birobidzhan in the Russian Far East. Intended as a socialist alternative to Zionism, the JAO offered Yiddish education, cultural institutions, and collective farms (**kolkhozy**). Although thousands of Jews gravitated to Birobidzhan in its early years, harsh climate and limited infrastructure hindered mass settlement. By the late 1930s, the JAO’s role had become largely symbolic amid broader centralization.

---

## Stalinist Repression and World War II (1937–1945)

During the **Great Purge** (1937–38), Jewish intellectuals, party cadres, and Yiddish writers were targeted alongside other nationalities. The dissolution of the Yevsektsiya in 1930 and later purges dismantled much of Jewish cultural leadership.

In World War II, approximately three million Soviet Jews—one third of the global Jewish population—fought in the Red Army, and many served as officers and partisans. The Nazis’ genocidal policies in occupied Soviet territories annihilated an estimated 2.7 million Jews. Soviet official discourse subsumed Jewish suffering under the general category of “Soviet citizens,” downplaying specifically Jewish losses.

---

## Postwar Anti‐Semitism and the Doctors’ Plot (1945–1953)

After 1945, returning Jewish veterans confronted renewed discrimination. State‐sponsored anti‐Zionist campaigns—framed as opposition to “rootless cosmopolitans”—targeted Jewish academics, writers, and artists. In 1952–53, the **Doctors’ Plot** accused predominantly Jewish physicians of conspiring to poison Soviet leaders, provoking an anti‐Semitic purge planned at the highest levels. Stalin’s death in March 1953 halted the campaign, and the accused were exonerated, but the episode underscored the precariousness of Jewish status.

---

## Khrushchev Thaw and Cultural Revival (1953–1964)

Under Nikita Khrushchev, overt state anti‐Semitism waned, and some Jewish cultural activities resumed. **Yiddish schools** and publications reopened, and Jewish writers (e.g., David Bergelson) were rehabilitated. However, many Jews found career advancement blocked by informal quotas (the “anti‐Jewish quota” in universities and professional bodies) and latent societal prejudice.

---

## Refuseniks and Human‐Rights Activism (1960s–1980s)

Beginning in the 1960s, a growing number of Soviet Jews applied for exit visas to Israel and were denied—a group known as **refuseniks**. Figures such as **Anatoly Shcharansky (Natan Sharansky)** and **Ida Nudel** became symbols of the struggle for emigration rights and human‐rights activism. International pressure, led by Western Jewish organizations and governments, elevated their cause. Trials and imprisonments of refuseniks for “parasitism” or “anti‐Soviet agitation” highlighted the regime’s repressive measures.

---

## Perestroika, Glasnost, and Mass Emigration (1985–1991)

Mikhail Gorbachev’s policies of **perestroika** (restructuring) and **glasnost** (openness) reduced emigration controls. Between 1989 and 1991, over one million Soviet Jews left for Israel, the United States, and Germany, dramatically reducing the Soviet Jewish population. In 1990, the **Congress of the Jewish People in the USSR** convened, and Jewish religious communities were legalized.

---

## Legacy and Contemporary Demographics

By the Soviet Union’s dissolution in December 1991, fewer than half a million Jews remained within its borders, down from approximately three million before World War II. Post‐Soviet states inherited Jewish communal structures: in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and the Baltic republics, Jewish organizations revived synagogues, schools, and cultural centers.

The Soviet experience left a complex legacy: the early revolutionary promise of equality and Yiddish secular culture; the forced experimentation of Birobidzhan; the devastation of the Holocaust; Stalin’s oppressive purges; and the later struggle for emigration and religious freedom. Contemporary Jewish life in the former Soviet Union reflects both the resilience of these communities and the enduring impact of Soviet policies on identity, culture, and demographics.
