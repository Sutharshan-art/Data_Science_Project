**Efforts to Impeach George W. Bush**

During and after the presidency of George W. Bush (2001–2009), various individuals and groups advanced proposals to impeach him, primarily in response to controversies over the Iraq War, warrantless domestic surveillance, and detainee treatment. Although none of these efforts gained sufficient traction to trigger a formal House inquiry, they reflect significant political dissent and debates over executive power in the post–September 11 era.

## Historical Context

Following the September 11, 2001, terrorist attacks, President Bush launched the **Global War on Terror**, including military operations in Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2003). Domestically, his administration enacted the **USA PATRIOT Act** (2001) and initiated the **President’s Surveillance Program** (2001–2007), authorizing warrantless monitoring of Americans’ communications. Reports of detainee abuses at **Guantánamo Bay**, **Abu Ghraib**, and CIA “black sites” further fueled criticism. Natural disaster response failures—most notably **Hurricane Katrina** (2005)—and evolving concerns about executive overreach catalyzed calls for impeachment.

## Early Petitions and Grassroots Campaigns

### MoveOn.org and ImpeachBush.org

Shortly after the launch of the Iraq War in March 2003, **ImpeachBush.org** formed to collect signatures on a citizens’ petition calling for Bush’s impeachment for “lying the country into war.” The group amassed over one million signatures by 2005 but characterized itself as an advocacy rather than a legal campaign. Similarly, **MoveOn.org** circulated online petitions and drove public demonstrations, pressuring Congress to consider impeachment for alleged constitutional violations.

### Congressional Petitions by Private Citizens

Individual constituents sent impeachment letters to their representatives throughout 2003–2007. While these petitions raised awareness, the House Judiciary Committee declined to act, and no member introduced a privileged resolution based solely on constituent demand.

## Congressional Resolutions and Statements

### Rep. Dennis Kucinich (D-OH)

On June 9, 2008, Representative **Dennis Kucinich** introduced H.Res. 1258, the most comprehensive impeachment resolution against Bush. It alleged eight “high crimes and misdemeanors,” including:

1. **Initiation of aggressive war** in Iraq without United Nations authorization.
2. **Authorization of warrantless wiretapping** in violation of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA).
3. **Imposition of illegal detention and torture** of terrorism suspects.
4. **Failure to secure New Orleans** prior to Hurricane Katrina.

Kucinich’s resolution garnered limited co-sponsorship—initially 18 Democrats—and was referred to the Judiciary Committee, where it languished without a hearing.

### Rep. John Conyers (D-MI)

As Judiciary Committee Chairman (2007–2011), **John Conyers** facilitated a June 2008 hearing on “Presidential Power” that examined the legal basis for the war in Iraq and warrantless surveillance. Conyers later introduced a narrower resolution (H.Res. 1200) focused on surveillance abuses, but it too failed to advance out of committee.

### Other Congressional Efforts

Between 2003 and 2009, various representatives introduced or supported impeachment resolutions:

* **Barbara Lee** (D-CA) proposed H.Res. 333 (2003) condemning the Iraq War and calling for impeachment inquiry.
* **Maxine Waters** (D-CA) introduced H.Res. 376 (2005) citing Katrina response failures.
* **Marcy Kaptur** (D-OH) and **Peter DeFazio** (D-OR) offered resolutions emphasizing detention and torture.

None gained majority support or were scheduled for floor votes.

## Senate Activity

No senator formally introduced an article of impeachment against President Bush. However, Senators **Russ Feingold** (D-WI) and **Barbara Boxer** (D-CA) publicly urged exploration of impeachment in response to warrantless surveillance revelations in 2006–2007, pressuring the Senate Intelligence Committee to hold additional hearings.

## Media and Public Opinion

Opinion polls conducted between 2005 and 2008 showed fluctuating public support for impeachment: a December 2005 Gallup poll found 40 percent in favor, but support waned to about 30 percent by mid-2007. Major newspapers—including *The New York Times* and *The Washington Post*—published editorials both supporting limited inquiries into executive conduct and cautioning against politically motivated impeachment.

## Legal and Constitutional Debates

Scholars debated whether Bush’s actions constituted “high crimes and misdemeanors.” Legal analyses considered:

* **War Powers Resolution** compliance and congressional authorization for the Iraq War.
* **FISA Amendments Act of 2008**, which retroactively legalized some surveillance programs.
* **Torture Convention** obligations under international law.

Conservative and centrist academics largely argued that policy disagreements did not merit impeachment, while others maintained that systematic constitutional violations did.

## Outcome and Legacy

With the 2008–2009 transition to President Barack Obama, impeachment resolutions against Bush were withdrawn or allowed to expire. No articles of impeachment reached the House floor. Nevertheless, the efforts to impeach Bush highlighted enduring tensions over executive authority, wartime powers, and civil liberties. These debates influenced subsequent legislation—such as stricter FISA oversight—and set precedents for later congressional responses to alleged presidential misconduct.

In retrospect, the impeachment campaigns against George W. Bush exemplify the mechanisms through which minority factions and grassroots activists seek congressional accountability, reflecting the constitutional checks and balances designed to restrain executive overreach in American democracy.
