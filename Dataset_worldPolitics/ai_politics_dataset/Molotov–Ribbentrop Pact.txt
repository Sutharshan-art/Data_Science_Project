**Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact**

The **Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact**, officially the **Treaty of Non-Aggression between Germany and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics**, was a neutrality pact signed on **23 August 1939** by the foreign ministers of Nazi Germany, **Joachim von Ribbentrop**, and the Soviet Union, **Vyacheslav Molotov**, in Moscow. The agreement stunned the world by uniting two ideologically opposed regimes—fascist Germany and communist Soviet Union—shortly before the outbreak of World War II. Its most consequential feature was a secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence, setting the stage for the invasions of Poland and the Baltic states.

---

### Historical Context

By the late 1930s, Nazi Germany under Adolf Hitler had repudiated the post-World War I settlement and pursued territorial expansion in Europe. Meanwhile, the Soviet Union, led by Joseph Stalin, sought security against potential German aggression but found Western diplomatic overtures—such as the proposed Franco-British alliance—inconclusive. Mutual distrust and competing interests in Eastern Europe prompted both powers to pursue a bilateral agreement.

---

### Negotiations

Negotiations began in early August 1939 after protracted diplomatic overtures by German envoys suggesting a non-aggression pact. Despite deep ideological antagonism, both sides recognized strategic advantages: Germany would secure its eastern flank for impending operations in the west, and the USSR would gain time to build its military and extend influence into Eastern Europe.

On **23 August 1939**, Ribbentrop and Molotov signed the public non-aggression treaty, committing each party to refrain from attacking the other, either independently or in conjunction with third parties, for a period of ten years. They also pledged to resolve disputes by peaceful means. The pact included protocols on economic cooperation, which supplied Germany with critical Soviet raw materials during the early war years.

---

### Secret Protocol

Simultaneously, Ribbentrop and Molotov agreed on a **secret protocol** detailing the partition of territories in Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence:

* **Eastern Poland**, **Lithuania**, and **Finland** fell within the Soviet sphere, with Germany renouncing claims to these areas.
* **Western Poland**, **Western Lithuania**, **Western Latvia**, and **Bessarabia** (part of Romania) were assigned to Germany.

A later amendment (dated 28 September 1939) shifted Lithuania from the German to the Soviet sphere. The protocol provided the legal-political justification for the coordinated invasions of Poland by Germany (1 September 1939) and the USSR (17 September 1939), leading to the country’s partition and disappearance from the map.

---

### Invasions and Occupation

Within days of the pact’s signing, Germany invaded Poland from the west, triggering Britain and France’s declarations of war on Germany. On **17 September 1939**, the Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland, claiming to protect Ukrainians and Belarusians. Polish forces, caught between two armies, collapsed swiftly. The joint occupation led to mass deportations, executions, and the silencing of Polish elites.

In late 1939 and early 1940, the Soviet Union forced the Baltic states—**Estonia**, **Latvia**, and **Lithuania**—to sign mutual assistance treaties and subsequently annexed them. The Red Army also engaged in the **Winter War** against Finland (1939–1940), carving off parts of Finnish territory under the Moscow Peace Treaty.

---

### Economic Cooperation

Under a **German–Soviet Commercial Agreement** (June 1940), the USSR provided vital raw materials—oil, grain, timber, and metals—to Germany, enabling the continued build-up of the Wehrmacht for campaigns in Western Europe. In return, Germany supplied machinery and military technology. This economic barter was a significant factor in Germany’s early war successes, including the invasions of Denmark, Norway, Belgium, the Netherlands, and France in 1940.

---

### Breakdown and Aftermath

Despite its public tenor of non-aggression, the pact was inherently unstable. Hitler’s long-term objectives included Lebensraum in the east and the destruction of Bolshevism. On **22 June 1941**, Germany launched **Operation Barbarossa**, invading the Soviet Union along a broad front. The surprise attack shattered the pact, drawing the USSR into the Allied camp and decisively altering World War II’s course.

After the war, the secret protocol’s legality was widely repudiated. In 1989–1991, the Soviet and later Russian governments formally denounced and declared the secret protocols void, acknowledging them as instruments of aggression.

---

### Political Significance

The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact had profound implications:

* **Delayed Allied cooperation**: By neutralizing the USSR temporarily, the pact undermined early attempts at collective security against Germany.
* **Partition of Poland and the Baltics**: The agreement facilitated the forcible annexation of territories and the redrawing of Eastern Europe’s political map.
* **Shifting alliances**: It exemplified realpolitik, as ideological adversaries forged tactical alliances.
* **Stimulus to Soviet military preparedness**: The invasion compelled rapid Soviet industrial and military mobilization, contributing to the USSR’s eventual resilience.

---

### Legacy

The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact remains a contentious subject in historical and political discourse. In Poland and the Baltic states, the pact symbolizes betrayal and occupation. In Russia, its legacy has been reassessed, with debates over its diplomatic necessity and moral consequences. The pact underscores the complexity of pre-World War II diplomacy and the tragic impact of ideological and strategic calculations on millions of lives.
