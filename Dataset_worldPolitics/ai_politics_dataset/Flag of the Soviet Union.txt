**Flag of the Soviet Union**

The flag of the Soviet Union was the official national banner of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) from its adoption on 30 December 1922 until the dissolution of the Soviet state on 25 December 1991. Characterized by a plain red field bearing a golden hammer and sickle beneath a five-pointed star outlined in gold, the flag symbolized the ideological foundations of the Soviet regime—communism, proletarian unity, and the leading role of the Communist Party.

### Historical Background

In the aftermath of the 1917 October Revolution and the establishment of the Russian Soviet Republic, Bolshevik leaders saw the need for a unifying emblem for the socialist state. Early revolutionary banners borrowed red—the color of social democratic movements—as their principal motif. As the nascent Soviet republics coalesced into the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics in December 1922, the Presidium of the Central Executive Committee commissioned a standardized national flag to represent the federal union of diverse republics under communist rule.

### Design and Adoption

On 30 December 1922, the Central Executive Committee approved a flag consisting of a solid red field with a golden hammer and sickle crossed beneath a single five-pointed star, all positioned in the upper hoist corner. The hammer represented industrial workers, the sickle symbolized peasants, and their crossing signified the union of these two classes. The star stood for the Communist Party’s leadership and the future global triumph of socialism.

The design was formalized by a decree of the Congress of Soviets on 12 November 1923, and minor graphic refinements followed in subsequent years. In 1924 a decree clarified proportions—1:1 by law, though in practice variants ranging from 1:1 to 1:2 were used. A definitive specification in 1955 prescribed a width-to-length ratio of 1:2, the hammer and sickle monumentally sized at 40% of the flag’s height, with the star just above them.

### Symbolic Meaning

* **Red Field**: Symbolized the blood shed by workers and peasants in the struggle for revolution and defense of socialism.
* **Hammer and Sickle**: Emblematic of the alliance between the urban proletariat and the rural peasantry, the social forces deemed necessary for building a classless society.
* **Five-Pointed Star**: Represented the leadership of the Communist Party and the guiding light of Marxism–Leninism, as well as the aspiration to spread socialism to the five continents.

The flag’s stark, geometric imagery and bright colors were intended to convey revolutionary zeal, ideological unity, and the break with monarchical and capitalist traditions.

### Variations and Republic Flags

While the red field, hammer and sickle, and star remained constant at the Union level, each constituent Soviet republic adopted its own flag from the 1930s onward, usually incorporating the republic’s initials and, in some cases, additional stripes or symbols. However, the USSR flag took precedence in all federal and military contexts, and republic flags were secondary in diplomatic and military use.

### Usage and Protocol

The Soviet flag was flown atop government buildings, factories, collective farms, schools, and military installations throughout the USSR. It was prominently displayed during revolutionary anniversaries (such as 7 November), May Day celebrations, and international events like the 1957 Moscow World Festival of Youth and Students. Regulations mandated that the flag be raised daily dawn to dusk and treated with the utmost respect: it was never allowed to touch the ground, and damaged flags were to be ceremonially incinerated.

On the battlefield during World War II, Soviet troops famously raised the banner atop the Reichstag in Berlin in April 1945, an image that became one of the war’s most iconic photographs. In naval use, the naval jack (worn at the bow) and the ensign (at the stern) featured minor modifications, such as a blue stripe along the bottom of the ensign.

### Political Context and Changing Meaning

Throughout its existence, the Soviet flag served not only as a state emblem but also as a political symbol of global communism. During the Cold War, it flew alongside the flags of Warsaw Pact allies, appearing at international communist gatherings and protests worldwide. Conversely, it was reviled by anti-communist movements as an emblem of authoritarianism.

In the final days of the USSR, as republics declared independence, the Soviet flag was progressively replaced by national banners dating from earlier historical periods or newly designed symbols. On 1 December 1991, Russian President Boris Yeltsin formally abolished the Soviet flag for federal use, reinstating the pre-1918 tricolor. The Soviet flag continued to fly in limited contexts until the formal dissolution of the USSR on 25 December 1991.

### Legacy

Today, the flag of the Soviet Union endures as a potent historical symbol. In former Soviet republics, it is often associated with the repression of Stalinist purges, forced collectivization, and curtailed freedoms. Yet among some leftist and nostalgic groups, it remains a symbol of social justice, worker solidarity, and the Soviet Union’s role in defeating fascism. In contemporary Russia, its public display is restricted, while in other successor states, it may be banned outright.

In vexillological and historical studies, the Soviet flag is recognized for its bold design and its role in embodying the political ideology of one of the 20th century’s major powers. Its simplicity and ideological clarity continue to attract analysis, remembrance, and debate.
