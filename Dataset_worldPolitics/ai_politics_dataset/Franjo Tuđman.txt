**Franjo Tuđman** (14 May 1922 – 10 December 1999) was a Croatian historian, politician, and statesman who served as the first President of Croatia from 1990 until his death in 1999. A leading figure in Croatia’s struggle for independence from Yugoslavia, Tuđman founded the Croatian Democratic Union (HDZ) and guided the country through the Homeland War (1991–1995), the postwar transition, and international recognition.

### Early Life and World War II

Franjo Tuđman was born in Veliko Trgovišće, then part of the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes. During World War II he joined the Yugoslav Partisans resisting Axis occupation, rising to the rank of major in the National Liberation Army. After the war he remained in the Yugoslav People’s Army (JNA), serving in intelligence and reaching colonel before retiring in 1961.

### Academic Career and Dissidence

After leaving the JNA, Tuđman earned a doctorate in historical sciences and became director of the Institute for the History of the Workers’ Movement in Zagreb. In the late 1960s he participated in the **Croatian Spring**, a reform movement calling for greater Croatian autonomy, which was suppressed by Yugoslav authorities in 1971. Arrested and imprisoned for “nationalist activity,” Tuđman spent the rest of the 1970s under surveillance. His books on Yugoslav and Croatian history were banned until the late 1980s.

### Founding of the HDZ and Rise to Power

With the collapse of communist regimes across Eastern Europe, Croatia introduced a multi-party system in 1990. Tuđman founded the **Croatian Democratic Union (HDZ)** on 17 June 1989, promoting national revival, political pluralism, and economic reform. In Croatia’s first multi-party elections (April–May 1990), HDZ won a plurality with 41 percent of the vote. Tuđman became President on 30 May after the Croatian Parliament amended the constitution to create a presidential system.

### Declaration of Independence and Homeland War

Amid rising Serb nationalism and armed unrest in Croatia’s Serb-majority areas, Tuđman’s government declared sovereignty on 25 June 1991 and full independence three days later. The Yugoslav People’s Army and Serb paramilitaries launched military operations against Croat-held territory, initiating the **Homeland War**. Under Tuđman’s leadership, Croatia mobilized its defense forces, secured arms—often clandestinely—and sought international recognition. By early 1992, Croatia achieved U.N. membership and diplomatic recognition from major Western states.

### Military and Diplomatic Strategy

Tuđman balanced military action with diplomacy. Croatia recaptured key areas in operations **Flash** (May 1995) and **Storm** (August 1995), reversing Serb territorial gains. Concurrently, Tuđman negotiated the **Vance–Owen** and **Z-4** peace plans—though rejected by local Serb leaders—and participated in the **Dayton Accords** (December 1995), which ended the Bosnian War and confirmed Croatia’s post-1991 borders.

### Domestic Governance and Reforms

Postwar, Tuđman oversaw the transition from a planned to a market economy. Privatization of state-owned enterprises, introduction of a convertible kuna (1994), and efforts to attract foreign investment marked his economic agenda. His government built new communications, energy, and transport infrastructure damaged during the war.

Politically, Tuđman centralized power in the presidency and HDZ. Critics accused his administration of authoritarian tendencies—media censorship, manipulation of elections, and suppression of dissent—and of favoring ethnonationalist policies that marginalized Croatia’s Serb minority. Supporters argued these measures were necessary for state-building and national cohesion after conflict.

### Foreign Policy and Regional Role

Under Tuđman, Croatia pursued integration with Euro-Atlantic institutions. He signed the **North Atlantic Cooperation Agreement** (1991) and applied for Association to the European Union (1996). Croatia joined the **Stabilization and Association Process** (2001 posthumously) and NATO’s Partnership for Peace (2000). Tuđman cultivated strategic partnerships with the United States, Germany, and Italy, while managing complex relations with neighboring Bosnia and Herzegovina over the status of Croatian-populated areas.

### Health Decline and Death

In the late 1990s, Tuđman’s health deteriorated due to cancer. He was re-elected in 1997 with over 61 percent of the vote but rarely appeared in public thereafter. Franjo Tuđman died on 10 December 1999 in Zagreb. He was given a state funeral, and his body was interred in the **Mirogoj Cemetery**.

### Legacy

Franjo Tuđman remains a divisive figure in Croatian history. He is credited with leading Croatia to independence, securing its territorial integrity, and laying foundations for a sovereign state. Monuments, institutions, and streets bear his name, and 10 December is observed by supporters as “Franjo Tuđman Day.” However, debates continue over his approach to minority rights, wartime conduct, and the concentration of political power. Historians assess Tuđman’s legacy as a blend of nationalist achievement and contested democratic practices, reflecting the challenges of nation-building in post-communist Europe.
