**Government of the Soviet Union**

The Government of the Soviet Union (USSR) operated from the country’s founding in 1922 until its dissolution in 1991. Structured as a federal one‐party socialist state, it combined formal soviet (council) institutions with the leadership of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU). Over seven decades, the Soviet government evolved through revolutionary beginnings, Stalinist centralization, post‐Stalin reform, Brezhnev‐era stability, and Gorbachev’s perestroika and glasnost, reflecting shifting political contexts and leadership dynamics.

**Founding and Constitutional Framework**
The 1922 Union Treaty established the USSR as a federation of the Russian, Ukrainian, Belorussian, and Transcaucasian republics. The 1924 Constitution formalized a governmental structure of supreme soviets at union and republic levels. The highest organ was the **Congress of Soviets**, meeting irregularly; between sessions, its authority passed to the **Central Executive Committee** (CEC). In 1936, Stalin’s Constitution replaced the Congress with the **Supreme Soviet**—a permanent legislature divided into the **Soviet of the Union** and **Soviet of Nationalities**—convening biannually to ratify policies and elect the **Presidium** as its standing body.

**Communist Party Dominance**
Although hallmark institutions mirrored parliamentary forms, real power rested with the CPSU. The **General Secretary** of the Party controlled appointments, policy direction, and oversight of state bodies. Under Stalin (1924–1953), the Party’s Politburo and Secretariat became supreme, with government ministries executing party directives. When Nikita Khrushchev (1953–1964) and later Leonid Brezhnev (1964–1982) led collective leadership models, the CEC and Council of Ministers operated under Politburo guidance. Gorbachev’s tenure (1985–1991) attempted to rebalance party and state, establishing the **Presidency of the USSR** in 1990 to strengthen state authority—but ultimately hastening fragmentation.

**Executive Branch and Administration**
The **Council of People’s Commissars** (Sovnarkom), created in 1917 and rebranded the **Council of Ministers** in 1946, served as the government’s executive arm. Headed by a Chairman (Premier), it oversaw economic planning, internal security, foreign affairs, and administration. Ministries and state committees reported to the Council, executing sectoral policies in heavy industry, agriculture, education, and internal affairs (through the Interior Ministry and later the KGB). Regional soviets and republican governments implemented central decrees, while local **soviets** provided grassroots administration.

**Economic Planning and Five‐Year Plans**
Centralized economic governance formed the USSR’s distinct model. The **State Planning Committee** (Gosplan) drafted successive **Five‐Year Plans**, setting production targets for industry, agriculture, and infrastructure. The government allocated resources through state budgets and administrative directives rather than market signals. Under Stalin, rapid industrialization and collectivization prioritized heavy industry; Khrushchev’s period saw agricultural reforms and the Virgin Lands Campaign; Brezhnev emphasized stability and consumer goods; Gorbachev’s perestroika (restructuring) introduced limited market mechanisms and enterprise autonomy.

**Judicial System and Repression**
Formally, the judiciary included **People’s Courts** and the **Supreme Court**, tasked with civil and criminal adjudication under the criminal code. However, extrajudicial bodies—most notably the **NKVD** and later the **KGB**—exercised broad powers for political surveillance, suppression of dissent, and enforcement of party orthodoxy. Show trials in the 1930s, purges of perceived “enemies,” and the Gulag labor‐camp system exemplified the government’s use of legal structures for state terror. After Stalin’s death, the government milderly reformed the security apparatus but maintained pervasive surveillance until Gorbachev’s glasnost relaxed strict controls.

**Federation Structure and Nationalities Policy**
The USSR comprised up to fifteen union republics, each with its own soviet, government, and constitution—yet subordinated to central authorities. The **Soviet of Nationalities** provided republic representation in the Supreme Soviet. The government pursued an official policy of “korenizatsiya” (indigenization) in the 1920s–1930s to promote minority elites, later shifting under Stalin toward Russification and centralized control. Gorbachev’s reforms granted republics greater autonomy in the late 1980s, fueling nationalist movements that contributed to the USSR’s collapse.

**Foreign Affairs and Defense**
Foreign policy was conducted by the **Ministry of Foreign Affairs** under the Politburo’s guidance. The USSR established the **Warsaw Pact** (1955–1991) and supported communist movements worldwide, juxtaposed with détente and arms‐control treaties (e.g., SALT I & II, INF Treaty). Defense affairs fell to the **Ministry of Defense** and the General Staff, subordinated to the Party leadership. Gorbachev’s “new thinking” reduced emphasis on ideological rivalry, leading to withdrawal from Afghanistan (1989) and the end of Soviet military commitments in Eastern Europe.

**Reform, Crisis, and Dissolution**
Gorbachev’s perestroika and glasnost (mid‐1980s) sought to invigorate governance through greater transparency, limited market reforms, and political pluralism. Creation of the **Congress of People’s Deputies** (1989) introduced contested elections, while establishment of the **Presidency** (1990) aimed to streamline executive authority. However, economic decline, political upheaval, and republic secessions overwhelmed central structures. The **August 1991 coup** by hardliners failed, accelerating the dissolution process. On **25 December 1991**, Gorbachev resigned, and the Soviet government ceased to exist, succeeded by the Russian Federation and independent states.

**Legacy**
The Soviet government’s blend of Party supremacy, centralized planning, and federal administration shaped 20th‐century political models worldwide. Its achievements in industrialization, education, and superpower status stand alongside its repression, economic inefficiencies, and failure to accommodate political freedoms. The USSR’s governmental structures continue to inform comparative studies of authoritarian regimes, federal systems, and transitions to democracy.
