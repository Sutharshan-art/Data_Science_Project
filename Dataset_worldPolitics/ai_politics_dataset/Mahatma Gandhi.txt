**Mahatma Gandhi**

**Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi** (2 October 1869 – 30 January 1948), commonly known as **Mahatma Gandhi**, was an Indian lawyer, anti-colonial nationalist, and political ethicist who led the Indian independence movement against British rule. Employing nonviolent civil disobedience as his core strategy, Gandhi played a pivotal role in mobilizing mass political participation and transforming the Indian National Congress into a platform for anti-colonial struggle. His political philosophy of *satyagraha* (truth-force or soul-force) not only shaped India’s path to independence but also inspired global movements for civil rights and freedom.

Gandhi's political activism was rooted in a moral vision that sought to integrate personal ethics with public leadership. His leadership marked a significant shift in the anti-colonial movement—from elite petitions and negotiations to mass mobilization and non-cooperation—and fundamentally reshaped Indian political identity in the early 20th century.

---

### Early Life and Political Beginnings

Gandhi was born in Porbandar, in present-day Gujarat, India, into a Hindu merchant caste family. He studied law in London and later worked as a barrister in South Africa from 1893 to 1914. It was in South Africa that Gandhi first engaged in political activism, organizing resistance against discriminatory laws targeting the Indian diaspora. His experience confronting racial injustice deeply influenced his political philosophy and solidified his commitment to nonviolent resistance.

Upon returning to India in 1915, Gandhi joined the Indian National Congress and quickly emerged as a prominent figure in the nationalist movement. Influenced by the teachings of Indian reformers and his experiences abroad, Gandhi called for moral regeneration as well as political liberation.

---

### Rise to National Leadership

Gandhi’s entry into Indian politics coincided with growing discontent over British colonial policies, particularly following World War I. In 1919, the British enacted the Rowlatt Acts, allowing for detention without trial, which sparked nationwide protests. Gandhi organized a campaign of *hartals* (strikes) and peaceful demonstrations, marking the beginning of his leadership in mass politics.

By the early 1920s, Gandhi had transformed the Congress into a mass movement representing peasants, workers, and the urban middle class. The **Non-Cooperation Movement** (1920–1922) called for Indians to boycott British institutions, including schools, courts, and products. Although the movement was suspended after violent incidents, it demonstrated the potential of civil disobedience as a political weapon.

Gandhi's emphasis on rural development, spinning (*khadi*), and self-reliance was not only symbolic but also strategic, aiming to weaken the economic foundations of British colonialism and unify a diverse Indian populace.

---

### Civil Disobedience and the Salt March

In 1930, Gandhi launched the **Salt March**, a 240-mile walk to the Arabian Sea to protest the British monopoly on salt production. This act of defiance mobilized widespread participation across India and drew international attention to the Indian independence movement. The campaign led to Gandhi’s arrest, along with thousands of others, but ultimately forced the British to negotiate with Indian leaders.

The **Round Table Conferences** in London (1930–1932) aimed to resolve constitutional questions, but failed to yield meaningful results. Gandhi continued to advocate for *swaraj* (self-rule), while also working to eliminate *untouchability*, promote interreligious harmony, and uplift marginalized communities.

---

### Role in World War II and the Quit India Movement

During World War II, Britain declared India a belligerent without consulting Indian leaders, prompting Gandhi and the Congress to demand immediate independence. In 1942, he launched the **Quit India Movement**, urging the British to leave India. The movement was met with harsh repression, and Gandhi was imprisoned for nearly two years.

Despite the failure of Quit India to immediately secure independence, it intensified anti-colonial sentiment and made British governance increasingly untenable. Following the war, negotiations resumed, and political momentum shifted decisively toward independence.

---

### Partition and Assassination

India gained independence on 15 August 1947, but the end of British rule came with the **Partition** of British India into the sovereign states of India and Pakistan. The partition led to one of the largest mass migrations in history and widespread communal violence, which deeply pained Gandhi.

In the final months of his life, Gandhi focused on calming sectarian tensions, especially in Bengal and Delhi. He undertook fasts and prayer meetings to promote Hindu-Muslim unity. On 30 January 1948, Gandhi was assassinated in New Delhi by **Nathuram Godse**, a Hindu nationalist who opposed his perceived appeasement of Muslims.

---

### Legacy and Political Impact

Mahatma Gandhi’s leadership redefined modern resistance movements by demonstrating the political efficacy of nonviolent civil disobedience. His ideas influenced global leaders including Martin Luther King Jr., Nelson Mandela, and Václav Havel. Politically, Gandhi helped lay the foundations for democratic nationalism in India and forged a mass political identity that transcended linguistic, religious, and caste divisions.

Critics have pointed to limitations in Gandhi’s approach, particularly regarding his views on industrialization, caste, and gender. Nevertheless, his impact on both Indian politics and global liberation struggles is enduring.

Gandhi remains a central figure in discussions of colonialism, ethics in politics, and strategies of resistance. His life and philosophy continue to shape contemporary debates about nonviolence, civil rights, and national sovereignty.
