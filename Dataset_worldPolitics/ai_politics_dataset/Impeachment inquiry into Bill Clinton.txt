**Impeachment Inquiry into Bill Clinton**

The impeachment inquiry into President Bill Clinton was a landmark political event in United States history, culminating in the House of Representatives’ formal investigation in late 1998. Rooted in allegations of perjury and obstruction of justice related to Clinton’s extramarital relationship with White House intern Monica Lewinsky, the inquiry intensified partisan divisions and shaped the broader discourse on presidential accountability, separation of powers, and political ethics.

### Background

In January 1998, Independent Counsel Kenneth Starr expanded his investigation—originally focused on financial improprieties in the Whitewater land deal—to include allegations arising from the dismissal of Clinton’s former aide, Vincent Foster, and later the Clinton–Lewinsky affair. When Lewinsky’s handwritten dress was turned over to Starr’s office, evidence emerged suggesting Clinton had lied under oath during a separate sexual harassment lawsuit filed by Paula Jones. Those sworn statements and subsequent misleading testimony formed the basis for allegations that the president had committed perjury and obstructed justice.

### Initiation of the Inquiry

On October 8, 1998, Starr submitted his report to Congress, detailing evidence that Clinton had provided false testimony and encouraged others to do likewise. The “Starr Report” was released publicly on October 12, delivering graphic descriptions of the relationship that ignited widespread media coverage and public debate. Two days later, Speaker of the House Newt Gingrich announced that the House Judiciary Committee would undertake an impeachment inquiry to determine whether the president’s actions warranted formal charges.

### Judiciary Committee Proceedings

Under the leadership of Committee Chairman Henry Hyde, the Judiciary Committee conducted a series of hearings from October through December 1998. Witnesses included Monica Lewinsky (whose grand jury testimony was read into the record), Linda Tripp, and other White House staff. Committee members—divided along party lines—debated the legal standards for impeachment, focusing on whether Clinton’s conduct met the constitutional threshold of “high Crimes and Misdemeanors.” Democrats argued that the misconduct was improper but did not rise to impeachable offenses, while Republicans maintained that deliberate perjury by a president constituted a breach of public trust requiring impeachment.

### Political Dynamics and Public Opinion

The inquiry unfolded against the backdrop of the 1998 midterm elections, in which Democrats defied historical trends by gaining seats in the House despite the scandal. This outcome signaled public weariness with what many perceived as partisan overreach. Polling showed a divided electorate: while a majority disapproved of Clinton’s personal behavior, fewer than half approved of impeachment as an appropriate remedy. Congressional Republicans faced criticism for tying up legislative business and risking public backlash, whereas Democrats leveraged the election results to argue for restraint.

### H. Res. 581 and Committee Vote

On December 1, 1998, the Judiciary Committee approved a resolution (H. Res. 581) authorizing the inquiry, with a vote largely along party lines. The resolution granted the committee authority to issue subpoenas, compel testimony, and gather evidence relevant to potential impeachment. Over subsequent weeks, committee members negotiated and drafted specific articles of impeachment, focusing on charges of perjury before the grand jury and obstruction of justice.

### Drafting of Articles of Impeachment

In mid-December, nine proposed articles were introduced by Republican committee members. After internal deliberations, the committee voted on December 11, 1998, to advance four articles to the House floor:

1. **Perjury before the Grand Jury**: Alleging that Clinton knowingly made false statements under oath concerning his relationship with Lewinsky.
2. **Perjury in the Paula Jones Case**: Accusing him of lying under oath about having a sexual relationship with Lewinsky.
3. **Obstruction of Justice**: Asserting that he had impeded law enforcement efforts by influencing the testimony of witnesses.
4. **Abuse of Power**: Contending that he used his office to conceal evidence and protect himself from legal accountability.

### House Floor Action and Outcome

On December 19, 1998, the full House debated the articles. After lengthy speeches and emotional appeals on both sides, the House voted to approve two articles: perjury before the grand jury and obstruction of justice. The voting margins reflected a narrow majority: 228–206 for perjury and 221–212 for obstruction. The two party-line defeats of the other two articles underscored the partisan split.

### Senate Trial and Acquittal

The approved articles were sent to the Senate in January 1999, where Chief Justice William Rehnquist presided over the trial. Senators effectively served as jurors, hearing evidence and arguments from House managers and White House counsel. In February, the Senate held votes on the two articles. Neither charge received the requisite two-thirds majority for conviction: the perjury article failed 45–55, and the obstruction article failed 50–50. As a result, Clinton was acquitted and remained in office for the remainder of his term.

### Historical and Political Significance

The impeachment inquiry into Bill Clinton had profound implications. It tested constitutional boundaries between the legislative and executive branches, raising questions about the nature of impeachable offenses and the role of partisanship in accountability processes. The inquiry’s timing, coinciding with midterm elections, highlighted voter attitudes toward ethics enforcement and legislative priorities. In subsequent years, the proceedings have informed debates over later impeachment efforts, such as those involving Presidents Donald Trump, and continue to serve as a reference point for discussions of presidential misconduct, legal standards, and political strategy.

Throughout the process, the inquiry underscored the tension between personal morality and public office, the capacity of Congress to check executive power, and the influence of public opinion on high-stakes constitutional mechanisms. The episode remains a pivotal chapter in American political history, illustrating both the strengths and vulnerabilities of the republic’s system of separated powers.
