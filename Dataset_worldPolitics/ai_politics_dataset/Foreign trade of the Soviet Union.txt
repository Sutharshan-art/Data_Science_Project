**Foreign Trade of the Soviet Union**

The foreign trade of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) was a key component of its centrally planned economy and international policy from its founding in 1922 until its dissolution in 1991. Heavily influenced by ideological commitments, geopolitical alignments, and domestic economic priorities, Soviet trade evolved through phases of New Economic Policy liberalization, Cold War autarky, and late-stage attempts at market integration.

### Early Period and the New Economic Policy (1921–1928)

In the aftermath of the Russian Civil War, the Bolshevik leadership instituted the **New Economic Policy (NEP)**, which permitted limited private enterprise and small-scale foreign trade to revive industry and agriculture. Under NEP, the USSR imported machinery, agricultural equipment, and consumer goods from Western Europe and the United States, while exporting grain and timber. Trade turnover remained modest, constrained by war devastation and ideological caution about capitalist dependence.

### First Five-Year Plans and Autarkic Shift (1928–1941)

With the launch of the **First Five-Year Plan** in 1928, the Soviet Union embarked on rapid industrialization and collectivization. Central planners aimed for self-sufficiency, reducing reliance on capitalist imports. Trade with Western countries declined sharply, while economic relations consolidated within the **Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (Comecon)** after its founding in 1949. Before Comecon, bilateral agreements were signed with Germany (1939 Treaty of Trade and Navigation) and Japan (1939), but global conflict soon disrupted such exchanges.

### Post–World War II Reconstruction and Comecon (1945–1953)

Following World War II, the USSR prioritized rebuilding its war-devastated economy. Through Comecon—which initially included Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Poland, and Romania—Moscow coordinated resource transfers: Soviet machinery and technical assistance flowed eastward, while Eastern Bloc raw materials (coal, iron ore, and agricultural products) supported Soviet industry. Trade with non-socialist countries remained limited and subject to **Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls (CoCom)** restrictions on technology transfer.

### Khrushchev Thaw and Limited Opening (1953–1964)

Under **Nikita Khrushchev**, the USSR relaxed some controls, slightly expanding trade with Western Europe and Canada. The 1957 **Nuclear Test Ban** and 1959 **Khrushchev–Eisenhower summit** at Camp David fostered modest détente. The USSR exported oil, natural gas, and metals to Western markets in exchange for machine tools, chemicals, and consumer goods. However, state planners maintained tight centralized control, and trade remained a fraction of GDP.

### Brezhnev Era and Oil Boom (1964–1982)

During the **Brezhnev stagnation**, rising global energy prices enabled the USSR to leverage its vast petroleum and natural gas reserves. Fuel exports—primarily to Western Europe—became the backbone of hard-currency earnings, financing imports of high-technology equipment and foodstuffs. At its peak in the late 1970s, oil and gas accounted for over 60 percent of Soviet export revenues. Meanwhile, the USSR continued to trade extensively within Comecon, often via nonmarket prices that masked inefficiencies.

### Gorbachev Reforms and Expanded Engagement (1985–1991)

Under **Mikhail Gorbachev**, Soviet foreign trade policy shifted toward **“perestroika”-era marketization** and diversification. The USSR sought **Most-Favored-Nation (MFN)** status from the United States and expanded joint ventures with Western firms—particularly in the Soviet Far East and energy sector. Membership in the **General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT)** was applied for, and **bilateral trade agreements** were negotiated with Japan, Italy, and France. Despite these efforts, systemic constraints—bureaucratic inertia, hard-currency shortages, and quality issues—limited significant expansion.

### Trade Structure and Composition

* **Major Exports**: Crude oil, natural gas, coal, iron ore, steel, timber, grain. These commodities provided essential foreign exchange.
* **Major Imports**: Machinery and equipment (especially computers, machine tools, and aircraft), pharmaceuticals, chemicals, foodstuffs, and luxury goods.
* **Trade Partners**: Early partners included Eastern Bloc states; Western partners by the 1980s included West Germany, Italy, France, and Japan. China was a minor partner until the late 1980s thaw.

### Trade Mechanisms and Pricing

Soviet trade operated largely through centralized state ministries and trading organizations rather than market mechanisms. Prices within Comecon were often set by the **International Pricing Commission**, leading to persistent imbalances and black-market arbitrage. Hard-currency trade with non-socialist countries required state approval and was subject to stringent foreign-exchange controls.

### Challenges and Decline

From the mid-1980s, falling world oil prices—down from over \$30/barrel in 1980 to under \$10/barrel by 1986—exacerbated fiscal pressures, causing foreign-exchange shortages. Attempts to ramp up manufactured exports—electronics, automobiles—were hampered by low competitiveness. Corruption and mismanagement further eroded trade efficacy. By 1990, trade volume had stagnated, contributing to broader economic crisis and playing a role in the USSR’s eventual collapse.

### Legacy

The Soviet foreign trade system highlights the tensions between autarkic planning and global economic integration. While energy exports temporarily bolstered state finances, overreliance on commodities and inefficient trade structures undermined sustainable growth. Post-1991 Russia and other successor states inherited this legacy, undertaking reforms toward market-based trade and greater participation in global institutions—such as the World Trade Organization, which Russia joined in 2012—reflecting a departure from Soviet-era practices.
