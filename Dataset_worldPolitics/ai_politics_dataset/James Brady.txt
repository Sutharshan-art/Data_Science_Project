**James Scott “Jim” Brady** (August 29, 1940 – August 4, 2014) was an American political figure and gun control advocate best known for his role as White House Press Secretary under President Ronald Reagan and for surviving an assassination attempt on Reagan that left him permanently disabled. His subsequent activism led to the landmark Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act (“Brady Bill”) of 1993, which instituted federal background checks on firearm purchases.

### Early Life and Education

Born in Centralia, Illinois, Brady was raised in Bloomington, Indiana, the eldest of four children. He graduated from Bishop MacNamara High School in Kankakee, Illinois, in 1958 and earned a B.S. in political science from Illinois State University in 1962. Active in campus politics, he met and married Sarah Jane Kemp in 1962. After working in state government and political campaigns, Brady joined the Richard Nixon presidential transition team in 1969 and moved to Washington, D.C.

### Rise in Republican Politics

In the Nixon administration, Brady served as Deputy Press Secretary under Ronald Ziegler, honing his skills as a communicator. He continued in the Ford White House (1974–1977), then worked on President Gerald Ford’s 1976 reelection campaign. Following Ford’s loss, Brady became a founding partner in a Washington public relations firm, Earnhardt & Brady, before returning to government service in 1981.

### White House Press Secretary

On January 21, 1981, Ronald Reagan appointed Brady White House Press Secretary. At age 40, Brady was among the youngest in that role, overseeing daily press briefings during the administration’s first weeks, a time marked by economic recession, the Iran hostage crisis’s resolution, and debates over tax cuts and deregulation. He was known for his polished delivery and ability to clarify complex policy decisions amid tense Cold War dynamics.

### Assassination Attempt and Aftermath

On March 30, 1981, as Brady and President Reagan left the Washington Hilton Hotel, John Hinckley Jr. attempted to assassinate the president. Brady was wounded by a .22‐caliber bullet that struck him in the head. Immediate surgery at George Washington University Hospital saved his life, but he was left permanently paralyzed on the left side and with limited speech and vision. He resigned as Press Secretary in June but remained active in the Reagan administration as a special assistant, advising on communications and senior staff morale.

### Transition to Advocacy and the Brady Campaign

After five years of rehabilitation, Brady and his wife Sarah founded the James S. and Sarah L. Brady Center to Prevent Gun Violence in 1989. Drawing on his personal experience, Brady lobbied Congress for stricter gun control. His efforts culminated in the 1993 Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act, signed by President Bill Clinton. The law mandated a five‐day waiting period and background checks for handgun purchases from federally licensed dealers. The Brady Act was a watershed in U.S. firearms policy, creating the National Instant Criminal Background Check System (NICS), which processed over 10 million checks in its first decade.

### Role in the George H. W. Bush Administration

President George H. W. Bush appointed Brady Special Assistant to the President for Public Liaison in 1992, a position he held until 1993. In this capacity, Brady continued to advocate for gun control measures, working with community and faith‐based organizations to build broad coalitions. He also advised on issues related to disability rights, drawing attention to accessibility and federal compliance with the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990).

### Legacy and Impact

Brady’s activism reshaped the gun‐control debate in the United States. While political polarization over firearms persists, the Brady Act remains a cornerstone of federal regulation, credited with preventing millions of unlawful purchases and access by felons and those with serious mental health histories. The Brady Center—now the Brady Campaign to Prevent Gun Violence—has continued to litigate, lobby, and educate on gun violence prevention, inspired by Brady’s legacy.

In addition to his gun‐control work, Brady served on nonprofit boards advocating for people with traumatic brain injuries and spinal cord injuries, reflecting his own rehabilitation journey. He received numerous honors, including the Presidential Medal of Freedom posthumously in 2014.

### Death

Brady suffered several health setbacks related to his 1981 injury and later battled chronic infections and heart disease. He died at his home in Alexandria, Virginia, on August 4, 2014, at age 73. His death prompted bipartisan recognition of his public service, with President Barack Obama and congressional leaders from both parties lauding his courage, dedication, and transformative impact on American policy.

James Brady’s career spanned journalism, political communication, and advocacy. His transition from White House Press Secretary to leading gun‐control advocate illustrates the interplay between personal tragedy and public policy, underscoring how individual experience can catalyze enduring legislative change.
