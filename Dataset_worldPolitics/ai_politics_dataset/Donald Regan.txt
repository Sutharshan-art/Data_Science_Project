**Donald Thomas Regan**

**Donald Thomas Regan** (December 21, 1918 – June 10, 2003) was an American businessman and Republican politician who served as the 66th United States Secretary of the Treasury (1981–1985) and the 11th White House Chief of Staff (1985–1987) under President Ronald Reagan. Regan played a central role in shaping the administration’s supply-side economic agenda—known as **Reaganomics**—and later managed White House operations during the Iran–Contra affair, ultimately resigning amid internal White House conflicts and scandal fallout.

## Early Life and Business Career

Born in Cambridge, Massachusetts, Regan graduated from Bowdoin College in 1939 and earned a law degree from Harvard Law School in 1942. He joined the United States Marine Corps during World War II, serving as a pilot in the Pacific theater and earning the Distinguished Flying Cross. After the war, Regan entered finance, joining the Boston-based brokerage **Smith Barney & Co.** in 1945. He rose through the ranks, becoming president in 1965 and chairman in 1975. Under his leadership, Smith Barney expanded substantially, emphasizing corporate finance, mergers and acquisitions, and international operations, reflecting the era’s growing globalization of capital markets.

## Secretary of the Treasury (1981–1985)

### Appointment and Economic Context

President Reagan appointed Regan Secretary of the Treasury in January 1981, following a landslide electoral victory. Reagan’s administration faced stagflation—a combination of high inflation, high unemployment, and slow growth—largely attributed to expansive fiscal policy and regulatory inflation of the 1970s. Regan, a proponent of **supply-side economics**, worked closely with Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker to curb inflation through tight monetary policy while advocating for steep tax cuts to stimulate investment and productivity.

### Policy Achievements

* **Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981**: Regan helped design and implement this landmark legislation, which cut individual marginal tax rates by 25 percent over three years, indexed the tax code for inflation, and introduced accelerated depreciation for business investment.
* **Tax Reform Act of 1986 (Conceptualization)**: Although signed after his tenure, Regan’s Treasury staff laid groundwork for the later 1986 overhaul that broadened the tax base and lowered the top rate from 50 to 28 percent.
* **Regulatory Reform and Deregulation**: Regan supported deregulation initiatives in transportation and telecommunications and championed the removal of “red tape” that he argued stifled economic growth.
* **Deficit and Debt**: While inflation declined from 13.5 percent in 1980 to 3.5 percent in 1983, federal budget deficits ballooned due to increased defense spending and the revenue impact of tax cuts. Regan resigned in December 1985 amid growing public concern about national debt.

## White House Chief of Staff (1985–1987)

### Transition to Chief of Staff

Replaced as Treasury Secretary by James Baker, Regan became White House Chief of Staff in February 1985. In this role, he centralized decision-making processes and sought to streamline presidential scheduling and communications. His appointment reflected Reagan’s trust in Regan’s managerial abilities and his desire to professionalize White House operations.

### Iran–Contra Affair and Internal Conflicts

During Regan’s tenure, the **Iran–Contra affair** emerged, involving covert arms sales to Iran and diversion of proceeds to Contra rebels in Nicaragua—actions undertaken without explicit Congressional authorization. As Chief of Staff, Regan was drawn into the scandal’s management, balancing loyalty to the president with legislative and legal investigations.

Regan also clashed with First Lady Nancy Reagan, who favored a “southern California” style of White House scheduling and personnel decisions. Their personal and professional rift became public during the Iran–Contra hearings, when Nancy’s influence over key appointments and private reservations about Regan’s style undermined his authority.

### Resignation

In November 1987, Regan abruptly resigned under pressure from Nancy Reagan and senior aides, amid the heaviest scrutiny of the Iran–Contra affair. His departure followed the release of tapes and testimony that raised questions about White House management and accountability. He was succeeded by Howard Baker.

## Later Life and Legacy

After leaving government, Regan retired to his estate in Massachusetts and authored his memoir, *For the Record: From Wall Street to Washington*, published in 1988. In it, he defended his administration’s economic policies and criticized the First Lady’s role in his ouster.

Regan’s legacy is mixed. He is credited with advancing the Reagan administration’s economic agenda, contributing to the period’s substantial economic growth and inflation reduction. Critics, however, fault the era’s fiscal deficits, rising income inequality, and the conflation of monetary and fiscal objectives that led to unsustainable debt levels. As Chief of Staff, his centralized management style and role during the Iran–Contra scandal underscore ongoing debates about executive power, White House staffing structures, and the balance between loyalty and oversight in presidential administrations.

Donald Regan died of congestive heart failure on June 10, 2003, in Villanova, Pennsylvania, at the age of 84. His papers, housed at the **Reagan Library** and **Bowdoin College**, continue to inform studies of Reagan-era politics, supply-side economics, and the evolution of White House staff roles in modern U.S. governance.
