**Who Dares Wins**

**Introduction**
*Who Dares Wins* is a 1982 British political action thriller film directed by Andrew V. McLaglen and written by Reginald Rose and David Sheldon. Its title references the motto of the British Special Air Service (SAS). The film stars Lewis Collins as Captain Peter Skellen, a newly seconded SAS officer, and Judy Davis as investigative journalist Helen Grant. Loosely inspired by the SAS’s involvement in counterterrorism operations—particularly the 1976 Iranian Embassy siege—the film situates its fictional narrative within the turbulent climate of late-1970s and early-1980s political violence in the United Kingdom.

**Plot Synopsis**
The plot follows PC Jamie Edwards (Lewis Collins), an ambitious Metropolitan Police officer recruited into the SAS after foiling an IRA bombing. Under the command of Major David Nairn (Edward Woodward), Edwards undergoes rigorous jungle and urban counterterrorism training. Concurrently, investigative reporter Helen Grant suspects a right-wing paramilitary conspiracy aiming to overthrow the British government. When the group—calling itself “Free Britain”—takes hostages at the U.S. Embassy in London, the SAS, now led by Edwards, mounts a high-stakes rescue operation. The film culminates in a dramatic breach and the exposure of extremist collusion between political insiders and domestic terrorists.

**Historical and Political Context**
During the 1970s and early 1980s, the United Kingdom faced a series of politically motivated violent campaigns. The Provisional Irish Republican Army (IRA) conducted bombings and hostage-takings aimed at ending British rule in Northern Ireland. Simultaneously, fringe right-wing groups, such as the National Front, sought to exploit social unrest amid economic decline and debates over immigration. The 1976 Iranian Embassy siege in London—successfully resolved by the SAS—galvanized public perception of elite counterterrorism forces. *Who Dares Wins* drew on these events to dramatize both the evolving domestic terrorist threat and the controversial expansion of military-style policing in peacetime Britain.

**Production**
The film was produced by Euan Lloyd and filmed on location in London and at Pinewood Studios. The screenplay combined original fiction with composite elements of real events—most notably, the IRA’s 1974 car bombing campaign and the hostage rescue at the Iranian Embassy. Lewis Collins, known for his role in the television series *The Professionals*, performed many of his own stunts to lend authenticity to the SAS operations. Director McLaglen, a veteran of Westerns and action films, emphasized realistic portrayals of small-unit tactics, intensive training sequences, and politically charged back-room intrigue.

**Themes**
*Who Dares Wins* engages with themes of state power, civil liberties, and the moral ambiguity of counterterrorism. The film depicts the SAS as disciplined and apolitical, yet it also portrays political figures manipulating extremist violence to justify authoritarian measures. The journalist Helen Grant embodies liberal concern for transparency and accountability, challenging the military-police nexus. The narrative raises questions about the limits of force in a democracy and the potential for political exploitation of fear.

**Reception**
Upon release, *Who Dares Wins* generated polarized responses. Supporters praised its taut action sequences, topical relevance, and sympathetic portrayal of the SAS. Critics, however, accused the film of right-wing propaganda, alleging it endorsed the “strong-hand” approach to domestic security and downplayed civil-liberties concerns. Several reviewers noted that its portrayal of a far-right conspiracy suggested equivalence between left-wing and right-wing extremism. At the British box office, the film achieved moderate success, but its controversial political overtones limited wider acclaim.

**Political Impact and Controversy**
The film’s release coincided with Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher’s administration, which pursued tough policies on law and order and Northern Ireland—most notably the 1981–82 hunger strikes by Irish republican prisoners. *Who Dares Wins* was interpreted by some as artistic reinforcement of Thatcherite security measures. Veteran politicians and human-rights advocates criticized the film for glamorizing military solutions to political problems. The debate over its political messaging reflected broader societal tensions regarding terrorism, state authority, and individual rights.

**Legacy**
Over time, *Who Dares Wins* has achieved cult status among action-film aficionados and those interested in military cinema. Its depiction of close-quarters combat and counterterrorism training influenced subsequent films and television series exploring special-operations themes. Historically, it remains a cinematic artifact of early-1980s Britain, encapsulating contemporaneous fears of political violence and the appeal of elite intervention. While never officially endorsed by the British military, the film contributed to public fascination with the SAS and the complexities of democratic security policy.

**Home Media and Adaptations**
*Who Dares Wins* has been released on VHS, DVD, and Blu-ray, often supplemented with interviews and “making-of” documentaries that explore the film’s relation to real SAS operations. No direct remakes have been produced, but its narrative style and political framing have inspired later works examining the intersection of terrorism, politics, and special forces.
