**Greater Serbia** (Serbian: **Велика Србија**, *Velika Srbija*) is an irredentist and nationalist political concept aiming to unite all territories perceived as historically or ethnically Serbian into a single state. The idea has evolved from 19th-century pan-Slavic movements to its most contentious manifestation during the breakup of Yugoslavia in the 1990s, when it underpinned armed conflicts and ethnic expulsions across the Balkans.

**Historical Origins**
The concept of uniting Serbian lands emerged in the mid-19th century amid the decline of the Ottoman and Habsburg empires. Influenced by Romantic nationalism and the pan-Slavic idea, Serbian intellectuals and political leaders envisioned a state encompassing not only the Principality of Serbia (autonomous after 1830) but also regions with significant Serbian populations—Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia (especially Dalmatia and Slavonia), Montenegro, parts of Macedonia, and the Sandžak region.

Key early proponents included Ilija Garašanin, author of the 1844 *Načertanije*, which outlined Serbian expansionist aims, and the Serbian Orthodox Church, which fostered cultural unity among Serbs under foreign rule. The Balkan Wars (1912–13) and World War I saw the expansion of the Kingdom of Serbia, later unified with South Slavic lands to form the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes (renamed Yugoslavia in 1929). While Yugoslav unification temporarily subsumed Greater Serbia claims under broader South Slavic statehood, underlying tensions persisted.

**Interwar and World War II Era**
During the interwar period, Serb‐dominated political structures in the Kingdom of Yugoslavia spurred accusations of “Serbian hegemony” from Croatian and Slovenian politicians. In World War II, some Chetnik factions led by Draža Mihailović espoused a Greater Serbia vision through ethnic cleansing campaigns against non-Serbs in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, and the Sandžak region, though the movement was fragmented and contested by the communist Partisans.

**Yugoslav Republic and Suppression**
Under Josip Broz Tito’s Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (1945–1991), nationalist ideologies were officially suppressed in favor of “brotherhood and unity.” Administrative republics—Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Macedonia, Montenegro, Serbia (including the autonomous provinces of Vojvodina and Kosovo), and Slovenia—guaranteed equal status. Nevertheless, debates over republican autonomy versus centralism continued, and undercurrents of Serbian nationalism reemerged during economic crisis in the 1980s.

**Resurgence in the 1990s**
The collapse of communist regimes across Eastern Europe and the weakening of federal institutions in the late 1980s revived nationalist movements. Slobodan Milošević, rising to power in Serbia in 1987, initially framed his agenda around protecting Serbs in Kosovo. By 1989–1990, he endorsed Serbian nationalist rhetoric, calling for the unification of Serbian territories and asserting the right to intervene in republics where Serbs were a significant minority.

During the Yugoslav Wars (1991–1995), Serbian political and paramilitary forces sought to establish “Serb autonomous regions” in Croatia and Bosnia. In Croatia, the self‐proclaimed Republic of Serbian Krajina controlled areas with substantial Serb populations. In Bosnia and Herzegovina, the Republika Srpska (established in January 1992) pursued ethnically homogeneous territories through campaigns of ethnic cleansing. In 1995, the Dayton Accords recognized Republika Srpska as one of two entities within Bosnia, institutionalizing the wartime partition.

**Ideological Tenets**
Greater Serbia ideology rests on several tenets:

* **Ethnic Principle**: Territories with a historical or contemporary Serbian majority or minority are deemed part of the Serbian national homeland.
* **Historical Claims**: Appeals to medieval Serbian kingdoms, the Serbian Empire under Stefan Dušan (14th century), and Ottoman-era migrations underpin territorial assertions.
* **Protectorate Justification**: The state’s duty to protect Serbs outside Serbia proper legitimized cross‐border interventions in the 1990s.

Scholars note that the ideology blends nationalism, historical mythmaking, and realpolitik, varying in scope from conservative revisionism (advocating cultural unity and minority rights) to maximalist irredentism (calling for redrawn borders).

**International Reaction and Legacy**
The pursuit of a Greater Serbia in the 1990s prompted widespread condemnation and international intervention. United Nations sanctions, NATO air campaigns, and the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) prosecutions targeted leaders responsible for war crimes, including Slobodan Milošević. The ICTY convicted several figures for crimes against humanity committed in pursuit of ethnically cleansed territories.

In Serbia, post‐Milošević governments formally renounced irredentist aims, joining the Council of Europe in 2003 and applying for European Union membership. Nevertheless, Greater Serbia sentiments persist in fringe nationalist parties and among some diaspora groups, often resurfacing during political crises or commemorations of wartime events.

**Contemporary Context**
Today’s Serbia asserts cooperation with neighbors and EU integration as official policy, though disputes over Kosovo’s independence (declared in 2008) remain a focal point for nationalist rhetoric. Serbian minority communities in Bosnia and Croatia occupy protected status under the Dayton framework and EU minority‐rights standards, reducing demands for territorial revision but keeping historical grievances alive.
