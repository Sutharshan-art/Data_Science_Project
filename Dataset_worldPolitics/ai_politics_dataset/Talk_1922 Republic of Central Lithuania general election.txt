\==Introduction==
The 1922 general election of the Republic of Central Lithuania was held on 8 January 1922 to select deputies for the Vilnius Sejm, the unicameral parliament of the short-lived state. Created in October 1920 following General Lucjan Żeligowski’s mutiny, Central Lithuania was politically and economically dependent on Poland, yet claimed by the Lithuanian Republic. The election’s principal aim was to produce a legislature that would legitimize incorporation into Poland, a move fiercely contested by Lithuania and unrecognized by the League of Nations.

\==Historical and Political Background==
After World War I, the borderlands between Poland and Lithuania became the site of competing territorial claims. In October 1920, Żeligowski—acting with covert Polish approval—weakened the Lithuanian hold on Vilnius by proclaiming the Republic of Central Lithuania. The entity’s provisional government relied on Polish military support and sought to settle its status via the Vilnius Sejm. Negotiations under League of Nations mediation had faltered by late 1921, as neither Poland nor Lithuania would accept a plebiscite outcome that might undermine their respective claims.

\==Electoral Framework and Preparations==
The electoral law, promulgated in October 1921, established a single-member constituency system across territory deliberately drawn to include predominantly Polish areas while excluding Lithuanian-majority districts. Official figures recorded 735,089 inhabitants in the designated constituencies: 11.5 percent Jews, 8.8 percent Belarusians, and 7.2 percent Lithuanians. Candidates were required to be at least 25 years old, possess basic education, and demonstrate proficiency in Polish. Although the law nominally guaranteed freedom of assembly and press, it imposed penalties—up to one year in prison—for campaigning “against the election,” effectively suppressing anti-incorporation voices.

\==Campaign and Boycott==
All participating political organizations were Polish-oriented. Right-wing groups, notably the Association of National Parties and Organizations, advocated unconditional union with Poland. Left-wing factions, such as the Popular Councils, supported incorporation within an autonomous framework. Lithuanian authorities, protesting the election’s legality, called for a boycott and sought to revive the plebiscite option, but the League of Nations had ceased mediation by this stage.

\==Voter Turnout and Results==
Official reports indicated overall turnout of 63.9 percent. However, participation varied sharply by ethnicity: 41 percent among Belarusians, 15.3 percent among Jews, and only 8.2 percent among Lithuanians. Jewish turnout in urban centers was particularly low (6.3 percent), and in the city of Vilnius itself it fell to 1.4 percent. The election returned 105 deputies: 43 seats went to the right-wing Association of National Parties and Organizations, 34 to the left-wing Popular Councils, and the remaining 28 to smaller Polish parties and groups.

\==League of Nations Reaction==
The Military Inter-Allied Commission of Control declared the elections invalid, citing widespread boycott by non-Polish minorities, reported use of military force, and procedural irregularities such as inadequate voter identification. On 13 January 1922, the League formally repudiated the Vilnius Sejm as unrepresentative of the region’s population.

\==Parliamentary Session and Incorporation Vote==
The newly elected Sejm convened on 1 February 1922. On 20 February, it unanimously voted to request formal annexation by Poland. The Polish Sejm approved this petition on 22 March, and on 24 March the territory of Central Lithuania was reorganized as the Wilno Land within the Second Polish Republic. These changes were recognized by the Conference of Ambassadors and the League of Nations, although Lithuania continued to regard Vilnius as its de jure capital and maintained non-recognition of Polish sovereignty over the area.

\==Aftermath and Legacy==
The absorption of Central Lithuania into Poland exacerbated Polish-Lithuanian hostilities, leaving relations frozen until the Polish ultimatum of 1938. Vilnius remained under Polish administration until World War II, when shifting frontlines again altered its status. Historians view the 1922 election as a classic example of interwar border politics—where electoral processes were employed to legitimize territorial gains—and as an illustration of the limits of international mediation when major powers’ interests diverged. The episode underscored the fragility of nascent states in contested borderlands and remains a key case in studies of plebiscitary democracy and minority rights in the interwar period.
