**Foča Ethnic Cleansing**

The **Foča ethnic cleansing** refers to a series of systematic attacks, forced expulsions, and atrocities committed against the Bosniak (Bosnian Muslim) population in the municipality of Foča and its surroundings during the Bosnian War (1992–1995). Perpetrated primarily by Bosnian Serb forces between April and December 1992, these operations aimed to create an ethnically homogeneous Serb territory in eastern Bosnia and Herzegovina. The campaign included widespread killings, sexual violence, destruction of religious and cultural sites, and the forcible transfer of civilian populations.

### Historical and Political Context

Foča, a town on the Drina River in southeastern Bosnia, was part of the Socialist Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina within Yugoslavia. Following Bosnia and Herzegovina’s March 1992 independence referendum, ethnic tensions—exacerbated by nationalist ambitions among Serb, Croat, and Bosniak leadership—escalated into armed conflict. The leadership of the self-proclaimed Republic of Srpska, backed by the Yugoslav People’s Army (JNA) and local Serb paramilitaries, pursued a strategy of territorial “cleansing” to secure contiguous Serb-dominated areas. Foča’s strategic location on the Drina corridor made it a priority in this plan.

### Timeline of Atrocities

* **April–May 1992**: Serb forces, including elements of the JNA, local police, and paramilitary units (notably the “Osmaci” and “Cobra” groups), entered Foča. They immediately separated Bosniak men of fighting age from women, children, and the elderly. Mass executions were carried out at sites such as Lake Vrbica and the Partizan sports hall.
* **June–July 1992**: Systematic abuses intensified. Bosniak men and boys were detained in makeshift camps (e.g., the Partizan Hall and former military barracks) where they endured torture, starvation, and summary killings. The town’s 14 mosques—including the 16th-century Aladža Mosque—were dynamited or set ablaze.
* **Summer–Autumn 1992**: Widespread sexual violence was used as a tool of terror and ethnic cleansing. Numerous Bosniak women and girls were detained in “rape camps”–notably the White House and Čelebići facilities—for repeated assaults. Families were driven from their homes and herded along evacuation routes toward Muslim-controlled areas.
* **December 1992**: Most of Foča’s remaining Bosniak population—estimated at 20,000 before the war—had been expelled or killed. The town was renamed “Srbinje” by Bosnian Serb authorities, who repopulated it with Serb refugees from other parts of Bosnia.

### Victims and Human Cost

Estimates place the number of Foča region fatalities between 2,000 and 5,000, the majority civilian. Over 1,000 cases of rape and sexual enslavement have been documented, making Foča one of the most notorious sites of wartime sexual violence in Bosnia. Entire villages were depopulated and razed, and thousands of displaced Bosniak refugees settled in makeshift camps in Muslim-held enclaves such as Goražde and Sarajevo.

### Legal Accountability

The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) indicted several Foča perpetrators for crimes against humanity, violations of the laws or customs of war, and genocide:

* **Dragoljub Kunarac, Radomir Kovač, and Milan Lukić**: Prosecuted in the Foča case for rape, enslavement, torture, and murder. Milan Lukić received a life sentence for particularly egregious crimes, including lynchings and firebombing of civilian shelters.
* **Momčilo Krajišnik** and **Radovan Karadžić**: Senior Bosnian Serb political leaders found guilty of participating in a joint criminal enterprise that included Foča’s ethnic cleansing.
* **Radislav Krstić**: Convicted for aiding and abetting genocide at Srebrenica, but his broader role in orchestrating Serb military campaigns in eastern Bosnia encompassed the Foča operations.

Numerous lower-level local officials and camp guards were also tried and convicted in Bosnian state courts for their roles in the Foča atrocities.

### Post-War Reconstruction and Returns

Following the Dayton Peace Agreement (1995), Foča returned to the jurisdiction of the Republika Srpska entity. Efforts to encourage the return of expelled Bosniaks have met with limited success; as of the early 2020s, only a few hundred returnees have resettled. The town’s former name was reinstated, and several mosques have been reconstructed—most notably the Aladža Mosque, reopened in 2019 after a decade-long rebuilding effort supported by international donors.

### Memorialization and Reconciliation

Sites of mass graves and former detention centers around Foča have become places of memory. A memorial complex at Lake Vrbica honors civilian victims, while annual commemorations draw survivors and international representatives. Local and international NGOs promote dialogue and reconciliation through community projects, though political divisions and denialism persist in some quarters of Republika Srpska.

### Legacy

The Foča ethnic cleansing exemplifies the use of systematic violence in pursuit of ethnically defined territorial objectives. Its legal aftermath contributed significantly to the development of international criminal jurisprudence on rape as a war crime and crimes against humanity. The Foča prosecutions underscored the principle of command responsibility and reinforced the international community’s evolving commitment to accountability for mass atrocity crimes.
