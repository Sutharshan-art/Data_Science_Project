**Croats of Bosnia and Herzegovina**

The Croats of Bosnia and Herzegovina (Croatian: Hrvati Bosne i Hercegovine) are one of the three constitutive peoples of Bosnia and Herzegovina, alongside Bosniaks and Serbs. Concentrated primarily in the southwestern and central regions of the country, they have played a key role in its historical development and contemporary political structure.

## Historical Background

The presence of Croats in the territory of modern Bosnia and Herzegovina dates to the early Middle Ages. Following the migration of Slavic tribes into the Western Balkans in the 6th and 7th centuries, a Slavic polity emerged that later split into several principalities. By the 10th century, the western and northern parts of Bosnia were integrated into the medieval Croatian kingdom under King Tomislav.

Over subsequent centuries, Ottoman conquest (mid-15th century onward) transformed the region’s demographic and religious landscape. Many local Croats retained their Roman Catholic faith and traditions under Ottoman rule, forming a distinct religious community alongside Orthodox Christians and Muslims. With the Austro-Hungarian occupation in 1878, Catholic Croats benefited from improved political rights and educational opportunities, leading to the rise of cultural and political organizations that asserted their national identity.

## Demographics and Distribution

According to the 2013 census, Croats comprise approximately 15.4 percent of Bosnia and Herzegovina’s population, numbering around 544,780 individuals. They are predominantly Roman Catholic and are heavily represented in three geographic areas:

* **Herzegovina**: Particularly in West Herzegovina and southwest Herzegovina, where Croats form an overwhelming majority. Key cities include Mostar, Široki Brijeg, and Livno.
* **Central Bosnia**: Municipalities such as Nova Bila and Vitez feature significant mixed communities, with Croats often concentrated in urban centers.
* **Posavina and Northern Bosnia**: Smaller Croat enclaves exist along the Sava River corridor, including the Brčko District.

Urban centers with substantial Croat populations include Mostar (where they are one of two major communities), Tuzla, and Sarajevo suburbs.

## Political Status and Constitutional Framework

Under the Dayton Peace Agreement (1995), Bosnia and Herzegovina was divided into two entities—the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (FBiH) and Republika Srpska—plus the Brčko District under joint authority. Croats are recognized as a constitutive people in both entities and at state level, guaranteeing their participation in government.

Key political provisions include:

* **Tripartite Presidency**: The collective head of state comprises one Croat, one Bosniak, and one Serb member, elected directly by voters in a two-entity model.
* **House of Peoples**: The upper chamber of the Parliamentary Assembly reserves equal representation for Croat, Bosniak, and Serb delegates, ensuring that legislation cannot pass without the consent of each constituent people.
* **Federation Cantons**: In the FBiH, ten cantons exercise significant autonomy. Four cantons have Croat majorities or substantial Croat populations, allowing local self-government and cultural preservation.

## Political Representation and Parties

Croat political interests are primarily represented by the Croatian Democratic Union of Bosnia and Herzegovina (HDZ BiH) and its splinter parties. HDZ BiH, modeled on Croatia’s HDZ, advocates for the protection of Croat rights, equitable political representation, and the decentralization of state powers. Other parties include:

* **Croatian Democratic Union 1990 (HDZ 1990)**: Formed in 2006 by dissidents seeking more moderate positions on national issues.
* **Croatian Party of Rights of Bosnia and Herzegovina (HSP BiH)**: A smaller right-wing party focused on nationalist goals and close ties with Croatia.

In recent years, Croat political leaders have repeatedly called for constitutional reforms to address “vital national interest” mechanisms, which they argue are misapplied and lead to Bosniak-dominated decisions in FBiH institutions.

## Cultural and Religious Life

The Roman Catholic Church plays a central role in the cultural life of Croats in Bosnia and Herzegovina. The Archdiocese of Vrhbosna (centered in Sarajevo) and the Diocese of Mostar-Duvno oversee parishes, education, and charitable activities. Catholic holidays, traditional folk customs (such as *klapa* singing), and Catholic schools contribute to communal cohesion.

Croat media outlets—including television, newspapers, and online portals—provide news and cultural programming in Croatian. Cultural associations promote folklore, dance, and literature, often collaborating with counterparts in neighboring Croatia.

## Contemporary Issues

Key challenges facing Croats in Bosnia and Herzegovina include:

* **Electoral Reform**: Ongoing debates over constituency boundaries and voting mechanisms to ensure genuine representation.
* **Economic Development**: Many Croat-majority areas face high unemployment and emigration, particularly among young people.
* **Interethnic Relations**: While cooperation with Bosniaks has stabilized since the 1994 federation agreement, occasional disputes arise over language rights, education curricula, and administrative competencies.

Efforts by international organizations and domestic NGOs aim to foster interethnic dialogue, youth engagement, and balanced regional development.

## Legacy and Outlook

As one of Bosnia and Herzegovina’s three constitutive peoples, Croats have shaped the country’s medieval heritage, religious mosaic, and modern political system. Their future role depends on sustained efforts toward political reform, economic revitalization, and intercommunal cooperation—ensuring that the multiethnic state remains inclusive and stable for all its citizens.
