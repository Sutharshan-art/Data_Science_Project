**George Herbert Walker Jr.** (January 24, 1905 – October 17, 1977) was an American banker, sports executive, and civic leader whose career intertwined with mid-20th-century developments in finance, professional sports, and Republican Party politics. A scion of the prominent Walker family, he was the son of banker George Herbert Walker and the uncle of President George H. W. Bush. Walker Jr. helped shape modern asset management, played a foundational role in bringing Major League Baseball to New York outside Manhattan, and supported national political campaigns during a period of postwar Republican realignment.

**Early Life and Education**
Born in St. Louis, Missouri, Walker was the eldest child of George Herbert Walker Sr., a founder of the G. H. Walker & Co. brokerage, and Lucretia Wear. He attended St. Louis Country Day School and entered Yale University in 1923, graduating with a B.A. in economics in 1927. At Yale he joined the Delta Kappa Epsilon fraternity and took part in intramural sports, reflecting a family tradition of valuing both athletics and professional networking. After Yale, he spent a year at the Wharton School’s executive-training program before entering the family’s financial firm in New York City.

**Banking and Financial Career**
In 1928 Walker joined G. H. Walker & Co., where he specialized in corporate bond underwriting. During the Great Depression, he helped guide the firm through New Deal regulatory changes and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. Rising to partner in 1936 and senior partner by 1945, he led the company’s expansion into municipal finance and consumer credit. Under his direction, G. H. Walker & Co. became one of Wall Street’s leading regional brokerages, advising Midwestern utilities on postwar reconstruction loans.

In 1952, Walker orchestrated the firm’s merger with the securities division of the W. A. Harriman & Co., creating a diversified investment bank that weathered the 1957–58 recession without major layoffs. He served on the New York Stock Exchange’s regional advisory board and was an early advocate for computerized trading systems, sponsoring a pilot program in 1961 that presaged NASDAQ’s electronic order entry.

**Role in Major League Baseball**
Walker’s longstanding passion for baseball led him to become a key investor in the effort to relocate and expand professional teams. In 1962, after the Brooklyn Dodgers and New York Giants departed for the West Coast, Walker joined a consortium led by Joan Whitney Payson to secure an expansion franchise for New York. As a co-founder and treasurer of the New York Mets, he underwrote early operating losses and helped negotiate construction financing for Shea Stadium. Walker served on the Mets’ board of directors from their inaugural season in 1962 through 1967, fostering community outreach programs in Queens and championing youth baseball clinics that strengthened the franchise’s local roots.

**Civic Leadership and Philanthropy**
Beyond finance and sports, Walker held leadership roles in numerous civic organizations. He was a trustee of the Manhattan Institute for Urban Renewal and chaired the board of the New York Chamber of Commerce from 1965 to 1968. His advocacy for urban revitalization in midtown Manhattan influenced zoning reforms and public-private partnerships that funded affordable-housing projects. Walker and his wife, Mary Carter Walker, endowed scholarships at Yale and the Horace Mann School, emphasizing opportunities for first-generation college students.

**Political Activity and Republican Party Involvement**
A committed Republican, Walker was active in party politics during a time of ideological realignment. He served as finance chair for New York’s gubernatorial campaigns in 1958 and 1962, helping to mobilize suburban donors around issues of tax restraint and anti-communism. Nationally, he was a major fundraiser for Dwight D. Eisenhower’s presidential bids in 1952 and 1956 and later supported Richard Nixon’s 1968 and 1972 campaigns. His efforts reflected the growing influence of Wall Street financiers in shaping Republican economic policy, particularly tax reform and financial deregulation.

Despite his close ties to conservative leaders, Walker maintained relationships across the aisle, hosting bipartisan policy forums at his Manhattan residence. He believed that sound fiscal management required dialogue with moderates and Democrats, a stance that mirrored the Rockefeller Republican wing dominant in New York during the 1960s.

**Later Years and Legacy**
In the early 1970s, as mergers and internationalization transformed U.S. banking, Walker advocated for careful deregulation to foster global competitiveness while safeguarding domestic investors. He retired from active management of G. H. Walker & Co. in 1974 but remained honorary chairman until his death. Walker died at age 72 in Greenwich, Connecticut, after a brief illness.

George Herbert Walker Jr.’s multifaceted career—spanning brokerage innovation, the birth of an expansion baseball franchise, civic urban development, and high-level political fundraising—embodied the intertwining of business acumen and public engagement characteristic of mid-20th-century American elites. His contributions to professional sports finance, urban policy, and the Republican Party left enduring marks on New York City’s institutions and on the broader contours of postwar American political economy.
