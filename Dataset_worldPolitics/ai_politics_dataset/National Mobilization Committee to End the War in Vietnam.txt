**National Mobilization Committee to End the War in Vietnam** (often referred to simply as “the Mobilization” or “MOBE”) was an umbrella coalition of American antiwar organizations formed in 1967 to coordinate large-scale protests against U.S. involvement in the Vietnam War. Through mass demonstrations, political lobbying, and public education campaigns, the Mobilization sought to shift U.S. policy toward a negotiated peace settlement and to galvanize domestic opposition to what participants characterized as an unjust and unwinnable conflict.

## Origins and Formation

In early 1967, rising public discontent over escalating U.S. military casualties and the expanding draft fused with growing skepticism among intellectuals, students, and religious leaders. Key figures from disparate groups—including Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), the National Student Association, and various church-based peace fellowships—convened to plan a coordinated protest. In June 1967, representatives met in New York City and formally established the National Mobilization Committee to End the War in Vietnam, setting its inaugural demonstration date for October 1967.

## Leadership and Organizational Structure

The Mobilization was governed by a steering committee composed of representatives from major participating organizations. Notable leaders included David Dellinger of the Fellowship of Reconciliation, Tom Hayden of SDS, A.J. Muste of the War Resisters League, and Cleveland Robinson of the Retail, Wholesale and Department Store Union. Decision-making combined consensus-building among these leaders with working groups tasked with logistics, publicity, legal support, and outreach to local chapters in more than thirty cities. Despite ideological differences—ranging from nonviolent pacifism to revolutionary socialism—the coalition maintained unity around a shared demand: immediate negotiations to end U.S. military involvement.

## Major Actions and Demonstrations

The Mobilization’s first and most famous action was the October 21–22, 1967, march on the Pentagon. Officially titled “March on the Pentagon to End the War in Vietnam,” the event drew upwards of 100,000 participants in Washington, D.C. Protesters staged a symbolic attempt to “levitate” the Pentagon through chanting and meditation—a tactic reflecting the influence of religious pacifism. Violent clashes between some demonstrators and military police occurred, underscoring tensions within the protest movement over tactics and raising questions about the possibility of peaceful mass action.

Following the Pentagon protest, the Mobilization organized periodic “Days of Resistance” in early 1968, coinciding with the New Hampshire primary in February and the March on Washington in April. After the Tet Offensive in January 1968 intensified public disillusionment with official war narratives, Mobilization leaders shifted focus to a national “Moratorium to End the War in Vietnam.” On October 15, 1969, an estimated two million Americans participated in local and national events, including teach-ins, rallies, and self-imposed work stoppages. The massive turnout demonstrated the depth of antiwar sentiment and placed sustained pressure on elected officials.

## Political Context and Impact

The Mobilization emerged amid a broader political realignment in the United States. President Lyndon B. Johnson’s Great Society ambitions were being eroded by war expenditures, and Democratic Party unity fractured between hawks and doves. The Mobilization’s high-profile actions influenced key political developments: they contributed to growing media scrutiny of battlefield realities, bolstered congressional calls for hearings on conduct and cost, and emboldened antiwar candidates such as Senator Eugene McCarthy and, later, Senator George McGovern. Although Johnson announced in March 1968 that he would not seek re-election, the war continued until 1975; nonetheless, the Mobilization’s ability to mobilize millions signaled a new era in U.S. grassroots activism.

## Internal Debates and Criticisms

The coalition faced recurring debates over strategy and ideology. Some participants argued for strictly nonviolent direct action, while others favored more confrontational tactics, including civil disobedience and draft resistance. Tensions also arose between student radicals and labor or church groups over questions of class, race, and revolutionary praxis. Critics outside the movement—from hawkish politicians to some veterans’ organizations—dismissed Mobilization protests as unpatriotic or as distractions that undermined troop morale. Nevertheless, the coalition’s leaders defended their approach as a necessary exercise of democratic dissent.

## Legacy and Decline

By 1972, U.S. troop withdrawals and shifting public opinion reduced the Mobilization’s centrality. Many member organizations transitioned to other social justice causes, such as opposition to apartheid in South Africa or advocacy for women’s and civil rights. The Mobilization formally disbanded in the mid-1970s, but its model of broad-based, issue-focused coalition-building endured. The Mobilization can be seen as a precursor to later mass movements—against nuclear weapons, for environmental protection, and for LGBTQ rights—that similarly combined local chapters, national oversight, and periodic large-scale demonstrations.

## Significance in American Political History

The National Mobilization Committee to End the War in Vietnam occupies a pivotal place in the history of American protest movements. It demonstrated the power of coordinated, nationwide direct action to influence public discourse and electoral politics. The Mobilization also paved the way for the professionalization of protest logistics—mass mailings, media relations, and legal support—that have become standard in subsequent movements. In the broader context of the 1960s, the Mobilization exemplified a confluence of youth activism, religious pacifism, and labor solidarity, marking a transformative moment in the evolution of civic engagement and the boundaries of dissent in a democratic society.
