**Serbia and Montenegro**

**Overview**
Serbia and Montenegro was a federal state in Southeast Europe that existed from February 2003 to June 2006. It was the successor to the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (FRY) and comprised the two remaining republics of the former Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia: the Republic of Serbia and the Republic of Montenegro. The union adopted a loose constitutional arrangement intended to balance the republics’ divergent political aspirations. The state dissolved peacefully in June 2006 following a Montenegrin independence referendum, leading to the establishment of two independent countries: the Republic of Serbia and the Republic of Montenegro.

**Historical Context**
After the breakup of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia in the early 1990s, Serbia and Montenegro remained united as the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, proclaimed on 27 April 1992. Under President Slobodan Milošević, the FRY was heavily involved in the Yugoslav Wars in Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Kosovo. International isolation and economic sanctions throughout the 1990s hampered development and fueled political tensions between Serbia and the more union-sceptical Montenegro. The overthrow of Milošević in October 2000 and subsequent democratic reforms opened the path to redefining the union’s structure.

**Constitutional Reform and Creation**
In March 2002, representatives of Serbia and Montenegro signed the Belgrade Agreement, brokered by the European Union, to transform the FRY into a more decentralized state. The agreement envisioned a confederation with limited shared institutions and the possibility for each republic to pursue independence after three years. On 4 February 2003, the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia was formally renamed “Serbia and Montenegro,” and a new Constitutional Charter came into force on 14 March 2003. This charter established a minimal central government, vesting most powers in the two republics while retaining responsibility at the union level for defense, foreign affairs, monetary policy, and border controls.

**Political Structure**
Serbia and Montenegro’s federal institutions included:

* **President of Serbia and Montenegro**: A ceremonial head of state elected by the parliament for a four-year term. Svetozar Marović of Montenegro served as the only president under the new name.
* **Council of Ministers**: Headed by a Prime Minister (officially the Chairman of the Council of Ministers), responsible for union-level executive functions. Dragan Đilas of Serbia was the first and only holder of this office.
* **Parliament**: A unicameral legislature comprised of 126 deputies—91 from Serbia and 35 from Montenegro—elected by proportional representation in each republic. The parliament possessed limited legislative authority, primarily approving budgets, ratifying treaties, and overseeing federal ministries.

**Republic Relations and Autonomy**
Under the Constitutional Charter, Serbia and Montenegro retained their own constitutions, presidents, parliaments, and governments. Each republic managed internal affairs, including education, healthcare, and policing. The union’s decentralized design aimed to accommodate Montenegro’s growing push for sovereignty, while preserving a single international legal personality for both republics during the transitional period.

**Foreign Policy and International Status**
Serbia and Montenegro inherited the FRY’s seat at the United Nations, NATO’s Partnership for Peace program, and membership in international financial institutions. The union pursued Euro-Atlantic integration and cooperation with the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia. However, Montenegro’s leadership—under President Milo Đukanović—progressively distanced itself from Serbia’s foreign policy, establishing separate economic and consular representations abroad and unilaterally introducing the euro alongside the Yugoslav dinar in the late 1990s.

**Economic and Social Dynamics**
The union faced significant economic challenges: post-war reconstruction, transition from a state-controlled economy, and the legacies of sanctions. Serbia, the larger republic, bore most of the federal debt and industrial capacity, while Montenegro—smaller and tourism-oriented—benefited from freer trade and investment policies. Economic disparities and differing strategic priorities heightened tensions, with Montenegrin calls for independence growing amid perceptions of unequal burden-sharing.

**Independence Referendum and Dissolution**
Article 60 of the Constitutional Charter permitted either republic to hold an independence referendum after three years. Montenegro organized a referendum on 21 May 2006 under EU supervision, with a 55-percent super-majority threshold required for secession. The vote yielded 55.5 percent in favor of independence on a 86-percent turnout. The Federal Assembly convened on 3 June 2006, and on 5 June adopted a declaration of dissolution, informing the UN Secretary-General that Serbia and Montenegro had ceased to exist. On 7 June 2006, Serbia was proclaimed the legal successor state.

**Aftermath**
Following dissolution:

* **Serbia** continued as the successor state to Serbia and Montenegro’s international obligations and membership in the UN and other bodies. It temporarily maintained the official name “Serbia and Montenegro” until adopting “Serbia” in August 2006.
* **Montenegro** gained full sovereignty, joining the UN as an independent member on 28 June 2006 and pursuing European Union and NATO integration as a separate polity.

Both republics retained the euro as legal tender—Montenegro unilaterally, Serbia alongside the dinar—reflecting economic divergences rooted in the union period.

**Legacy**
Serbia and Montenegro’s brief existence represents a transitional phase between the disintegration of socialist Yugoslavia and the emergence of fully independent successor states. Its highly decentralized structure and negotiated dissolution under international auspices are often cited as a model for peaceful state breakup, contrasting with the violent conflicts of the 1990s. The union’s experience continues to influence regional politics, integration debates, and scholarship on federalism and secession.
