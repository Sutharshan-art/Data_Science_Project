**Iraqi Invasion of Kuwait**

The Iraqi invasion of Kuwait was a brief military campaign conducted by the armed forces of Iraq under President Saddam Hussein, beginning on 2 August 1990. It resulted in the annexation of Kuwait as Iraq’s “19th province” and precipitated the Gulf War, a U.S.-led coalition intervention to reverse Iraqi occupation. The invasion was driven by economic disputes, territorial claims, and regional power dynamics in the aftermath of the Iran–Iraq War.

### Historical and Economic Background

In the 1980s, Iraq emerged from its long and costly war with Iran heavily indebted to Gulf Arab states, including Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, which had provided financial support. Baghdad sought relief through debt forgiveness and reparations. Meanwhile, Kuwait—along with the United Arab Emirates—was accused by Iraq of exceeding its OPEC-mandated oil production quotas, driving down global oil prices and thereby reducing Iraq’s export revenues needed for reconstruction. Iraq also accused Kuwait of engaging in “slant drilling” into the Rumaila oil field, shared across the Iraq–Kuwait border, and sought compensation.

Beyond economic grievances, Saddam Hussein revived historical claims: he argued that Kuwait had been a district of Ottoman-ruled Basra until British imperial carving in the early 20th century. These assertions combined with domestic pressures on Saddam’s regime—facing rising military and civilian discontent—to create a pretext for military action.

### Planning and Decision to Invade

In early July 1990, President Saddam Hussein convened his military and Baʿth Party leadership to discuss potential actions against Kuwait. Iraqi forces had already conducted military exercises near the border. Diplomatic efforts by Arab and Western mediators failed to secure satisfactory concessions from Kuwait. On 25 July, Iraq expelled an emissary from the Arab League who had attempted to broker a settlement. Within a week, Iraq finalized invasion plans.

### The Invasion (2–4 August 1990)

In the pre-dawn hours of 2 August 1990, around 100,000 Iraqi troops—organized into mechanized corps—crossed the border at multiple points. The Iraqi Air Force secured Kuwaiti airspace before airborne and ground units advanced on Kuwait City and key oil installations. Kuwaiti defenses, limited to several commando battalions and border guard units, were quickly overwhelmed. Within three days, Iraqi forces had occupied the entire country, capturing the Emir, his family, and most of the government, which quickly fled to exile in Saudi Arabia.

On 8 August, Iraq issued a decree annexing Kuwait as its “19th governorate,” placing it under military and civilian Iraqi administration. Saddam declared the merger a historic reunification, ordering Kuwaiti institutions to be reorganized under Iraqi authority.

### International Reaction and United Nations Response

The invasion prompted immediate international condemnation. On 2 August, the United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution 660, demanding Iraq’s unconditional withdrawal. Despite Iraq’s claims of legitimate unification, Kuwait’s diplomatic mission remained recognized by most states, and international recognition of the annexation was virtually nonexistent.

Subsequent UN resolutions imposed progressively stringent measures:

* **Resolution 661** (6 August) instituted comprehensive economic and diplomatic sanctions on Iraq and occupied Kuwait.
* **Resolution 664** (18 August) demanded unfettered access to humanitarian relief in Kuwait.
* **Resolution 678** (29 November) authorized Member States to use “all necessary means” to uphold and implement previous resolutions if Iraq had not withdrawn by 15 January 1991.

Regionally, Saudi Arabia and other Gulf Cooperation Council members, alarmed by Iraq’s expanded reach, invited U.S. and coalition forces to deploy on their soil under Operation Desert Shield.

### Coalition Mobilization and Gulf War

From August 1990 to January 1991, over 500,000 coalition troops from more than 30 nations assembled in Saudi Arabia and the Persian Gulf region. Diplomatic efforts to avert war continued but failed. On 17 January 1991, Operation Desert Storm commenced with an extensive air campaign against Iraqi military and infrastructure targets. A ground offensive launched on 24 February swiftly dislodged Iraqi forces from Kuwait by 27 February. A formal ceasefire under UN auspices followed on 28 February; Kuwait’s sovereignty was restored.

### Aftermath and Consequences

The invasion and subsequent Gulf War had profound effects:

* **Kuwait** suffered extensive damage to its oil industry, including the deliberate ignition of over 600 oil wells by retreating Iraqi forces, and the displacement of much of its population.
* **Iraq** faced heavy military losses, decimation of its armored and air forces, and continued UN sanctions throughout the 1990s that exacerbated economic hardship and humanitarian crises.
* **Regional Security** was reshaped: the United States maintained a significant military presence in the Gulf, and Gulf Cooperation Council states pursued closer security cooperation.
* **International Law**: The crisis reinforced the principle of territorial integrity and the efficacy of UN collective security measures, though debates over post-war occupation policies and sanctions endured.

Politically, Saddam’s gamble in Kuwait weakened his regime’s legitimacy, setting the stage for growing internal dissent and eventually contributing to the circumstances leading to the 2003 U.S.-led invasion of Iraq. Kuwait’s experience informed its subsequent foreign policy orientation, emphasizing alliances with Western powers and active participation in international organizations to safeguard sovereignty.
