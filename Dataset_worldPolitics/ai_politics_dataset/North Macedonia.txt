**North Macedonia**, officially the **Republic of North Macedonia** (Macedonian: Република Северна Македонија, Republika Severna Makedonija; Albanian: Republika e Maqedonisë së Veriut), is a landlocked country in Southeast Europe on the Balkan Peninsula. It declared independence from the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia in 1991 and adopted its current name in 2019, resolving a longstanding dispute with Greece.

## Early History and Ottoman Rule

The territory of present-day North Macedonia has been inhabited since prehistoric times and was incorporated into the ancient kingdom of Paeonia before becoming part of Macedon under Philip II and Alexander the Great. Over the subsequent centuries, it fell successively under Roman and Byzantine rule, Slavic settlement in the 6th–7th centuries, and Ottoman administration from the late 14th century until the Balkan Wars (1912–1913).

Under Ottoman rule, the region was part of the vilayets of Salonica and Kosovo. It was characterized by a mix of Orthodox Slavic, Muslim Turk, and Albanian communities, whose coexistence—often peaceful, sometimes tense—shaped local identities. National awakenings in the 19th century fostered competing Bulgarian, Greek, and Serbian claims, laying the groundwork for 20th-century conflicts.

## In Yugoslavia (1945–1991)

After World War II, the People’s Republic of Macedonia was established as one of six constituent republics of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia under Josip Broz Tito. The Communist authorities promoted a distinct Macedonian language and national identity to counter Bulgarian and Serbian irredentism. Industrialization and urbanization accelerated, and Skopje became the republic’s capital.

Ethnic minority rights—particularly for Albanians concentrated in the north and west—were enshrined in the 1974 Yugoslav constitution, granting cultural autonomy and representation in republican bodies. Nonetheless, economic disparities and occasional unrest surfaced, especially in regions with mixed populations.

## Independence and the Name Dispute

On 8 September 1991, following a referendum, the Macedonian parliament declared independence, initially under the name “Republic of Macedonia.” Greece objected, arguing that the name implied territorial claims over its northern province of Macedonia and usurped Hellenic heritage. Greece imposed an economic embargo (1994–1995) and blocked the new state’s accession to the United Nations under its constitutional name.

A provisional reference—“the former Yugoslav Republic of Macedonia” (FYROM)—was adopted for UN entry on 8 April 1993. Negotiations under UN auspices continued for over two decades, affecting the country’s NATO and EU aspirations.

## Political System and Governance

North Macedonia is a parliamentary republic. The President, elected for a single five-year term, serves as head of state with largely ceremonial duties. Executive power resides in the Prime Minister and the Cabinet, who command a majority in the unicameral Assembly (Sobranie) of 120 members, elected by proportional representation for four-year terms.

Since independence, politics have been dominated by two major parties: the centre-right internal Macedonian Revolutionary Organization–Democratic Party for Macedonian National Unity (VMRO-DPMNE) and the centre-left Social Democratic Union of Macedonia (SDSM). Coalition governments frequently include ethnic Albanian parties—most notably the Democratic Union for Integration (DUI)—reflecting the country’s multiethnic makeup.

## Ohrid Framework Agreement and Ethnic Relations

In August 2001, an insurgency by the ethnic Albanian National Liberation Army (NLA) led to armed confrontations in several municipalities. The **Ohrid Framework Agreement**, brokered by the EU and the United States, ended hostilities by granting Albanian communities greater rights: official use of the Albanian language where they comprised at least 20% of residents, equitable public-sector employment, and decentralized local governance. The agreement remains the cornerstone of interethnic peace and political cooperation.

## Name Change and Euro-Atlantic Integration

In June 2018, Prime Minister Zoran Zaev’s government concluded the **Prespa Agreement** with Greek Prime Minister Alexis Tsipras, renaming the country **Republic of North Macedonia**. The agreement went into effect on 12 February 2019, after parliamentary ratifications and referendums in both states.

Following the name resolution, North Macedonia joined NATO on 27 March 2020, becoming the 30th member. EU accession negotiations formally opened on 19 July 2022, though they remain subject to reforms in rule of law, judiciary, and public administration.

## Economy and Development

North Macedonia transitioned from a state-controlled to a market-oriented economy in the 1990s. Key sectors include manufacturing (textiles, machinery), agriculture (tobacco, grapes), and services (tourism, information technology). GDP growth averaged around 3–4% annually before the COVID-19 pandemic, fueled by foreign investment and export diversification.

Challenges persist: high unemployment—especially among youth—regional disparities, and the need for infrastructure modernization. The government has pursued fiscal stabilization, anti-corruption measures, and incentives for small and medium-sized enterprises to bolster competitiveness and social inclusion.

## Foreign Relations

Balancing relations with EU and NATO partners, North Macedonia maintains diplomatic ties with neighboring Balkan states and seeks constructive engagement with Serbia, Bulgaria, and Greece. Bilateral disputes—such as Bulgarian objections over historical interpretations and language—continue to affect EU accession momentum. North Macedonia participates in regional initiatives like the Berlin Process and the Central European Free Trade Agreement (CEFTA).

## Contemporary Political Context

Domestic politics revolve around governance reforms, consolidation of the Ohrid Agreement, and managing nationalist currents. The COVID-19 pandemic and energy price shocks tested institutional resilience, while environmental protests—sparked by infrastructure projects in protected areas—highlight growing civic activism. The pursuit of EU membership remains the central unifying agenda, framing public discourse and policy priorities.

North Macedonia’s journey from a Yugoslav republic to a sovereign state navigating identity, integration, and reform exemplifies the complex political transformation of Southeast Europe in the post–Cold War era. Its experience underscores the interplay of national sovereignty, ethnic pluralism, and supranational cooperation in the modern European project.
