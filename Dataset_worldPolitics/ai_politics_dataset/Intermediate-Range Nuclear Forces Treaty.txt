**Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty**

The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty was a landmark bilateral arms control agreement signed on December 8, 1987, by U.S. President Ronald Reagan and Soviet General Secretary Mikhail Gorbachev. It required the elimination of all land-based ballistic and cruise missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometres, along with their launchers, and established extensive verification measures. The treaty marked the first time the superpowers agreed to eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons, significantly reducing Cold War tensions in Europe and reshaping the strategic balance.

### Historical and Political Background

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, both the United States and the Soviet Union deployed intermediate-range nuclear forces (INF) in Europe. The Soviet Union’s SS-20 Saber missiles, capable of striking targets across Western Europe, prompted NATO’s decision in 1979 to deploy Pershing II ballistic and ground-launched cruise missiles in Western Europe as a counterbalance. Public opposition to missile deployment—fueled by fears of nuclear war and peace movements across Europe—created political pressure on NATO governments. Simultaneously, reformist currents in the Soviet leadership under Gorbachev opened a window for renewed arms control diplomacy.

### Negotiation Process

INF negotiations began in Geneva in November 1981 under the framework of the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT). Progress was slow due to disagreements over data exchange, verification methods, and the scope of covered systems. A breakthrough occurred during a series of high-level summits in the mid-1980s, particularly at the Reykjavík Summit in October 1986, where the two leaders sketched the outlines of a treaty eliminating intermediate-range missiles. Detailed negotiations continued, culminating in a comprehensive treaty text agreed in Washington, D.C., and signed in December 1987.

### Treaty Provisions

The INF Treaty required both parties to:

* **Eliminate** all ground-launched ballistic and cruise missiles with ranges of 500–5,500 km and their launchers, amounting to over 2,600 missiles.
* **Destroy** these systems and associated infrastructure within three years of entry into force.
* **Establish verification** through on-site inspections, data exchanges, and notifications of missile production, deployment, and elimination activities.
* **Maintain transparency** via annual exhibitions of destroyed missile components and continuous monitoring at designated facilities.

The treaty did not address sea- or air-launched missiles, intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), or strategic bombers, focusing exclusively on land-based systems that posed a direct threat to Europe.

### Implementation and Verification

The INF Treaty entered into force on June 1, 1988. Over the next three years, the United States destroyed 846 missiles and 296 launchers, while the Soviet Union eliminated 1,846 missiles and 654 launchers. Verification activities included more than 2,000 on-site inspections at missile production, storage, and elimination sites. The unprecedented transparency fostered confidence between the former adversaries and set new standards for arms control verification.

### Political and Strategic Impact

By eliminating an entire class of nuclear weapons, the INF Treaty reduced the immediate nuclear threat to European populations and helped defuse public protests against NATO deployments. It also bolstered Gorbachev’s policy of **glasnost** (openness) and **perestroika** (restructuring) by demonstrating Soviet willingness to engage cooperatively with the West. The treaty paved the way for subsequent arms reduction agreements, including the Strategic Arms Reduction Treaties (START I and II), and contributed to the end of the Cold War.

### Compliance Disputes and Treaty Demise

In the 2000s, the United States alleged that Russia had developed and deployed a ground-launched cruise missile (designated SSC-8 by NATO) in violation of the treaty’s range limits. Russia denied the accusations and countered that U.S. missile defense systems in Europe threatened its strategic deterrent, arguing that the treaty had become outdated. After years of consultations failed to resolve the dispute, the U.S. announced its intention to withdraw in October 2018. On August 2, 2019, both the United States and Russia formally withdrew from the INF Treaty, ending its 31-year tenure.

### Legacy

The INF Treaty remains notable for being the first Cold War arms control agreement to eliminate an entire category of nuclear weapons. Its verification regime established precedents for subsequent treaties and confidence-building measures. The treaty’s demise has raised concerns about a renewed arms race in Europe and the erosion of the post–Cold War arms control framework. Nonetheless, the INF Treaty’s record of transparent elimination and inspection is widely regarded as a high point in superpower diplomacy and a testament to the potential of negotiated restraint in reducing nuclear dangers.
