**Laura Bush** (born Laura Lane Welch; November 4, 1946) is an American educator, librarian, and author who served as First Lady of the United States from 2001 to 2009. As the wife of President George W. Bush, she played a prominent role in shaping domestic policy initiatives on education, literacy, and women’s health, while also serving as a steadying public figure during the tumultuous events of the early 21st century—including the September 11 attacks and the ensuing “War on Terror.”

**Early Life and Education**
Born in Midland, Texas, Laura Welch graduated from Cypress-Fairbanks High School in Houston and earned a Bachelor of Science in education from Southern Methodist University in 1968. After marrying George W. Bush in 1977, she completed a Master of Library Science at the University of Texas at Austin in 1979 and worked as a school librarian and reading specialist in Houston and Austin-area public schools. Her professional background in education presaged the policy focus she would bring to the White House.

**Path to the White House**
During George W. Bush’s 1994 campaign for Governor of Texas, Laura Bush mounted a low-profile media tour, emphasizing her experience as a teacher and her interest in promoting literacy. Following Bush’s landslide victory, she championed statewide reading initiatives and helped expand after-school programs. Her tenure as First Lady of Texas (1995–2000) laid the groundwork for national prominence: she served on the board of the Texas Book Festival and advocated for increased funding for public libraries and early childhood education.

**First Lady of the United States (2001–2009)**
Upon her husband’s inauguration on January 20, 2001, Laura Bush established herself as a policy-driven First Lady with a focus on education, health, and cultural diplomacy:

* **Education and Literacy:** She launched the National Book Festival in 2001 in collaboration with the Library of Congress, drawing thousands of authors and attendees to Washington, D.C. In 2005, she founded the “Ready to Read, Ready to Learn” initiative, promoting book-driven early literacy programs and family reading time in underserved communities.
* **Women’s Health and Rights:** Laura Bush took an active interest in global women’s issues, traveling to Afghanistan and Africa to advocate for refugee children, literacy for girls, and HIV/AIDS awareness. In 2007 she helped secure U.S. backing for the President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR), underscoring the administration’s commitment to combating the epidemic in sub-Saharan Africa.
* **Cultural Diplomacy:** Partnering with the U.S. Information Agency and international counterparts, she led cultural-exchange programs emphasizing women’s empowerment through education and the arts. A fluent Spanish speaker, she addressed Latin American audiences on maternal and child health, strengthening bilateral outreach.

**Historical and Political Context**
Laura Bush’s tenure coincided with defining events of the early 21st century. In the immediate aftermath of the September 11, 2001, terrorist attacks, she helped shape the national mood of resilience, participating in relief efforts and comfort visits to Ground Zero in New York City. As the administration pursued military campaigns in Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2003), she represented a compassionate counterpoint—highlighting the human costs of conflict through her focus on social welfare, education reconstruction, and veterans’ family support.

Domestically, while her policy portfolio avoided debates over No Child Left Behind (2001) or broader education-funding battles, her advocacy for literacy and libraries dovetailed with the administration’s goals of boosting school accountability and student achievement. Although she maintained a nonpartisan public stance, Laura Bush’s initiatives enjoyed bipartisan support among educators and health-care professionals.

**Public Image and Criticism**
Often described as “America’s librarian,” Laura Bush cultivated an image of warmth, steadiness, and professionalism. Polls regularly ranked her among the most popular First Ladies. Critics, however, viewed her role as largely ceremonial and questioned whether her initiatives produced measurable outcomes; some women’s-rights activists lamented her reluctance to engage publicly on controversial social issues such as reproductive health policy.

**Post–White House Activities**
Since leaving the White House in January 2009, Laura Bush has continued her advocacy through the Laura Bush Foundation for America’s Libraries, which awards grants to public school libraries. She co-founded the George W. Bush Presidential Center at Southern Methodist University, which houses the presidential library, a policy institute, and a forum for global education and public-service programs. In her memoir, *Spoken from the Heart* (2010), and in occasional public speeches, she has reflected on her experiences and the changing role of the First Lady.

**Legacy**
Laura Bush’s legacy rests on her emphasis on literacy, education, and women’s health within both domestic and international arenas. By leveraging the soft power of the First Lady’s office, she reinforced U.S. engagement in global humanitarian issues and left enduring institutions—such as the National Book Festival and the Presidential Center—that continue to promote public-policy dialogue and cultural outreach. In the broader sweep of American political history, her tenure exemplifies a First Lady who combined traditional ceremonial duties with targeted policy advocacy, navigating complex global crises while advancing causes rooted in her professional background.
