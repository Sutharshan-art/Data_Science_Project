**Cambodian–Vietnamese War**

The Cambodian–Vietnamese War was a prolonged military conflict between the Socialist Republic of Vietnam and the Khmer Rouge regime of Democratic Kampuchea (modern-day Cambodia) that began in 1978 and ended formally in 1989, though political ramifications continued well into the 1990s. Rooted in ideological differences, territorial disputes, and regional instability, the war marked a critical episode in Southeast Asian history during the Cold War era.

### Background

Following the Vietnam War and the fall of Saigon in 1975, Vietnam was reunified under a communist government aligned with the Soviet Union. Around the same time, the Khmer Rouge, led by Pol Pot, seized control of Cambodia and established Democratic Kampuchea. Despite their shared communist ideology, the two regimes quickly developed deep antagonisms due to historical tensions, border disputes, and ideological divergence. Vietnam followed a more orthodox Marxist-Leninist model with Soviet backing, while the Khmer Rouge adopted an extreme form of agrarian communism with strong nationalist and anti-Vietnamese sentiment, supported largely by China.

Historical animosity between the two nations dated back centuries, when Vietnamese and Siamese (Thai) empires vied for influence over Cambodia. In modern times, the Khmer Rouge viewed Vietnam as a regional hegemon and sought to assert Cambodia’s independence through militaristic and radical means. Border skirmishes and incursions escalated throughout the mid-1970s, with Cambodian forces attacking Vietnamese villages along the frontier.

### Escalation and Invasion

The conflict escalated dramatically in late 1978. The Khmer Rouge carried out massacres of Vietnamese civilians in border provinces, notably the Ba Chúc massacre, which further inflamed tensions. In response, Vietnam launched a full-scale invasion of Cambodia in December 1978, citing self-defense and the need to end the genocidal policies of the Khmer Rouge.

Vietnamese forces, allied with Cambodian defectors forming the Kampuchean United Front for National Salvation (FUNSK), swiftly overran much of Cambodia. By January 7, 1979, Vietnamese troops had captured Phnom Penh, effectively toppling the Khmer Rouge regime. A new government, the People’s Republic of Kampuchea (PRK), was installed under Heng Samrin, composed largely of former Khmer Rouge members who had fled the purges of Pol Pot.

### International Response

The Vietnamese intervention sparked a complex international response. While Vietnam and the PRK were recognized by the Soviet Union and its allies, China, the United States, and ASEAN nations condemned the invasion as a violation of Cambodian sovereignty. The Khmer Rouge retained Cambodia’s seat at the United Nations, with support from China and several Western governments.

China, angered by Vietnam’s alignment with the Soviet Union and its actions in Cambodia, launched a brief but intense punitive invasion of northern Vietnam in February 1979. The Sino-Vietnamese War ended within a month but signaled deepening Cold War divisions in the region.

### Occupation and Guerrilla War

Following the overthrow of the Khmer Rouge, Vietnam maintained a significant military presence in Cambodia to support the PRK and fend off insurgent activity. The Khmer Rouge, along with other anti-Vietnamese groups such as the FUNCINPEC royalists and KPNLF nationalists, launched a guerrilla war from bases along the Thai-Cambodian border. These factions received varying levels of support from China, the United States, and other Western and regional powers.

The conflict evolved into a protracted insurgency that drained Vietnam’s economy and military resources, particularly as it faced a growing international isolation and an economic embargo led by the United States. Within Cambodia, the PRK struggled to gain widespread legitimacy, partly due to its association with Vietnam and the devastation left by the Khmer Rouge era.

### Withdrawal and Peace Process

By the late 1980s, changing geopolitical dynamics, including the easing of Cold War tensions and the reformist policies of Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, prompted Vietnam to seek a diplomatic solution. Concurrently, Cambodia’s warring factions came under increasing international pressure to negotiate.

Vietnam began a phased withdrawal of its troops in 1988 and completed it by September 1989. In the following years, extensive international mediation led to the signing of the Paris Peace Agreements in 1991. These agreements established a ceasefire, disarmament process, and the framework for a United Nations Transitional Authority in Cambodia (UNTAC), which organized democratic elections in 1993.

### Aftermath

The Cambodian–Vietnamese War had lasting effects on both countries and the region. Cambodia was left politically fractured and economically devastated. The Khmer Rouge remained active in remote areas until the late 1990s but never regained national power. Vietnamese influence in Cambodia remained significant through the 1990s, although overt control diminished.

In Vietnam, the war strained the economy and international relations, particularly with China and the West. However, the eventual resolution allowed Vietnam to reintegrate into the international community and shift toward economic reform in the 1990s.

The war continues to shape Cambodian political discourse, with accusations of Vietnamese interference still present in domestic politics. It also remains a pivotal case in international law and humanitarian intervention, as it involved the overthrow of a genocidal regime by a neighboring power.
