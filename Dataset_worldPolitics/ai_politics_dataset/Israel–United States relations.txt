**Israel–United States Relations**

Israel–United States relations refer to the diplomatic, military, economic, and cultural interactions between the State of Israel and the United States of America. Since Israel’s establishment in 1948, the bilateral relationship has evolved from cautious early engagement to a strategic partnership characterized by close coordination on regional security, intelligence sharing, economic aid, and political support in international forums.

### Early Recognition and Cold War Alignment

The United States was among the first countries to extend de jure recognition to Israel, announcing diplomatic recognition on 14 May 1948, mere minutes after Israel declared independence. Initial U.S. policy sought to balance support for the new Jewish state with maintaining ties to Arab neighbors. During the 1950s and 1960s, Israel and the United States found common cause against Soviet influence in the Middle East, forging a Cold War alliance. U.S. military assistance began modestly with surplus World War II equipment and grew in response to Israel’s needs during the Suez Crisis (1956) and the Six-Day War (1967).

### Military and Security Cooperation

Following the 1967 and 1973 conflicts, U.S. military aid to Israel expanded significantly under successive security assistance programs. In 1976, the United States and Israel signed Formal Military Assistance Agreements, institutionalizing annual grants of advanced weaponry, such as F-15 and F-16 fighter aircraft, tank technology, and missile defense systems. In 1981, the two countries reached a Memorandum of Understanding ensuring Israel’s Qualitative Military Edge, pledging continued U.S. support to prevent regional adversaries from achieving military parity. Joint military exercises and intelligence sharing have deepened cooperation on counterterrorism, missile defense (including the Arrow and David’s Sling programs), and cybersecurity.

### Economic and Foreign Aid

Since 1949, the United States has provided Israel with nearly \$150 billion in bilateral assistance, more than to any other country. From the 1980s onward, over 75 percent of aid has been devoted to defense procurement under Foreign Military Financing, with the remainder supporting economic development and refugee absorption. In recent years, aid has transitioned to a ten-year bilateral Memorandum of Understanding (signed in 2016) guaranteeing \$38 billion in U.S. military assistance from 2019 through 2028. Economic ties extend beyond aid: the 1985 U.S.–Israel Free Trade Agreement fostered bilateral commerce in high‐technology, agriculture, and services.

### Political Support and Diplomatic Engagement

The United States has consistently used its UN Security Council veto power to block or modify resolutions critical of Israel. U.S. administrations have played central roles in Arab–Israeli peacemaking efforts, including the 1978 Camp David Accords, the 1993 Oslo Accords, and the 2000 Camp David Summit. While successive governments have affirmed a two‐state solution as the preferred outcome, U.S. policy has also varied with respect to Israeli settlement activity, recognition of Jerusalem as Israel’s capital (formally acknowledged by Presidential proclamation in 2017), and the relocation of the U.S. embassy to Jerusalem in 2018.

### Israeli–American Public Opinion and Diaspora Influence

Americans generally express favorable views of Israel relative to other Middle Eastern countries, though attitudes vary by political affiliation and religious identification. A robust American Jewish community—numbering approximately six million—serves as a significant constituency in U.S. domestic politics, often advocating for close bilateral ties. Pro‐Israel advocacy organizations, such as the American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC), have played prominent roles in shaping congressional support for aid packages and legislation reinforcing the strategic alliance.

### Points of Tension and Policy Disagreements

Despite broad cooperation, the relationship has experienced periodic strains. U.S. administrations have publicly criticized Israeli settlement expansion in the West Bank, occasionally withholding diplomatic support or imposing symbolic measures. Differences have arisen over approaches to Iran’s nuclear program: the George W. Bush administration’s policy of regime change contrasted with the Obama administration’s negotiations leading to the 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action, which Israel’s government opposed. More recently, debates over normalization agreements between Israel and Arab states (the Abraham Accords) and U.S. pressure on Gaza‐strip aid have highlighted evolving policy dynamics.

### Cultural and Scientific Collaboration

Cultural exchanges, academic partnerships, and scientific cooperation bolster people‐to‐people ties. Hundreds of U.S. universities host joint research programs with Israeli institutions in fields such as medicine, agriculture, and technology. Bilateral science and technology agreements emphasize innovation in water management, cybersecurity, and renewable energy. Tourism flows in both directions and shared cultural festivals further cement societal connections.

### Future Prospects

Looking ahead, Israel–United States relations are likely to remain anchored in strategic imperatives and shared democratic values, even as regional realignments and global challenges reshape Middle East geopolitics. Continued U.S. security guarantees, evolving economic partnerships, and sustained diplomatic engagement on peace initiatives will define the trajectory of this long-standing alliance. As both countries navigate domestic political shifts and external threats, the depth and resilience of their bilateral relationship will continue to be tested and reaffirmed.
