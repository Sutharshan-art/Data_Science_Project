**Washington Summit (1987)**

**Introduction**
The Washington Summit of 1987 was a two-day meeting between U.S. President Ronald Reagan and Soviet General Secretary Mikhail Gorbachev, held from December 8 to 10 at the White House and Camp David. As the third of four Reagan–Gorbachev summits, it marked a turning point in Cold War diplomacy by culminating in signature of the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty, the first agreement to eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons.

**Historical Context**
By the mid-1980s, superpower relations had shifted from the heightened tensions of the early decade to a cautious engagement. President Reagan’s “peace through strength” posture and Strategic Defense Initiative confronted Soviet leaders with an accelerating arms race. Meanwhile, Gorbachev’s domestic reforms—perestroika (restructuring) and glasnost (openness)—signaled willingness to negotiate reductions. The Reykjavik Summit of October 1986 had come close to a comprehensive ban on strategic nuclear weapons but faltered over U.S. defense research. Both leaders agreed to meet again in Washington to finalize negotiations on shorter-range missiles and broader arms-control frameworks.

**Preparations and Agenda**
In the months preceding the summit, U.S. and Soviet delegations held intensive talks in Geneva and New York under the Mutual and Balanced Force Reductions (MBFR) framework and on INF verification protocols. The Washington agenda included:

* **INF Treaty Finalization:** Resolving technical and verification issues concerning land-based missiles with ranges of 500–5,500 kilometers.
* **Strategic Arms Reduction Talks (START):** Establishing parameters for future negotiations on strategic offensive arms.
* **Regional Conflicts:** Discussing developments in Central America, Southern Africa, and Afghanistan, where superpower competition had fueled proxy wars.
* **Human Rights and Cultural Exchanges:** Building on Gorbachev’s openness policies to increase exchanges in science, culture, and education.

**Summit Proceedings**
President Reagan and General Secretary Gorbachev began formal talks on December 8 at the White House Cabinet Room. After opening statements emphasizing shared interest in avoiding nuclear war, the leaders moved to detailed treaty discussions. Key moments included:

* **INF Treaty Signing:** On December 8, the two leaders signed the INF Treaty on the South Lawn. The accord required destruction of all U.S. and Soviet ground-based cruise and ballistic missiles in the 500–5,500 km range within three years, and establishment of rigorous on-site inspection regimes.
* **Camp David Retreat:** On December 9–10, discussions shifted to Camp David. Over a working breakfast and dinner, Reagan and Gorbachev addressed trust-building measures, including military risk reduction talks and expansion of the 1972 Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty’s provisions.
* **Public Addresses:** Both leaders gave joint statements to the press, highlighting the significance of the INF Treaty as a model for future disarmament and expressing optimism for deeper cooperation.

**Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty**
The INF Treaty represented a breakthrough in arms control:

* **Elimination Clause:** It mandated the destruction of 2,692 Soviet and 846 U.S. missiles, including Pershing II and SS-20 systems.
* **Verification Protocol:** It introduced unprecedented on-site inspections, data exchanges, and continuous monitoring of missile production facilities.
* **Implementation:** Over the next three years, more than 2,600 missiles were destroyed under the treaty, verified by both sides and overseen by a joint commission.

**Political and Diplomatic Impact**
The Washington Summit and INF Treaty had wide-ranging effects:

* **Cold War Détente:** They reinforced a shift from confrontation to engagement, encouraging further multilateral arms reductions.
* **Domestic Reactions:** In the United States, congressional ratification debates underscored divisions between skeptics of Soviet intentions and advocates of arms control. The Senate approved the treaty by a 93–5 vote in May 1988. In the Soviet Union, hardliners expressed concern over relinquishing deterrent capabilities, but Gorbachev’s reformist faction prevailed.
* **Global Signal:** The agreement inspired other arms-control initiatives, including the START I negotiations (signed in 1991) and the 1990 Conventional Armed Forces in Europe Treaty.

**Legacy**
The Washington Summit stands as a landmark in U.S.–Soviet relations. By achieving verifiable elimination of an entire class of nuclear weapons, it built momentum for the eventual end of the Cold War. The summit’s emphasis on transparency and mutual verification laid foundations for later non-proliferation and disarmament efforts. In subsequent years, the cooperative spirit fostered in Washington contributed to the withdrawal of Soviet forces from Afghanistan, conventional force reductions in Europe, and the opening of new channels of cultural and scientific exchange between East and West.
