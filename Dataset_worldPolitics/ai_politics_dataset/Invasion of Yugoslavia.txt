**Invasion of Yugoslavia**

The Invasion of Yugoslavia, also known as the April War (Serbo-Croatian: Aprilski rat), was a brief but decisive Axis campaign conducted from 6 to 17 April 1941 that led to the dismemberment of the Kingdom of Yugoslavia and its occupation by Germany, Italy, Hungary, and Bulgaria. The operation formed part of the wider Balkans Campaign of World War II, intended to secure Axis flanks before the invasion of the Soviet Union and to assist in the simultaneous assault on Greece.

### Political Background

In the late 1930s, Yugoslavia’s multi-ethnic monarchy sought to maintain neutrality amid growing Axis and Allied pressures. Under Regent Prince Paul, Belgrade concluded a Treaty of Friendship with Italy in 1937 and resisted German requests for access to its territory. By March 1941, however, German influence had grown: under threat of economic blockade and diplomatic isolation, Yugoslavia signed the Tripartite Pact on 25 March, formally joining the Axis alignment while retaining nominal sovereignty. This decision provoked widespread public outrage—particularly among Serbs—and prompted a military-backed coup d’état on 27 March 1941, which deposed Prince Paul, installed the pro-British 17-year-old King Peter II, and repudiated the alliance.

### Axis Decision to Invade

Adolf Hitler viewed the Yugoslav coup as a direct challenge to German strategic interests in Southeast Europe. Control of the Yugoslav rail network and the Danube corridor was vital for supplying Axis forces in Greece and for securing access to Romanian oil. On 31 March, Hitler issued Führer Directive No. 25, ordering the rapid occupation and military dismemberment of Yugoslavia. Simultaneously, Germany committed to a renewed offensive against Greece, coordinated under the codename “Operation Marita.”

### Axis Forces and Plan

The Axis invasion force comprised approximately 22 divisions and supporting air units from Germany’s 2nd Army (under Generaloberst Maximilian von Weichs), reinforced by Italian, Hungarian, and Bulgarian formations along Yugoslavia’s southern, northeastern, and eastern borders, respectively. German Army Group E, based in Romania and Bulgaria, was tasked with seizing key river crossings, mountain passes, and the capital, Belgrade. Luftwaffe units were assigned to suppress Yugoslav airfields and communications, while Italian forces in Albania and along the Adriatic coast threatened the western and southern borders.

### Yugoslav Defenses and Internal Divisions

Yugoslavia mobilized seven field armies, with dispositions reflecting an ethnic compromise: Serb-dominated units held critical sectors, while Croat and Slovene formations covered the northwest. The Royal Yugoslav Air Force numbered some 460 aircraft—many obsolete biplanes—and the Royal Yugoslav Navy was small and confined to the Adriatic. Political fragmentation and poor inter-service coordination undermined defensive planning. Moreover, Ustaše and other Croatian fascist elements awaited Axis success to proclaim an independent Croatian state, eroding troop morale and cohesion.

### Course of the Campaign

* **6 April**: German bombers struck Belgrade and other major cities in one of the first major urban aerial bombardments of the war. Simultaneously, German motorized corps crossed the Drava and Danube rivers in the north and advanced through mountain passes in the east and southeast.
* **7–9 April**: Rapid armored thrusts by the 8th Panzer and 14th Panzer Divisions overran the Vardar and Morava river valleys, severing communications between Macedonian and Serbian forces. Yugoslav units attempted counterattacks but were outmatched by superior German mobility and air supremacy.
* **10–12 April**: Italian forces advanced from Albania, capturing towns in Kosovo and western Macedonia, while Hungarian troops occupied Bačka and Baranja in the north. Bulgarian forces invaded eastern Macedonia and the Raška region, further encircling Belgrade.
* **13–15 April**: Axis panzer divisions reached the outskirts of Belgrade. Infantry divisions seized key mountain passes and rail junctions. Sporadic Yugoslav resistance continued in isolated pockets, notably in Montenegro and parts of the Dalmatian hinterland.
* **16–17 April**: With the capital threatened and lines of communication severed, the Yugoslav Supreme Command authorized unconditional surrender. Late on 17 April, the Yugoslav government capitulated in Belgrade. Fighting persisted only in remote areas until 18 April.

### Aftermath and Occupation

Yugoslavia’s collapse in eleven days had profound consequences. Germany and Italy carved the country into occupation zones and client states. The Independent State of Croatia (NDH) was established under a fascist Ustaše regime. Serbia was occupied directly by Germany with a puppet government in Belgrade, while Montenegro became an Italian protectorate. Slovenia was annexed in part by Germany, Italy, and Hungary. The German occupational authority also instituted forced labor and reprisals against civilians, igniting widespread resistance.

### Resistance and Legacy

Axis brutality and the dismantling of Yugoslavia spurred the emergence of two major resistance movements: the royalist Chetniks under Draža Mihailović and the communist-led Partisans under Josip Broz Tito. These factions soon clashed over strategy and ideology, setting the stage for a complex civil war within the broader anti-Axis struggle. The swift defeat and dismemberment of the pre-war state exacerbated ethnic tensions that would reverberate throughout the Balkans for decades.

The Invasion of Yugoslavia demonstrated the effectiveness of modern combined-arms warfare and the importance of political unity in national defense. It reshaped the political map of Southeastern Europe, contributed to the opening of the Balkan front in World War II, and laid the groundwork for Yugoslavia’s postwar federal structure under communist rule.
