**Partition of Bosnia and Herzegovina**

The **Partition of Bosnia and Herzegovina** refers to political proposals, war-time strategies, and diplomatic initiatives during the 1990s aimed at dividing the multiethnic republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina into separate territorial entities along ethnic lines. These partition efforts emerged in the context of the dissolution of Yugoslavia and the subsequent Bosnian War (1992–1995), involving Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims), Croats, and Serbs.

Though an outright partition was never formally enacted, the war and subsequent peace agreements resulted in a de facto division of Bosnia and Herzegovina into two main autonomous entities. The legacy of partition remains a central and contentious issue in the country’s post-war political structure and interethnic relations.

---

### Background

Bosnia and Herzegovina was one of six constituent republics of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia. It was home to three main ethnic groups—Bosniaks, Serbs, and Croats—who had coexisted under Yugoslav rule. With the weakening of the federal system and rising nationalism in the late 1980s and early 1990s, tensions escalated across the republics.

In 1991, Slovenia and Croatia declared independence, triggering the Yugoslav Wars. In Bosnia and Herzegovina, the 1992 independence referendum—boycotted by most Bosnian Serbs—led to an internationally recognized declaration of independence. Soon after, war erupted among the three main ethnic groups, with the newly independent government, supported primarily by Bosniaks, facing opposition from Bosnian Serb and Bosnian Croat nationalist forces.

---

### Partition Proposals

During the war, political leaders from the Bosnian Serb and Croat communities, often backed by neighboring Serbia and Croatia, advanced proposals to divide Bosnia and Herzegovina along ethnic lines. These efforts were frequently justified in terms of protecting ethnic interests or achieving self-determination.

#### Bosnian Serb Proposals

Under the leadership of **Radovan Karadžić** and the military command of **Ratko Mladić**, Bosnian Serb forces aimed to create a contiguous Serb territory that could either gain autonomy or unify with Serbia. The self-proclaimed **Republika Srpska** was established in 1992 and expanded through military campaigns, often involving ethnic cleansing of non-Serbs in captured areas.

The goal was to secure large swathes of eastern and northern Bosnia where Serbs formed significant populations. This vision of partition was supported by **Slobodan Milošević**, the President of Serbia, who pursued the idea of a “Greater Serbia.”

#### Bosnian Croat Proposals

Simultaneously, Bosnian Croats, under the political leadership of the **Croatian Democratic Union of Bosnia and Herzegovina (HDZ-BiH)** and military assistance from Croatia, sought to establish **Herceg-Bosna**, a Croat-dominated entity in the western part of the country. This move aligned with Croatian President **Franjo Tuđman**’s vision of a possible territorial union with Croatia.

Although initially allied with Bosniaks against Serb forces, tensions between Croats and Bosniaks escalated into open conflict in 1993, leading to another front in the war and further complicating partition dynamics.

---

### International Peace Plans

Throughout the war, international actors proposed various peace plans that effectively endorsed or accommodated the idea of partition, albeit under the guise of federalism or power-sharing.

* The **Carrington–Cutileiro Plan** (1992) proposed ethnic cantons but was rejected by Bosniak leaders.
* The **Vance–Owen Peace Plan** (1993) suggested dividing Bosnia into ten semi-autonomous provinces along ethnic lines. While accepted by some parties, it was ultimately undermined by continued fighting.
* The **Owen–Stoltenberg Plan** (1993) offered a loose confederation of three ethnic republics, but was rejected by Bosniaks as legitimizing ethnic division.

These plans reflected a general international reluctance to force a single centralized government on warring factions but also demonstrated the difficulty of reconciling competing nationalistic ambitions with Bosnia’s multiethnic reality.

---

### Dayton Agreement and De Facto Partition

The **Dayton Peace Agreement**, signed in December 1995, ended the Bosnian War and established Bosnia and Herzegovina as a single sovereign state composed of two main entities:

* The **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina**, inhabited mainly by Bosniaks and Croats.
* **Republika Srpska**, dominated by Bosnian Serbs.

Each entity received significant autonomy, with its own government, police, and institutions, while overarching state-level structures were created for foreign policy and fiscal coordination. A weak central government and the complex power-sharing arrangement effectively institutionalized ethnic divisions.

Though not officially termed a “partition,” the Dayton framework entrenched the wartime territorial realities, creating what many observers regard as a de facto partition of the country.

---

### Legacy and Contemporary Debates

The partition debate remains a sensitive and polarizing issue in Bosnia and Herzegovina. While some political actors—particularly among Bosnian Serbs—continue to advocate for greater autonomy or secession, others, especially Bosniaks, push for a more unified and centralized state.

Efforts to reform the Dayton constitution and strengthen the central government have largely stalled due to political gridlock and nationalist agendas. International actors, including the European Union and the Office of the High Representative, have struggled to foster post-war reconciliation and state-building in a deeply divided society.

The legacy of partition, both as a wartime goal and a post-war reality, continues to shape Bosnia and Herzegovina’s political landscape, affecting its governance, identity, and prospects for Euro-Atlantic integration.
