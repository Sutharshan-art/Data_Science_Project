**Nigel Lawson, Baron Lawson of Blaby**

**Nigel Lawson, Baron Lawson of Blaby** (11 March 1932 – 3 April 2023) was a British Conservative politician, journalist, and financier best known for his tenure as Chancellor of the Exchequer from 1983 to 1989 under Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher. A leading architect of Thatcherite economic policy, Lawson oversaw major tax reforms, financial deregulation, and a period of sustained economic growth, while his later career included high-profile roles in the media and vigorous advocacy on fiscal and environmental issues.

## Early Life and Career

Nigel Lawson was born in Hampstead, London, the son of Jack Lawson, a barrister, and Winifred Burnet-Stubbs. He was educated at Westminster School and Christ Church, Oxford, where he read Philosophy, Politics and Economics, graduating with first-class honors in 1953. After national service in the Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve, Lawson entered the financial sector, working as a merchant banker with Schroders and later co-founding the investment bank Lawson Mardon & Co.

In parallel with his banking career, Lawson wrote extensively on economics for *The Sunday Telegraph* (1961–1972) and *The Spectator*, establishing a reputation as a clear and persuasive commentator on fiscal and monetary policy.

## Parliamentary Career

Lawson entered Parliament at the February 1974 general election, winning the safe Conservative seat of Blaby in Leicestershire. He served as a junior treasury spokesman in Edward Heath’s shadow cabinet and, after the Conservative defeat in 1974, became an influential backbench critic of Labour’s economic policies. Under Margaret Thatcher’s leadership, he was appointed Economic Secretary to the Treasury (1979–1981), where he supported monetarist measures to control inflation, and then Financial Secretary to the Treasury (1981–1983).

## Chancellor of the Exchequer (1983–1989)

In June 1983, following the Conservatives’ electoral landslide, Prime Minister Thatcher appointed Lawson Chancellor. Over his six-year tenure, he pursued a broadly neoliberal agenda:

* **Tax Reform:** Lawson reduced the top rate of personal income tax from 60% to 40% and cut the basic rate from 30% to 25%, financed in part by raising Value Added Tax from 8% to 15%.
* **Privatisation:** He continued the sale of state-owned enterprises initiated earlier in the Thatcher government, including British Telecom (1984) and British Gas (1986).
* **Deregulation and the “Big Bang”:** In 1986, Lawson oversaw the deregulation of London’s financial markets—known as the Big Bang—abolishing fixed commission rates and allowing foreign and institutional ownership of brokers, which revitalised the City of London as a global financial centre.
* **Monetary Policy:** His tenure saw a shift toward greater reliance on interest rates to control inflation, culminating in the 1988 move to grant operational independence to the Bank of England for setting monetary policy targets.
* **Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM):** Lawson negotiated Britain’s entry into the ERM in October 1990 (announced before his resignation), aiming to stabilise the pound by tying it to European currencies—a policy later abandoned during the 1992 crisis.

Under Lawson, the UK experienced sustained growth, low unemployment, and reduced inflation, though critics pointed to rising home-ownership costs and widening income inequality.

## Resignation and the “Lawson Boom”

Mounting tensions with Prime Minister Thatcher over ERM entry and budgetary discipline led to Lawson’s resignation in October 1989. The subsequent “Lawson boom” of credit-fuelled growth and tax cuts, followed by a sharp recession in the early 1990s, sparked debate over the long-term wisdom of his expansionary fiscal policies.

## Later Career and Media Work

After leaving frontline politics, Lawson was created Baron Lawson of Blaby in 1992. He served as a financial commentator for the BBC’s *Today* programme and founded the Centre for Policy Studies think tank with fellow Thatcherites Sir Keith Joseph and Sir Alfred Sherman. He held directorships in banking and media and wrote several books, including the memoir *The View from No. 11* (1992).

## Political Views and Advocacy

A committed free-market liberal, Lawson continued to champion lower taxes, deregulation, and fiscal prudence into the 21st century. In the 1990s and 2000s, he became a leading voice of Euroscepticism, arguing that European integration undermined British sovereignty and economic flexibility.

Later in life, he mounted high-profile challenges to prevailing environmental orthodoxy. As chair of the Global Warming Policy Foundation (founded 2009), Lawson criticised mandatory carbon-emission targets and questioned aspects of climate-change science, earning both support from climate-policy sceptics and condemnation from environmentalists.

## Personal Life

In 1955, Lawson married Marigold Davies, with whom he had four children, including broadcaster Dominic Lawson. The marriage ended in divorce in 1995; Lawson married Theresa Gorman, a former Conservative MP, in 1999. He died on 3 April 2023 at age 91.

## Legacy

Lawson’s chancellorship left a profound imprint on the British economy—his tax reforms and financial deregulation reshaped the relationship between the state, markets, and consumers. Advocates credit him with modernising Britain’s fiscal framework and revitalising its financial sector; detractors highlight the social and economic imbalances intensified by his policies. His later roles as a public intellectual and climate-policy critic further positioned him as a pivotal, if polarising, figure in late 20th- and early 21st-century British political debate.
