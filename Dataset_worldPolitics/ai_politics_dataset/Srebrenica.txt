**Srebrenica**

**Srebrenica** is a town and municipality located in eastern **Bosnia and Herzegovina**, near the border with Serbia. It is situated in the entity of **Republika Srpska**, one of the two main administrative divisions of the country established by the Dayton Peace Agreement in 1995. Though modest in size, Srebrenica holds profound historical and political significance due to its role in the **Bosnian War** and as the site of the **1995 Srebrenica massacre**, which was later ruled a genocide by international courts.

The town has been at the center of various political, ethnic, and historical developments, particularly during the disintegration of Yugoslavia in the 1990s. Today, Srebrenica continues to be a symbol of post-war reconciliation challenges, transitional justice, and the contested narratives surrounding the conflict in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

---

### Historical Background

Srebrenica has a long and complex history dating back to Roman and medieval times. The town’s name is derived from the Slavic word for “silver” (*srebro*), referencing its long-standing reputation as a mining center. In the Middle Ages, Srebrenica was known for its silver mines and was part of the medieval Bosnian state and later the Ottoman Empire.

During the **Austro-Hungarian** and **Yugoslav** periods, the town retained its economic importance as a mining and spa destination. Srebrenica’s population historically consisted of both **Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims)** and **Bosnian Serbs**, with interethnic relations fluctuating over time.

By the late 20th century, prior to the outbreak of the Bosnian War, the municipality had a **Bosniak majority** and a **Serb minority**, reflecting the multi-ethnic character typical of many parts of Bosnia and Herzegovina.

---

### Bosnian War and UN Safe Area

The outbreak of the **Bosnian War (1992–1995)**, following the disintegration of Yugoslavia and Bosnia’s declaration of independence, brought widespread violence and ethnic cleansing throughout the country. Eastern Bosnia, including Srebrenica, became a central battleground between Bosniak and Bosnian Serb forces.

Srebrenica was under siege for much of the war. As violence intensified and thousands of Bosniaks fled from surrounding areas, the town became overcrowded and increasingly dependent on humanitarian aid. In **April 1993**, the **United Nations** declared Srebrenica a **“safe area”**, to be protected by UN peacekeepers, in an effort to prevent further violence against civilians.

A Dutch battalion (**Dutchbat**) of UN peacekeepers was stationed in Srebrenica, but its mandate and resources were limited. Over time, the town remained isolated and vulnerable to attack.

---

### 1995 Srebrenica Massacre

On **July 11, 1995**, Bosnian Serb forces under **General Ratko Mladić** entered Srebrenica and overran the UN-declared safe area. Over the following days, more than **8,000 Bosniak men and boys** were systematically separated from women and children, executed, and buried in mass graves. Women, children, and the elderly were forcibly expelled to Bosniak-held areas.

The atrocity, committed by the **Army of Republika Srpska (VRS)**, was later recognized as an act of **genocide** by the **International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY)** and the **International Court of Justice (ICJ)**. It marked the worst mass killing in Europe since World War II and had a significant impact on the course of the war and international responses.

The massacre underscored the limitations of international peacekeeping and was a critical factor in shifting Western policy toward greater military intervention, including **NATO airstrikes** that eventually helped bring about the **Dayton Peace Agreement**.

---

### Post-War Status and Governance

After the war, Srebrenica became part of **Republika Srpska**, one of the two entities of **Bosnia and Herzegovina**, alongside the **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina**. This arrangement was formalized by the **Dayton Accords** in **1995**, which ended the conflict but also entrenched ethnic divisions through a complex power-sharing system.

The town has since faced numerous political and administrative challenges, including disputes over governance, representation, and the return of displaced persons. Many **Bosniak survivors** and their families returned to Srebrenica after the war, though demographic shifts and political tensions continue to affect the municipality.

The position of mayor has alternated between Serb and Bosniak candidates, often reflecting broader national-level tensions. Commemorations of the massacre remain politically charged, and the acknowledgment of historical events continues to divide political elites in Republika Srpska and Serbia.

---

### Memorialization and Reconciliation

The **Srebrenica–Potočari Memorial and Cemetery**, located near the UN compound where thousands sought refuge in 1995, was established to honor the victims. Each year on **July 11**, mass commemorations are held, with newly identified remains from mass graves interred during memorial ceremonies.

The memorial has become a focal point for both mourning and calls for justice. It is visited by international dignitaries, survivors, and organizations committed to genocide prevention and human rights. However, some political leaders in Republika Srpska and Serbia continue to deny or downplay the genocide classification, hindering reconciliation efforts.

---

### Contemporary Significance

Today, Srebrenica stands as a powerful symbol of the consequences of ethnic violence and the challenges of post-conflict recovery. It represents not only the human cost of war but also the long-lasting implications of international decisions, domestic politics, and efforts at transitional justice.

Efforts to rebuild and revitalize Srebrenica have been met with mixed success, due in part to economic underdevelopment and political fragmentation. Nevertheless, the town remains central to ongoing discussions about memory, justice, and the future of a multi-ethnic Bosnia and Herzegovina.
