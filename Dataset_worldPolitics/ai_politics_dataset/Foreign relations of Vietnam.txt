**Foreign Relations of Vietnam**

The foreign relations of the Socialist Republic of Vietnam encompass the diplomatic, economic, and security interactions that Vietnam maintains with other countries and international organizations. Shaped by its colonial past, the Indochina Wars, Cold War alignments, and the post–Đổi Mới reforms, Vietnam’s external policy has evolved from revolutionary solidarity and isolation to pragmatic multilateral engagement and economic integration.

### Historical Context

**Revolutionary and Cold War Era (1945–1975)**
After proclaiming independence from France on 2 September 1945, the Democratic Republic of Vietnam (North Vietnam) aligned itself with the Soviet Union and the People’s Republic of China, securing military and economic assistance during the First Indochina War (1946–1954). Following the 1954 Geneva Accords, Vietnam was temporarily partitioned along the 17th parallel. North Vietnam deepened its socialist ties, while South Vietnam received backing from the United States and other anti-communist allies. During the Vietnam War (1955–1975), North Vietnam’s foreign policy focused on securing support for reunification, culminating in the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975.

**Post–Reunification Socialist Bloc (1975–1986)**
After reunification, the Socialist Republic of Vietnam maintained close relations with Moscow and Beijing but was caught between the Sino–Soviet split. Vietnam’s 1978 intervention in Cambodia against the Khmer Rouge severed ties with China, leading to a brief but costly Sino–Vietnamese War in 1979. Vietnam’s occupation of Cambodia (1979–1989) brought international isolation and economic sanctions from Western and non–socialist states.

### Đổi Mới and Reorientation (1986–2000)\*\*

Facing economic crisis and diplomatic isolation, the Communist Party of Vietnam launched **Đổi Mới** (Renovation) in 1986, initiating market-oriented reforms and a “diversification and multilateralization” of foreign relations. Vietnam withdrew from Cambodia in 1989, normalized relations with China in 1991, and joined the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) in 1995—its first major post–Cold War diplomatic breakthrough. In 1995, Vietnam also normalized diplomatic and trade relations with the United States, ending decades of hostility. The same year, it established ties with Japan, South Korea, and the European Union, attracting foreign investment and development aid.

### Economic Integration and Multilateral Engagement (2000–Present)\*\*

Vietnam continued to deepen economic ties through bilateral and multilateral free-trade agreements, joining the World Trade Organization (WTO) in 2007 and negotiating entry into the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) in 2018. It has signed bilateral investment treaties with major partners—China, Japan, South Korea, the European Union, and the United States—while pursuing membership in the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) in 2020.

On the multilateral front, Vietnam has been an active member of ASEAN, serving as its chair in 2010 and 2020, and has contributed to ASEAN-led security initiatives such as the ASEAN Regional Forum (ARF) and the ASEAN Defense Ministers’ Meeting–Plus (ADMM-Plus). It also participates in Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC), the East Asia Summit, and holds observer status in the Organization of Islamic Cooperation (OIC), reflecting engagement beyond its immediate neighborhood.

### Bilateral Relations

* **China**: Vietnam’s largest trading partner and neighbor. Relations are characterized by deep economic interdependence and periodic maritime tensions in the South China Sea over territorial claims.
* **United States**: Since normalization in 1995, ties have strengthened across trade, defense, education, and health. The U.S. lifted its arms embargo in 2016, and bilateral trade surpassed \$100 billion by 2020.
* **Russia**: The successor to the Soviet Union remains a key partner in energy, defense procurement, and nuclear cooperation, including collaboration on the Ninh Thuận nuclear power project.
* **Japan and South Korea**: Major sources of development aid, investment, and technology transfer; both have strategic partnerships with Vietnam, focusing on infrastructure, human-resource development, and supply-chain integration.
* **European Union**: Vietnam signed an EU–Vietnam Free Trade Agreement (EVFTA) in 2019, boosting trade, investment, and cooperation on sustainable development and human rights.

### Security and Defense Cooperation

Vietnam’s defense diplomacy has expanded in response to regional security challenges. It conducts joint exercises and port calls with the United States, Japan, India, and Australia under frameworks such as the Quad-plus engagement. Vietnam also cooperates with Russia on military modernization and participates in UN peacekeeping missions—first sending troops to South Sudan in 2018. Balancing external partnerships with a policy of non–alignment, Vietnam avoids formal alliances while deepening practical defense ties.

### South China Sea Disputes

Central to Vietnam’s foreign relations is the dispute over maritime sovereignty in the South China Sea (East Sea). Vietnam asserts claims in the Gulf of Tonkin and the Spratly and Paracel Islands, conflicting with China’s “nine-dash line,” as well as with the Philippines and Malaysia. Vietnam has filed submissions to the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) and strengthened legal and diplomatic efforts, seeking ASEAN unity and international support for peaceful dispute resolution.

### Development Assistance and Diaspora Engagement

Vietnam leverages its growing economy to provide South–South assistance—in sectors like agriculture and infrastructure—to Laos, Cambodia, and Pacific island states. It also maintains an active overseas Vietnamese (Việt Kiều) network, fostering investment, skills transfer, and cultural diplomacy, while addressing consular and welfare concerns of its diaspora.

### Challenges and Future Directions

Vietnam’s foreign policy must navigate intensifying U.S.–China strategic competition, global supply-chain shifts, and the imperatives of sustainable development and climate resilience. Its emphasis on diversification—cultivating ties with the EU, India, and Middle Eastern countries—seeks to reduce overreliance on any single partner. Vietnam’s continued participatory approach in multilateral forums, balanced bilateral outreach, and commitment to international law underpin its strategy to safeguard national sovereignty and sustain economic growth in a complex geopolitical environment.
