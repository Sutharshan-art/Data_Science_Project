**Women in Bosnia and Herzegovina**

**Introduction**
Women in Bosnia and Herzegovina have played vital roles in the country’s social, economic, and political development, navigating shifting norms from the Austro-Hungarian and Yugoslav periods through the 1992–1995 war and into the post-Dayton era. While legal frameworks guarantee gender equality, enduring ethnic divisions, economic challenges, and traditional social structures continue to shape women’s opportunities and representation.

**Historical Context**
Under Austro-Hungarian rule (1878–1918), modernization efforts introduced limited educational and employment opportunities for urban women, though rural patriarchal structures remained entrenched. During the Kingdom of Yugoslavia (1918–1941), women gained the right to vote (1940) and increasingly participated in civic life. The Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (1945–1992) promoted gender equality through state policies: women entered the workforce in large numbers, benefited from guaranteed maternity leave, and had access to universal education and healthcare. Nevertheless, traditional gender roles persisted, especially in rural and Bosniak-Muslim communities.

**Impact of the 1992–1995 War**
The Bosnian War profoundly affected women’s lives. An estimated 20,000 to 50,000 women—from Bosniak, Croat, and Serb communities—were subjected to systematic sexual violence, later recognized as genocide in international tribunals. Displacement uprooted more than half the female population, many becoming heads of households in refugee camps. Simultaneously, women served as combatants, medics, and relief workers, organizing survival networks and cross-ethnic aid initiatives. Their wartime experiences galvanized a generation of activists advocating for human rights and transitional justice.

**Legal and Constitutional Framework**
The 1995 Dayton Peace Agreement enshrined Bosnia and Herzegovina as a state composed of two entities (the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina and Republika Srpska) and the Brčko District. The country’s Constitution—an annex to the Dayton Accords—prohibits discrimination based on gender and mandates equal rights. Subsequent legislation, including the 2003 Gender Equality Law, established the Agency for Gender Equality and set quotas requiring a minimum 30 percent female representation on electoral lists for state and entity legislatures. International conventions—such as CEDAW (ratified 1993)—further obligate the state to combat gender-based discrimination.

**Political Representation**
Quota systems have steadily increased women’s parliamentary presence. As of the 2022 general election, women held approximately 40 percent of seats in the House of Representatives of the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina, 33 percent in the state-level House of Representatives, and 25 percent in the National Assembly of Republika Srpska. Despite numerical gains, women often occupy secondary roles in party leadership and face barriers to executive positions, reflecting ongoing patriarchal party structures and ethnic power-sharing agreements that prioritize representation by Bosniak, Croat, and Serb “constituent peoples” over gender equality.

**Economic Participation and Challenges**
Women’s labor-force participation stands at roughly 48 percent, below the European average. Gender wage gaps persist, with women earning on average 15–20 percent less than men. High unemployment rates—especially among young women—and limited access to capital and property rights in rural areas exacerbate economic marginalization. Informal employment in agriculture and small-scale retail—often unpaid or low-paid—remains common. Social norms continue to assign primary caregiving responsibilities to women, limiting career advancement.

**Civil Society and Women's Movements**
Bosnian women’s NGOs have been instrumental in post-war recovery and advocacy. Organizations such as the Association of Women Victims of War and Medica Zenica provide legal aid, psychosocial support, and campaign for reparations and truth commissions. The 2005 establishment of the Council of Ministers’ Gender Equality Agency and the 2008 formation of the Inter-Parliamentary Union’s Local Chapter in Sarajevo enhanced coordination between government and civil society. Youth networks—like UN Women’s Young Women’s Christian Association initiatives—focus on leadership training and combating gender-based violence.

**Transitional Justice and War Crimes Accountability**
Women’s testimonies before the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) were pivotal in prosecuting crimes against humanity and sexual enslavement. Landmark judgments—such as the Conviction of Radovan Karadžić and Ratko Mladić—recognized rape as a war crime and genocide act. Domestic war-crimes chambers have since tried lower-level perpetrators, though many cases remain unresolved. The Women’s Court Project (2016–2019), a civil-society initiative, sought symbolic judicial forums to document and commemorate sexual-violence survivors outside formal courts.

**Current Issues and Policy Debates**
Despite legal guarantees, implementation gaps persist. Debates over the gender-based violence law (2013) highlighted clashes between secular and conservative religious values, especially regarding family-law provisions in entity legislatures. Advocacy for comprehensive sex education and reproductive rights faces resistance from traditionalist factions. Women’s rights activists continue to press for integration of gender-mainstreaming in public administration and judiciary reforms to enhance female judges’ representation.

**International Support and Integration**
European Union accession negotiations—opened in 2016—mandate progress on gender equality and minority rights, tying reforms to funding and visa-liberalization roadmaps. United Nations Development Programme and Council of Europe projects support capacity building for gender focal points in ministries and police forces. NATO-led stabilization efforts include gender advisors in peacekeeping training.

**Conclusion**
Women in Bosnia and Herzegovina have transitioned from wartime trauma to leadership in politics and civil society, shaping the country’s democratic and reconciliation processes. While legal frameworks offer a foundation for gender equality, entrenched ethnic power-sharing, economic disparities, and traditional norms continue to challenge full realization of women’s rights. Ongoing advocacy by women’s movements, supported by international frameworks and EU integration pressures, remains crucial to advancing gender parity in Bosnia and Herzegovina’s evolving political landscape.
