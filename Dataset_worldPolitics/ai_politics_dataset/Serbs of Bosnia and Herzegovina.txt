**Serbs of Bosnia and Herzegovina**

The **Serbs of Bosnia and Herzegovina** (Bosnian/Serbian: *Srbi u Bosni i Hercegovini*) are one of the three constitutionally recognized constituent peoples of Bosnia and Herzegovina, alongside Bosniaks and Croats. As South Slavic Orthodox Christians, Bosnian Serbs share ethnic, cultural, and historical ties with Serbs in Serbia and Montenegro. They have played a significant role in the political and social history of Bosnia and Herzegovina, particularly during periods of conflict and state formation.

As of the last official census conducted in 2013, Serbs made up approximately 30.8% of the country’s population. The majority of Bosnian Serbs live in **Republika Srpska**, one of the two constitutional and legal entities of Bosnia and Herzegovina, established by the Dayton Peace Agreement in 1995.

### Historical Background

The presence of Serbs in Bosnia and Herzegovina dates back to the early medieval period. During the time of the **Nemanjić dynasty**, parts of Bosnia came under Serbian rule, particularly eastern regions. By the 15th century, the Ottoman Empire had conquered the entire territory of modern Bosnia and Herzegovina. Under Ottoman rule, the Serbian Orthodox Church played a central role in preserving Serb identity, culture, and religion, especially through the Patriarchate of Peć.

During Ottoman times, many Serbs were involved in uprisings against Ottoman authorities and retained strong religious and cultural distinctions. The **Millet system** allowed Orthodox Christians to maintain autonomy in religious and communal affairs, reinforcing a sense of separate identity from the Muslim and Catholic populations.

In the 19th century, the rise of nationalism among South Slavs included growing demands by Serbs in Bosnia and Herzegovina for unity with Serbia. The Austro-Hungarian annexation of Bosnia in 1908 was opposed by many Serbs, who viewed it as a barrier to national unification. The **assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand** in Sarajevo in 1914 by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb affiliated with the nationalist group *Young Bosnia*, played a key role in sparking World War I.

### Kingdom of Yugoslavia and World War II

After World War I, Bosnia and Herzegovina became part of the **Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes**, later known as Yugoslavia. Serbs held a dominant position within the royalist state, with many state institutions led by ethnic Serbs. This period saw centralization policies that favored Serbian elites, creating resentment among Croats and Bosniaks.

During World War II, Bosnia and Herzegovina was incorporated into the Nazi-aligned **Independent State of Croatia (NDH)**, where Serbs were subject to systematic persecution by the Ustaše regime. Hundreds of thousands of Serbs were killed or expelled, and many joined the royalist Chetnik or communist Partisan resistance movements.

### Socialist Yugoslavia

In post-war socialist Yugoslavia, Bosnia and Herzegovina became one of six republics within the federation. The regime promoted a Yugoslav identity and attempted to suppress ethnic nationalism. However, Serbs in Bosnia maintained strong connections to Serbia and were prominent in the Communist Party leadership and the federal military.

Despite attempts at ethnic balance, inter-republic tensions simmered beneath the surface. In Bosnia, Serbs constituted the largest ethnic group according to the 1991 census (approximately 31.3%). They lived throughout the country, with significant populations in eastern Bosnia, the Sarajevo region, and the Krajina.

### Bosnian War and Republika Srpska

Following the declaration of independence by Bosnia and Herzegovina in 1992, which was opposed by the majority of Serbs, the country descended into war. Bosnian Serb political and military leaders, led by **Radovan Karadžić** and **Ratko Mladić**, declared the creation of **Republika Srpska**, seeking to unite Serb-majority areas with Serbia.

The war was marked by widespread violence, including the **Siege of Sarajevo**, ethnic cleansing campaigns, and the **Srebrenica massacre**, in which over 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were killed. These events were later classified as genocide and war crimes by international courts. The **Dayton Accords**, signed in 1995, ended the conflict and established Bosnia and Herzegovina as a single state composed of two entities: the **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina** and **Republika Srpska**.

### Post-War Politics

In the post-Dayton political system, Serbs are one of three constituent peoples with guaranteed representation in state institutions. The Serb member of the tripartite Presidency represents the interests of Serbs at the national level. Republika Srpska enjoys a high degree of autonomy, with its own president, parliament, police force, and government.

Serb political parties, most notably the **Alliance of Independent Social Democrats (SNSD)** led by **Milorad Dodik**, have frequently advocated for greater autonomy and have at times challenged the authority of state-level institutions. Tensions between Republika Srpska and the central government have persisted, especially on issues related to judicial authority, defense, and foreign policy.

### Cultural and Religious Identity

Bosnian Serbs are predominantly adherents of the **Serbian Orthodox Church**, with the **Metropolitanate of Dabar-Bosna** being one of the most important religious institutions. Cultural identity is shaped by language, religious traditions, and historical narratives centered around resistance, survival, and Orthodox Christianity.

Cultural contributions include literature, music, and art that draw on both rural and urban traditions. While integration efforts exist, interethnic relations in some regions remain strained due to wartime memories and divergent national narratives.

### Contemporary Issues

Today, Serbs in Bosnia and Herzegovina continue to navigate complex questions of identity, governance, and coexistence. While Republika Srpska remains a central pillar of Serb political life, debates over constitutional reform, European Union integration, and reconciliation efforts continue to shape the political landscape.

Relations with Serbia remain strong, both culturally and politically, though Serbia officially supports the territorial integrity of Bosnia and Herzegovina. The role of Bosnian Serbs remains critical to the country’s stability, political functionality, and long-term prospects for peace and integration.
