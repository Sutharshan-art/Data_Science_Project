**List of Convicted War Criminals**

War crimes prosecutions have been conducted under various national and international tribunals to hold individuals accountable for grave breaches of international humanitarian law. This list highlights notable convictions across major conflicts, illustrating the evolving legal and political contexts of war crimes justice.

---

## Nuremberg Trials (1945–1946)

Following World War II, the **International Military Tribunal (IMT)** at Nuremberg prosecuted senior officials of Nazi Germany. Key convictions included:

* **Hermann Göring** (1893–1946): Founder of the Gestapo and Reichsmarschall, convicted of crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity; sentenced to death, committed suicide before execution.
* **Rudolf Hess** (1894–1987): Deputy Führer, convicted of crimes against peace; sentenced to life imprisonment.
* **Joachim von Ribbentrop** (1893–1946): Foreign Minister, convicted of war crimes and crimes against humanity; executed.
* **Albert Speer** (1905–1981): Minister of Armaments, pleaded guilty to crimes against humanity; sentenced to 20 years’ imprisonment.

These trials established precedents for individual criminal responsibility and codified definitions of war crimes and crimes against humanity.

---

## Eichmann Trial (1961)

* **Adolf Eichmann** (1906–1962): A principal organizer of the Holocaust, abducted from Argentina and tried by Israel’s **District Court of Jerusalem**. Convicted of crimes against humanity and war crimes; executed in 1962, the only Israeli civilian ever executed.

---

## International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) (1993–2017)

Established by UN Security Council Resolution 827 (1993) to address atrocities in the Balkans wars. Notable convictions include:

* **Radislav Krstić** (born 1948): Bosnian Serb general, convicted in 2001 of aiding genocide at Srebrenica; sentenced to 46 years (later reduced to 35).
* **Radovan Karadžić** (born 1945): President of the Republika Srpska, convicted in 2016 of genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity; life sentence.
* **Ratko Mladić** (born 1943): Commander of the VRS, convicted in 2017 of genocide and crimes against humanity; life sentence.
* **Slobodan Milošević** (1941–2006): Former President of Yugoslavia and Serbia, indicted for crimes in Croatia, Bosnia, and Kosovo; died in custody before trial concluded.

The ICTY’s jurisprudence clarified legal definitions of genocide, ethnic cleansing, and command responsibility.

---

## International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda (ICTR) (1994–2015)

Created by UN Security Council Resolution 955 (1994) to prosecute those responsible for the Rwandan genocide. Key convictions:

* **Jean Kambanda** (born 1955): Prime Minister of interim government during the genocide, pleaded guilty to genocide and crimes against humanity; sentenced to life imprisonment in 1998.
* **Théoneste Bagosora** (born 1941): Colonel in the Rwandan Army, convicted in 2008 for genocide planning; sentenced to life, later reduced to 35 years.
* **Édouard Karemera** (1951–2020): Minister of the interior, convicted in 2011 of genocide and crimes against humanity; life sentence.

The ICTR established the legal standard that political leaders and military officers could be held criminally responsible for orchestrating genocide.

---

## Special Court for Sierra Leone (SCSL) (2002–2013)

A hybrid tribunal established by the UN and Sierra Leone to address war crimes during the civil war (1991–2002). Notable convictions:

* **Charles Taylor** (born 1948): Former President of Liberia, convicted in 2012 of aiding and abetting war crimes and crimes against humanity; sentenced to 50 years.
* **Issa Hassan Sesay** (born 1970): Revolutionary United Front (RUF) commander, convicted in 2009 of war crimes and crimes against humanity; sentenced to 52 years.
* **Morris Kallon** (born 1971): RUF field commander, convicted in 2009; sentenced to 40 years.

The SCSL was the first to convict a former head of state (Taylor) for aiding rebel-era atrocities in another country.

---

## Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (ECCC) (2006–present)

A Cambodian–UN tribunal to prosecute senior Khmer Rouge leaders for Democratic Kampuchea’s crimes (1975–1979). Convictions include:

* **Kaing Guek Eav (“Duch”)** (born 1942): Commander of the Tuol Sleng (S-21) prison camp, convicted in 2010 of crimes against humanity and grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions; sentenced to life in 2012.
* **Nuon Chea** (1926–2019) and **Khieu Samphan** (born 1931): Senior leaders, convicted in 2018 of genocide and crimes against humanity; life sentences.

The ECCC’s hybrid structure combined international and Cambodian law, addressing challenges of domestic legal capacity post-genocide.

---

## International Criminal Court (ICC) Convictions (2002–present)

The **ICC**, established by the Rome Statute (1998), prosecutes individuals for genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes. Convictions to date include:

* **Thomas Lubanga Dyilo** (DRC, born 1960): Convicted in 2012 of conscripting child soldiers; sentenced to 14 years.
* **Germain Katanga** (DRC, born 1978): Convicted in 2014 of war crimes and crimes against humanity; sentenced to 12 years.
* **Jean-Pierre Bemba** (DRC, born 1962): Initially convicted in 2016 of war crimes and crimes against humanity for acts in Central African Republic, conviction overturned on appeal in 2018.
* **Ahmad al Faqi al Mahdi** (Mali, born 1975): Convicted in 2016 of war crimes for destruction of cultural property in Timbuktu; sentenced to 9 years.
* **Dominic Ongwen** (Uganda, born c. 1975): Convicted in 2021 of crimes against humanity and war crimes as a Lord’s Resistance Army commander; sentenced to 25 years.

The ICC’s decisions have advanced international criminal law on child soldiers, gender-based crimes, and cultural destruction.

---

## Political and Historical Significance

Convictions of war criminals reflect shifting norms of international justice and state accountability:

* **Post–World War II Precedents:** Nuremberg established that individuals, including heads of state, could be prosecuted for state-sanctioned atrocities.
* **Cold War Constraints:** Early tribunals were limited by superpower politics; many atrocities in Vietnam and Afghanistan went unprosecuted at the time.
* **Post–Cold War Expansion:** The 1990s saw a surge in ad hoc tribunals (ICTY, ICTR) as UN mandates enabled prosecutions in Europe and Africa.
* **Permanent Jurisdiction:** The ICC represents the institutionalization of permanent international criminal justice, albeit constrained by state ratifications and political will.

These prosecutions underscore the principle that grave violations of humanitarian law invite individual criminal liability, shaping contemporary debates on intervention, sovereignty, and global accountability.
