**Republika Srpska (1992–1995)**

**Overview**
Republika Srpska (Serbian Cyrillic: Република Српска, “Serb Republic”) was the self-proclaimed Serb political entity and de facto state within Bosnia and Herzegovina during the Bosnian War (1992–1995). Established on 9 January 1992 by Bosnian Serb leaders opposed to Bosnian independence from Yugoslavia, it controlled roughly 70 percent of Bosnia’s territory at the height of the conflict. Republika Srpska maintained its own government institutions, army, and police, and pursued policies of ethnic consolidation that drew international condemnation and led to war crimes indictments.

**Historical Context and Formation**
In the collapse of communist Yugoslavia, rising nationalist sentiment among Bosnia and Herzegovina’s three principal ethnic groups—Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims), Croats, and Serbs—led to competing visions for the republic’s future. Bosnian Serb leaders, under the umbrella of the Serbian Democratic Party (SDS) and guided by Radovan Karadžić and military commander Ratko Mladić, rejected the 1 March 1992 independence referendum called by the Bosnian government in Sarajevo. On 9 January 1992, the Bosnian Serb assembly in Pale issued the “Declaration of the Republic of the Serb People of Bosnia and Herzegovina,” later renamed Republika Srpska, asserting the right to remain within a “common state” with Serbia and Montenegro.

**Territory and Demographics**
Through a combination of military advances, sieges, and forced displacement—often labeled “ethnic cleansing”—Republika Srpska forces carved contiguous Serb-controlled areas across northern, eastern, and parts of central and southern Bosnia. By war’s end, its territory totaled approximately 49 percent of Bosnia’s land surface. Before the conflict, Serbs made up about 31 percent of Bosnia’s population, concentrated in eastern and northern regions; wartime demographic policies, including expulsions of non-Serb civilians, significantly increased the Serb proportion in Republika Srpska.

**Government and Institutions**
Republika Srpska’s civilian government was headquartered in Pale and modeled on a presidential-parliamentary system. Radovan Karadžić served as its first president, while a National Assembly (Narodna skupština) adopted laws under the wartime constitution. Parallel administrative bodies oversaw health, education, and economic planning. The army of Republika Srpska (Vojska Republike Srpske, VRS) operated under the direct command of General Mladić, with separate ministries for defense, interior affairs (MUP), and intelligence (SDB). Despite formal structures, real power often resided with SDS leadership and military commanders.

**Military Campaigns and Humanitarian Impact**
From spring 1992, VRS forces besieged Sarajevo for nearly four years, subjecting the city to relentless artillery bombardment and sniper fire. Military operations also targeted towns such as Srebrenica, Prijedor, and Foča, where mass killings, detention camps, and systematic rape occurred. The July 1995 Srebrenica massacre—during which over 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were executed—became the conflict’s most egregious atrocity and was later recognized by international tribunals as genocide. The UN and Western governments imposed limited sanctions and deployed UNPROFOR peacekeepers, but efforts to halt violence were repeatedly frustrated by VRS control of supply lines and refusal to honor ceasefires.

**International Status and Diplomacy**
No government formally recognized Republika Srpska’s sovereignty. Its leaders maintained close ties with the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (Serbia and Montenegro), which provided political, military, and economic support despite UN embargoes. International mediators—led by UN Special Envoy Cyrus Vance and EU envoy Lord Owen—proposed partition plans that reflected Republika Srpska’s wartime acquisitions but were rejected or only partially implemented. Dayton peace talks ultimately centered on preserving Bosnia and Herzegovina as a single state while accommodating Republika Srpska’s territorial gains.

**Dayton Peace Agreement and Transformation**
The General Framework Agreement for Peace in Bosnia and Herzegovina, reached at Dayton, Ohio, in November 1995, recognized Republika Srpska as one of two constituent entities of a single sovereign state, the other being the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (shared by Bosniaks and Croats). Under the agreement, Republika Srpska retained its assembly, president, and executive council, but ceded foreign affairs, defense, customs, and fiscal policy to the central institutions of Bosnia and Herzegovina. International overseers—the Office of the High Representative and NATO’s Implementation Force (IFOR)—monitored compliance, including the return of refugees and reconstruction.

**Legal Accountability and Legacy**
Key figures of Republika Srpska’s wartime leadership were indicted by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide. In 2016, Radovan Karadžić was sentenced to life imprisonment; Ratko Mladić received the same sentence in 2017. The legacy of Republika Srpska remains contested: while its institutions continue to function within Bosnia and Herzegovina’s decentralized system, debates over war memory, refugee return, and inter-entity relations underscore ongoing challenges to reconciliation. Republika Srpska’s wartime experience profoundly shaped Bosnia’s post-Dayton politics, entrenching ethnic divisions even as it offered a framework for shared sovereignty and power-sharing in a multiethnic state.
