**George Eliava Institute** (Georgian: გეორგი ელიოვის ინსტიტუტი) is a leading research center for bacteriophage biology and phage therapy in Tbilisi, Georgia. Founded in the early Soviet period, the Institute has played a central role in the study of bacteriophages—viruses that infect bacteria—and developed therapeutic applications that persisted through political upheavals, from Stalin’s purges to the collapse of the USSR and into the post-Soviet era.

**Founding and Early Years**
The Institute was formally established in 1923 as the Tbilisi Institute of Microbiology, Epidemiology and Bacteriophage under the auspices of the People’s Commissariat for Health of the Georgian Soviet Socialist Republic. Its creation reflected a wider Soviet emphasis on public‐health science and disease control following the devastation of World War I and the Russian Civil War. In 1930, the Institute was renamed in honor of its founder, Professor George Eliava, a Georgian microbiologist who had trained in Paris and collaborated closely with French‐Canadian scientist Félix d’Herelle, co-discoverer of bacteriophages. Together, Eliava and d’Herelle envisioned phage therapy as a revolutionary alternative to the emerging yet still limited antibiotics.

**Stalinist Era and Institutional Growth**
During the 1930s, the Eliava Institute benefited from significant state support as Moscow sought indigenous solutions to recurrent epidemics. Laboratories expanded, phage banks were established, and pilot clinics opened in Tbilisi and Batumi. However, the Institute’s founder fell victim to Stalin’s Great Purge: arrested in 1937 and executed on charges of “bourgeois nationalism,” Eliava’s death cast a pall over the Institute. Nevertheless, under new leadership, the Institute was able to distance itself from its victimized founder, reasserting its scientific mission and securing funding for phage‐production facilities during World War II, when antibiotic shortages made bacteriophages a vital tool against dysentery and wound infections among Soviet troops.

**Cold War Consolidation and Scientific Distinction**
In the postwar period, as the USSR and its satellite states embraced penicillin and sulfonamides, the Eliava Institute carved out a unique niche by persisting with phage research and therapy. The Institute developed standardized phage cocktails against *Staphylococcus*, *Shigella*, *Salmonella* and other pathogens, distributing them through a network of Soviet clinics. Its work garnered attention in Eastern Bloc countries and, to a limited extent, among sympathetic Western scientists during the 1960s. The Eliava Institute also trained bacteriologists from across the Soviet Union and allied nations, reinforcing Tbilisi’s reputation as a center of excellence in microbial therapeutics.

**Impact of Soviet Collapse and Georgian Independence**
The dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991 precipitated a sharp decline in state funding and a breakdown of distribution channels. Facilities fell into disrepair, and many researchers emigrated or shifted to other fields. Nevertheless, the Institute remained open under the newly independent Georgian government, buoyed by its historic stockpile of well‐characterized phages and a modest flow of international humanitarian grants. Political instability and economic crisis in the 1990s limited large‐scale production, but small‐batch phage preparations continued to be supplied to local hospitals, preserving institutional knowledge through lean decades.

**Modern Revival and Global Collaborations**
Since the early 2000s, growing global concern over antibiotic resistance has sparked renewed interest in phage therapy. The George Eliava Institute has leveraged its unrivaled phage library and specialist expertise to forge partnerships with European, American, and Asian research centers. Joint projects have included clinical trials for multidrug‐resistant *Pseudomonas* infections, characterization of novel phages via genomic sequencing, and development of phage‐based diagnostic kits. Georgian legislation enacted in 2016 formally recognized phage therapy as a medical option, allowing the Institute to expand pilot treatment programs and attract private investment in biomanufacturing facilities.

**Governance and Political Significance**
The Institute operates under Georgia’s Ministry of Health, Labour and Social Affairs, with a supervisory board that includes representatives from academia, the pharmaceutical sector, and patient‐advocacy groups. Its survival and resurgence have been cited by successive governments as a symbol of Georgia’s scientific resilience and international engagement. In parliamentary debates over healthcare reform and biotech regulation, the Eliava Institute often features as a case study in aligning national research heritage with market‐driven innovation.

**Legacy and Continuing Influence**
With nearly a century of uninterrupted phage research—save for the disruption of the late Stalinist purges—the George Eliava Institute stands as one of the world’s oldest and most comprehensive centers for bacteriophage science. Its legacy encompasses not only pioneering publications and phage catalogs, but also a model of how a specialized research institution can navigate ideological shifts, economic collapse, and renewed political will. Today, the Institute contributes to the global quest for alternatives to antibiotics and represents a distinct thread in the intertwined history of Soviet science, Georgian statehood, and international biomedical collaboration.
