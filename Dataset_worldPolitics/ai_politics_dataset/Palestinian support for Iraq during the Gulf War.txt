**Palestinian Support for Iraq During the Gulf War**

**Palestinian support for Iraq during the Gulf War** refers to the political alignment and expressions of solidarity by segments of the Palestinian leadership and population toward Iraq, particularly under President Saddam Hussein, during the 1990–1991 Gulf War. This support, shaped by regional dynamics and longstanding grievances against Western powers and Israel, had significant political and diplomatic consequences for the Palestinian national movement, especially in relation to the Arab world and the peace process with Israel.

### Background

The Gulf War erupted following Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait on August 2, 1990. The international community, led by the United States, condemned the occupation and formed a military coalition to expel Iraqi forces from Kuwait. The war culminated in **Operation Desert Storm**, launched in January 1991, which swiftly led to Iraq’s defeat and withdrawal from Kuwait.

At the time, the Palestinian national movement was primarily represented by the **Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO)** under the leadership of Yasser Arafat. The PLO had recently gained international legitimacy through its participation in diplomacy following the First Intifada and its engagement with the United States and other actors in the late 1980s.

### Expressions of Support

During the conflict, the PLO and a significant portion of the Palestinian public expressed political support for Iraq. Yasser Arafat refrained from condemning Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait and instead emphasized Arab unity and opposition to Western intervention. Arafat met with Saddam Hussein in Baghdad and made public statements that were widely interpreted as sympathetic to Iraq’s position.

The motivations for this stance included:

* **Pan-Arab sentiment**: Iraq was perceived by many Palestinians as a leading Arab power resisting Western domination and defending Arab interests.
* **Anti-Western sentiment**: The U.S.-led coalition was seen as biased, especially in light of perceived American support for Israel and lack of progress on Palestinian national rights.
* **Strategic calculations**: Arafat and the PLO hoped that aligning with Iraq might yield political benefits, particularly if Saddam Hussein emerged stronger in the Arab world.

Among ordinary Palestinians, particularly in the West Bank and Gaza Strip, pro-Iraq demonstrations and public expressions of solidarity were common. Iraqi promises to link the liberation of Kuwait to the resolution of the Palestinian issue further fueled this support. Saddam Hussein’s rhetoric often portrayed Iraq as a defender of the Palestinian cause, and he launched Scud missiles at Israel during the war, which, though militarily ineffective, were symbolically significant to some Palestinians.

### Regional and International Backlash

The PLO’s perceived alignment with Iraq had severe consequences. It caused a major rupture in relations with the **Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC)** states, especially **Kuwait** and **Saudi Arabia**, which had traditionally been significant financial and political supporters of the Palestinian cause.

In **Kuwait**, where over 400,000 Palestinians lived before the war, the government viewed the PLO’s stance as a betrayal. Following the liberation of Kuwait in early 1991, the new Kuwaiti leadership expelled most of the Palestinian population, accusing them of collaboration with the Iraqi occupation. This led to a dramatic demographic and economic displacement of Palestinians from the Gulf.

Saudi Arabia and other Gulf states also reduced their financial support to the PLO, severely impacting the organization’s operations. The broader Arab world grew divided, with many governments distancing themselves from Arafat’s leadership. The diplomatic setback contributed to a period of political and financial isolation for the Palestinian national movement.

### Impact on the Peace Process

The political cost of supporting Iraq indirectly influenced the PLO’s subsequent decision-making. Isolated and weakened after the war, the PLO leadership became more receptive to engaging in the U.S.-sponsored peace process. This shift culminated in the **Madrid Conference of 1991**, which marked the beginning of direct Israeli–Palestinian negotiations.

By 1993, the PLO entered into the **Oslo Accords** with Israel, a process that some analysts argue was shaped by the PLO’s need to re-legitimize itself internationally and regain financial and diplomatic support.

### Domestic Ramifications

Within the Palestinian territories, the Gulf War and its aftermath had mixed effects. While some Palestinians continued to view Saddam Hussein as a champion of the Palestinian cause, others became more critical of the PLO’s political judgment. The decline in remittances from the Gulf, coupled with the economic impact of returning refugees, exacerbated hardships in Palestinian communities.

The alignment with Iraq also reinforced divisions among Palestinian factions. Islamist groups such as **Hamas**, which were gaining popularity during the First Intifada, adopted a more ambiguous stance during the war, avoiding overt support for Saddam while continuing to oppose Western intervention and Israel.

### Legacy

Palestinian support for Iraq during the Gulf War remains a controversial chapter in modern Middle Eastern history. While reflecting genuine frustration with Western policies and sympathy for pan-Arab causes, the PLO’s position led to a significant loss of Arab support and forced a strategic reorientation. The expulsion of Palestinians from Kuwait and the financial cutbacks from Gulf states had lasting repercussions on the Palestinian diaspora and the national movement.

The episode also served as a cautionary example of the complex interplay between regional alliances and national liberation movements, highlighting how broader geopolitical shifts can dramatically alter the course of local struggles.
