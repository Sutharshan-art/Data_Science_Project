**Aco Apolonio**

**Aco Apolonio** (11 November 1916 – 2001) was a Yugoslav and later Serbian lawyer and political figure who became the president of the self-proclaimed **Dubrovnik Republic** during the Croatian War of Independence. His brief and controversial role in this wartime entity placed him at the center of one of the lesser-known separatist initiatives in the Balkan conflicts of the 1990s.

---

### Early Life and Legal Career

Apolonio was born on 11 November 1916 in Kamenari, near Herceg Novi, then part of Austria-Hungary. He was of mixed heritage, with an Istrian Italian father and a mother from Dubrovnik. After completing his legal education, Apolonio moved to Dubrovnik in 1941, where he became active in the Yugoslav legal system. He rose to the position of public prosecutor in Dubrovnik during the era of Socialist Yugoslavia, becoming a respected member of the local legal and civic community.

Throughout the 1950s and 1960s, Apolonio was a well-known figure in Dubrovnik, participating in cultural and political life under the one-party rule of the League of Communists of Yugoslavia. He was regarded as a professional committed to the Yugoslav socialist framework, although his political views during this period remained largely within the established norms of the federal system.

---

### Political Context of the Early 1990s

The dissolution of Yugoslavia in the early 1990s led to a series of violent conflicts, including the Croatian War of Independence. Following Croatia’s declaration of independence in 1991, the Yugoslav People’s Army (JNA), with strong backing from Serbian political and military leadership, launched offensives against Croatian-held territories. One of the targets was the southern Dalmatian region, including the historic city of Dubrovnik.

In the autumn of 1991, JNA forces began a siege of Dubrovnik, bombarding the city and surrounding areas. As they advanced, the military and pro-Serb political actors sought to establish control over areas they occupied. It was in this context that the idea of creating a local authority aligned with the JNA and Serbia emerged in the Dubrovnik area.

---

### The Dubrovnik Republic

On 15 October 1991, following the JNA’s occupation of Cavtat and surrounding villages in the Konavle region, Aco Apolonio proclaimed the formation of the **Dubrovnik Republic**, a short-lived political entity aligned with Yugoslav and Serbian interests. Apolonio was installed as its president, and the entity claimed to act as a civil administration for the occupied territories south of Dubrovnik.

The Dubrovnik Republic was not internationally recognized and was viewed by the Croatian government as a tool of occupation. While it purported to protect the interests of local residents, its formation was widely seen as part of broader efforts by the JNA and Serb leadership to create a series of pro-Serb or pro-Yugoslav mini-states within Croatia—similar to entities in Eastern Slavonia and the Krajina region.

Apolonio remained president of the republic until May 1992, when the JNA withdrew from the region as part of broader military and diplomatic shifts. With their withdrawal, Croatian forces reestablished control, and the Dubrovnik Republic effectively ceased to exist.

---

### Legal Proceedings and Exile

Following the war, Croatian authorities charged Aco Apolonio with treason and collaboration, holding him responsible for his leadership role in an entity that facilitated military occupation and undermined Croatian sovereignty. He was tried in absentia and sentenced to 12 years in prison.

By the time of the trial, Apolonio had fled to what remained of Yugoslavia—specifically, Serbia—where he lived in relative obscurity until his death in 2001. Despite his conviction, he never served time in prison, as he remained outside Croatian jurisdiction.

---

### Political and Historical Assessment

Apolonio's brief presidency of the Dubrovnik Republic is viewed as emblematic of the local political fragmentation and ethnic tensions that accompanied the Yugoslav wars. His transition from a Yugoslav prosecutor to the leader of a Serb-aligned separatist entity reflects the broader ideological and national shifts that occurred across the former Yugoslavia during the early 1990s.

Supporters of the Dubrovnik Republic at the time claimed it was a defensive measure to protect Serb interests in the region and to maintain a Yugoslav identity in the face of Croatian independence. Critics argue that the entity, and Apolonio’s leadership, provided a civilian cover for military occupation and ethnic realignment.

The creation and dissolution of the Dubrovnik Republic, along with the fate of its leadership, have been studied by historians as part of a pattern of wartime governance efforts designed to legitimize territorial control through quasi-political means.

---

### Legacy

Aco Apolonio remains a polarizing figure. In post-war Croatia, he is largely remembered as a collaborator with occupying forces. In Serbia and among some former Yugoslav leftists and nationalists, his actions are interpreted within a narrative of preserving the unity of Yugoslavia or defending threatened communities.

The Dubrovnik Republic, though ephemeral, continues to serve as a cautionary example of how legal and political tools were used to support wartime occupation and how local figures, such as Apolonio, became instrumental in advancing broader nationalist agendas during one of Europe’s most turbulent post-Cold War conflicts.
