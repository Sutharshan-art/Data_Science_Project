**Serbian Nationalism**

**Serbian nationalism** refers to the political ideology and cultural movement that promotes the unity, identity, and interests of the Serbian people. It has historically emphasized the preservation of Serbian ethnic, religious, and cultural identity, often advocating for the political and territorial unification of Serbs within a common national framework. Serbian nationalism has evolved through various historical epochs, from its roots in the struggle against Ottoman rule to its role in the Yugoslav Wars and contemporary Balkan politics.

### Historical Origins

Serbian nationalism began to emerge in the late 18th and early 19th centuries during the decline of the Ottoman Empire. Influenced by Romantic nationalism and Enlightenment ideals spreading across Europe, Serbian intellectuals and clergy promoted national awakening through the revival of Serbian history, folklore, and Orthodox Christianity. The First Serbian Uprising (1804–1813) and the Second Serbian Uprising (1815) marked the beginning of Serbia’s path toward autonomy and eventual independence, with national consciousness deeply tied to liberation from Ottoman domination.

Key figures such as Vuk Karadžić, a language reformer and folklorist, played a central role in codifying the Serbian language and shaping national identity. The Kosovo Myth, centered on the 1389 Battle of Kosovo, became a foundational element of Serbian national memory, symbolizing sacrifice and resistance.

### 19th and Early 20th Century

Following the recognition of Serbia as an independent state at the Congress of Berlin in 1878, Serbian nationalism increasingly took a political form, with aspirations to unite Serb-inhabited territories in the Balkans. The ideology of **Greater Serbia** emerged, advocating the expansion of Serbia’s borders to include all ethnic Serbs, particularly those in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, and parts of Croatia.

This vision influenced Serbia’s foreign policy and military campaigns during the Balkan Wars (1912–1913) and contributed to tensions with the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in 1914 by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb associated with the nationalist group Young Bosnia, played a pivotal role in triggering World War I.

After the war, the formation of the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes (later Yugoslavia) in 1918 marked a shift in Serbian nationalism toward a Yugoslav framework. However, tensions persisted over the perceived dominance of Serbs in the new state’s political and military institutions, leading to ethnic grievances among Croats, Slovenes, and others.

### World War II and Post-War Yugoslavia

During World War II, Serbian nationalism manifested in complex and divergent ways. The royalist **Chetnik** movement, led by Draža Mihailović, promoted monarchist and Serbian nationalist goals while opposing both the Axis occupiers and the communist Partisans. Chetnik actions included collaboration with Axis forces and atrocities against non-Serb populations in certain regions.

In post-war socialist Yugoslavia, the communist regime under Josip Broz Tito suppressed overt nationalist expressions in favor of a federal, multiethnic Yugoslav identity. Serbian nationalism was officially discouraged, though underlying tensions persisted. Some Serbian intellectuals, particularly in the 1980s, began to voice concerns about the status of Serbs in other republics, especially in Kosovo, where the Albanian majority's demands for greater autonomy raised fears of marginalization among the Serbian population.

### Late 20th Century and Yugoslav Dissolution

The resurgence of Serbian nationalism became a central force in the breakup of Yugoslavia in the 1990s. Under the leadership of Slobodan Milošević, nationalism was used to consolidate power and mobilize support. Milošević’s rhetoric emphasized the protection of Serbs in other republics and sought to redefine Yugoslavia in ways that favored Serbian interests.

This period witnessed the rise of nationalist political parties, the promotion of ethnic solidarity, and efforts to create a unified Serbian space across former Yugoslav republics. Serbian nationalism was a driving factor in the wars in Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and later Kosovo. Nationalist paramilitary groups, such as those led by Arkan and others, were accused of war crimes and ethnic cleansing campaigns.

The concept of **Greater Serbia**, though officially denied by the Serbian government, was widely associated with wartime objectives and played a role in regional and international perceptions of Serbian policy.

### Post-2000 Developments

Following the fall of Milošević in 2000, Serbian nationalism underwent transformation. While radical nationalist elements persisted, the political mainstream gradually shifted toward pro-European integration and regional reconciliation. However, nationalist rhetoric remains a feature of certain political parties and movements, especially regarding Kosovo, which declared independence in 2008—a move not recognized by Serbia.

Parties such as the Serbian Radical Party and, to a lesser extent, the Serbian Progressive Party have invoked nationalist themes, though the latter has also pursued EU accession. Issues such as the status of Kosovo, war crimes prosecutions, and minority rights continue to elicit nationalist sentiments within Serbian society.

### Contemporary Significance

Today, Serbian nationalism exists in multiple forms—from moderate cultural pride and historical commemoration to more extreme political ideologies advocating territorial claims or ethnic exclusivity. It remains an influential force in Serbia’s political discourse, especially during periods of national or regional tension.

Serbian nationalism also plays a role in diaspora communities and among ethnic Serbs in neighboring countries. Its future trajectory is shaped by domestic political developments, international relations, and broader Balkan stability.
