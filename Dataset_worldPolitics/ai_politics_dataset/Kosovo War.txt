**Kosovo War** (February 1998 – June 1999) was an armed conflict in the Serbian province of Kosovo between the forces of the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (FRY)—composed of Serbia and Montenegro—and the ethnic Albanian Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA), later joined by NATO forces. It centered on the political and territorial status of Kosovo and ended with the withdrawal of Yugoslav forces, the establishment of a UN administration, and the deployment of a NATO-led peacekeeping mission.

**Background**
Kosovo, a province of Serbia with a majority ethnic Albanian population, had enjoyed substantial autonomy under the 1974 Yugoslav constitution. In 1989–90, Slobodan Milošević’s government rescinded this autonomy, imposed direct Serbian control, and cracked down on Kosovo’s Albanian political and cultural institutions. The repression gave rise to non-violent resistance under the leadership of Ibrahim Rugova’s Democratic League of Kosovo (LDK). From 1996 onwards, the more militant KLA emerged, arguing that armed struggle was necessary to secure Kosovo’s independence.

**Escalation of Conflict**
Hostilities began in early 1998 as clashes between KLA guerrillas and Serbian police and paramilitary units intensified. The KLA launched attacks on border posts and outposts, prompting large-scale counter-insurgency operations known as “Operation Horseshoe” by Yugoslav authorities. Reports of civilian casualties and the destruction of Albanian villages fueled international concern and created waves of internal displacement.

**Humanitarian and Diplomatic Efforts**
By mid-1998, over 200,000 Kosovars had fled into neighboring Albania and Macedonia, while hundreds of thousands were internally displaced. The United Nations and the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) attempted mediation. In October 1998, under growing Western pressure, Milošević agreed to an armistice and the deployment of an OSCE Verification Mission (OSCE-V). However, ceasefire violations by both sides persisted into early 1999.

**Breakdown and NATO Intervention**
In March 1999, following the failure of peace talks at Rambouillet and Paris—where the FRY refused NATO’s terms for an interim international presence—NATO commenced Operation Allied Force on 24 March 1999. Over the next 78 days, NATO aircraft conducted sustained air strikes against Yugoslav military and infrastructure targets, aiming to compel Belgrade to withdraw security forces from Kosovo and accept a peace agreement.

**Yugoslav Counter-Measures and Refugee Crisis**
Yugoslav and Serbian forces responded by accelerating expulsions of ethnic Albanians—between 800,000 and 1 million Kosovars were driven across the border—creating one of Europe’s most acute refugee crises since World War II. Serb paramilitary units were implicated in massacres (notably in Račak) that later informed international war-crimes indictments. Despite NATO bombing, Yugoslav ground forces remained largely intact until the air campaign’s political pressure led to negotiations.

**Resolution and Aftermath**
On 9 June 1999, Milošević agreed to the terms of UN Security Council Resolution 1244, which mandated the withdrawal of all Yugoslav forces and the introduction of an international security presence. By mid-July, NATO’s Kosovo Force (KFOR) had deployed some 50,000 troops from 39 countries. Concurrently, the United Nations Interim Administration Mission in Kosovo (UNMIK) began governing the province, overseeing civilian administration, institution building, and the return of refugees.

**Political and Legal Consequences**
Resolution 1244 affirmed Yugoslavia’s sovereignty over Kosovo but placed it under UN administration pending a final status. In 2008, Kosovo unilaterally declared independence. Serbia and several UN member states have not recognized this declaration, and the question of Kosovo’s status remains a central issue in Balkan and European diplomacy. The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) indicted and later convicted senior Serbian and KLA figures for war crimes and crimes against humanity.

**Significance and Legacy**
The Kosovo War was the first major NATO military action conducted without a UN mandate, setting a precedent for humanitarian intervention. It underscored the controversial doctrine of “Responsibility to Protect” and raised questions about the legality of air campaigns absent Security Council authorization. The conflict accelerated the dissolution of the FRY and influenced subsequent NATO engagement in the Balkans, notably in Bosnia and later in Afghanistan. Kosovo’s unresolved status continues to shape regional stability, EU accession processes, and international debates over sovereignty, self-determination, and humanitarian intervention.
