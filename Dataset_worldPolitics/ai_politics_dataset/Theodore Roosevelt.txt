Theodore Roosevelt

**Early Life and Career**
Theodore Roosevelt Jr. (1858–1919) was born on October 27, 1858, into a wealthy family in New York City. Educated at Harvard College (A.B., 1880), he entered public service soon after, serving in the New York State Assembly (1882–1884) as a Republican reformer opposed to party machine politics. After a brief hiatus following his wife’s and mother’s deaths in 1884, Roosevelt returned to public life as New York City police commissioner (1895–1897), where he implemented professional standards, modernized the force, and cracked down on corruption.

**Expansion of National Profile**
In April 1897, President William McKinley appointed Roosevelt Assistant Secretary of the Navy. Advocating a strong naval posture, Roosevelt ordered fleet preparations in anticipation of conflict with Spain. With the outbreak of the Spanish–American War in April 1898, he resigned to form and lead the 1st U.S. Volunteer Cavalry—known as the “Rough Riders”—achieving fame for his charge up San Juan Hill in Cuba. His wartime heroism propelled him to the governorship of New York (1899–1900), where he continued progressive reforms against political patronage.

**Vice Presidency and Presidential Succession**
Selected as McKinley’s running mate in 1900 to balance the ticket, Roosevelt was elected Vice President and inaugurated in March 1901. Following McKinley’s assassination by an anarchist in September 1901, Roosevelt became the 26th President of the United States at age 42, the youngest to assume the office.

**Domestic Policy and the Square Deal**
Roosevelt’s presidency (1901–1909) defined the early Progressive Era, emphasizing federal intervention to promote social justice, economic opportunity, and public welfare. His “Square Deal” rested on three pillars:

* **Trust Regulation:** Roosevelt distinguished between “good” and “bad” trusts, initiating antitrust actions under the Sherman Antitrust Act. His administration filed suit against Northern Securities Company (1902) and secured dissolution of the railroad monopoly, setting a precedent for federal oversight.
* **Consumer Protection:** In response to Upton Sinclair’s *The Jungle* (1906), Roosevelt championed the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act (both 1906), establishing the Food and Drug Administration’s regulatory basis.
* **Conservation:** A pioneering conservationist, Roosevelt created the U.S. Forest Service (1905) under Gifford Pinchot, established five national parks, 18 national monuments, and 150 national forests, safeguarding approximately 230 million acres of public land.

**Political Context and Progressive Alliances**
Roosevelt’s reforms reflected broader Progressive Era currents reacting against Gilded Age excesses: corporate monopolies, urban poverty, and political corruption. He courted alliances with urban reformers, labor leaders, and middle‐class progressives, though he resisted radical proposals such as nationalization of industry. His interventions in labor disputes—most notably the 1902 anthracite coal strike—demonstrated willingness to use federal power to mediate between business and labor.

**Foreign Policy and Big Stick Diplomacy**
Roosevelt redefined the presidency’s international role, advocating an activist foreign policy summarized by his motto, “Speak softly and carry a big stick.” Key initiatives included:

* **Panama Canal:** Orchestrated U.S. support for Panamanian independence from Colombia in 1903 to secure canal‐zone rights. Construction began in 1904 and was completed in 1914, transforming maritime trade.
* **Roosevelt Corollary:** Added to the Monroe Doctrine in 1904, asserting U.S. right to intervene in Latin America to stabilize economies and forestall European intervention. This policy underpinned military interventions in the Caribbean and Central America.
* **Russo-Japanese Peace Mediation:** Roosevelt brokered the Treaty of Portsmouth (1905), ending the Russo-Japanese War and earning the Nobel Peace Prize in 1906—the first American to receive it.

**1912 Bull Moose Campaign**
After leaving office in 1909, Roosevelt grew disillusioned with his successor, William Howard Taft, over tariff policy and conservation. In 1912, Roosevelt challenged Taft for the Republican nomination, lost, and founded the Progressive (“Bull Moose”) Party. Running on a platform of direct democracy reforms—women’s suffrage, social insurance, stricter regulation of corporations—he split the Republican vote, enabling Democrat Woodrow Wilson’s victory.

**Later Life and Death**
Roosevelt campaigned in support of Wilson’s preparedness measures prior to U.S. entry into World War I. He prepared to lead an African American–inclusive volunteer division in France but was denied command by Wilson. In 1918, he embarked on a safari in East Africa. Returning to his home in Oyster Bay, New York, he died suddenly of a pulmonary embolism on January 6, 1919.

**Legacy**
Roosevelt’s presidency expanded the federal government’s regulatory and conservation roles, reshaping American politics. He left an enduring legacy as a symbol of vigorous executive leadership and Progressive reform. Monuments—such as the Theodore Roosevelt National Park and Mount Rushmore—honor his contributions. His brand of progressivism influenced later reform movements and established precedents for antitrust enforcement, environmental stewardship, and American global leadership.
