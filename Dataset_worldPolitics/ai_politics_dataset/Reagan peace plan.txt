**Reagan Peace Plan**

**Overview**
The Reagan Peace Plan was a United States–sponsored proposal to resolve the Arab–Israeli conflict, announced by President Ronald Reagan on September 1, 1982. Aimed principally at the Israeli occupation of territories captured in the 1967 Six-Day War, the plan outlined a framework for autonomy for the Palestinian inhabitants of the West Bank and Gaza Strip while preserving Israel’s security and territorial integrity. Although endorsed in principle by several Western and Arab states, it ultimately failed to secure agreement and was superseded by later diplomatic initiatives.

**Historical Context**
In June 1967, Israel’s swift victory over Egypt, Jordan, and Syria in the Six-Day War resulted in its occupation of the Sinai Peninsula, the Golan Heights, the West Bank (including East Jerusalem), and the Gaza Strip. Subsequent United Nations resolutions called for Israeli withdrawal in exchange for peace and recognition. After the 1978 Camp David Accords between Egypt and Israel, which led to Israel’s withdrawal from Sinai, the Palestinian question remained unresolved. By 1982, the Lebanese civil war and Israel’s invasion of southern Lebanon had further destabilized the region, prompting the Reagan administration to articulate a broader peace strategy.

**Key Principles of the Plan**

1. **Territorial and Security Arrangements**: The plan proposed that Israel retain control of East Jerusalem and key strategic positions in the West Bank “for an interim period,” with phased withdrawals tied to security guarantees. Demilitarized zones and international observers were to be deployed to prevent cross-border attacks.
2. **Establishment of Palestinian Autonomy**: A five-year transitional period was envisioned, during which local councils in the West Bank and Gaza would exercise self-government under an autonomous “Palestinian entity.” This body would manage internal affairs such as education, taxation, and municipal services, while foreign policy and security remained under Israeli and U.S. oversight.
3. **Negotiations on Final Status**: At the end of the interim period, negotiations would address permanent status issues—including the political status of Jerusalem, the fate of Israeli settlements, and the right of return for Palestinian refugees. All parties would be required to renounce threats and use of force as part of a binding peace treaty.
4. **Role of the International Community**: The United States, European allies, and Arab states would provide economic assistance to bolster Palestinian institutions and invest in regional development. A multinational peacekeeping force under United Nations auspices was to monitor the transition.

**Political Motivations**
President Reagan and key advisers viewed the plan as a means to stabilize the Middle East, secure oil flows vital to Western economies, and counter Soviet influence among radical Arab and Palestinian groups. The administration sought to balance its traditional support for Israel with growing recognition of Palestinian national aspirations. Domestically, Reagan aimed to demonstrate leadership in a complex international arena while maintaining strong ties to the U.S. Jewish community and congressional supporters of Israel.

**Reception and Diplomacy**
Initial Israeli reaction was cautious but non-rejectionist; Prime Minister Menachem Begin’s government insisted on retaining strategic control over the Jordan Valley and opposed any multinational force entering the West Bank and Gaza. Arab states publicly welcomed the emphasis on Palestinian self-government but criticized the plan’s conditional approach to sovereignty and the retention of “final status” issues. The Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO) was excluded from direct negotiations, leading Yasser Arafat to condemn the initiative as insufficient. European allies, including the United Kingdom and France, expressed tentative support but urged inclusion of all relevant parties, particularly the PLO.

**Implementation Challenges**
Negotiations stalled amid mutual distrust, ongoing violence, and political turnover. Israel’s continued settlement expansion complicated discussions over territorial arrangements. The PLO’s exclusion from talks undermined Palestinian confidence in any framework that did not recognize them as the primary representative. Regional crises—most notably the 1982 Lebanon War—diverted diplomatic attention and fueled further hostility. By the mid-1980s, the plan had lost momentum, eclipsed by the emergence of the 1987 First Intifada and shifting U.S. policy under Secretary of State George P. Shultz.

**Impact and Legacy**
While the Reagan Peace Plan did not result in a lasting agreement, it influenced subsequent U.S. diplomacy by reaffirming several enduring principles: the linkage of Israeli security guarantees to Palestinian self-rule, the concept of an interim transition leading to permanent status talks, and the engagement of broader international support. Elements of the plan resurfaced in the 1991 Madrid Conference, where Israel, neighboring Arab states, and Palestinian delegates (not formally representing the PLO) embarked on parallel negotiations. The principle of phased autonomy ultimately informed the Oslo Accords of 1993, which established the Palestinian Authority and set a five-year timetable for final status negotiations.

**Assessment**
Scholars and policymakers view the Reagan initiative as a product of its time: reflecting Cold War strategic imperatives, domestic political constraints, and the complexities of regional power dynamics. Critics argue that by postponing core issues—such as Jerusalem’s status and refugees’ rights—the plan deferred rather than resolved fundamental disputes. Supporters contend it represented a pragmatic effort to break diplomatic deadlock and laid groundwork for later breakthroughs. In retrospect, the Reagan Peace Plan stands as an early American attempt to reconcile competing priorities in the Arab–Israeli conflict through a structured, if ultimately unrealized, road map toward peace.
