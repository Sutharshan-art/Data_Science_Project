**Presidency of Ronald Reagan**

The **presidency of Ronald Reagan** began on January 20, 1981, and ended on January 20, 1989. Reagan, a Republican and former governor of California, served as the 40th president of the United States. His administration marked a major ideological shift in American politics, characterized by economic liberalization, tax reform, a renewed focus on military strength, and a more confrontational approach to the Soviet Union during the latter stages of the Cold War. Reagan’s presidency is often credited with reshaping the modern conservative movement and influencing global geopolitics during the 1980s.

---

### Election and Inauguration

Reagan won the **1980 presidential election** by defeating incumbent Democrat **Jimmy Carter**, amid widespread dissatisfaction over economic stagnation, inflation, the Iran hostage crisis, and perceived American decline. Running on a platform of smaller government, lower taxes, and national renewal, Reagan secured 489 electoral votes to Carter’s 49.

He was inaugurated on January 20, 1981, the same day that Iran released the American hostages. His presidency began with a clear mandate for conservative reform and a revitalization of American confidence.

---

### Economic Policy and “Reaganomics”

Reagan’s domestic agenda was dominated by economic policy, particularly the implementation of **supply-side economics**, later dubbed “**Reaganomics**.” His administration pursued significant **tax cuts**, beginning with the **Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981**, which lowered income tax rates across the board.

Reagan also sought to reduce government regulation and cut federal spending, particularly on social welfare programs. At the same time, defense spending increased dramatically. His policies were intended to stimulate investment, reduce inflation, and foster economic growth. The economy entered a deep recession in the early 1980s but rebounded by 1983, initiating a sustained period of growth.

Critics argued that Reaganomics increased income inequality and federal deficits, while supporters claimed it revitalized the private sector and restored confidence in the U.S. economy.

---

### Foreign Policy and the Cold War

Reagan adopted a hardline approach toward the **Soviet Union**, labeling it the “**Evil Empire**” and initiating a large military buildup. His administration advanced the **Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI)**—a missile defense system that critics labeled "Star Wars." Reagan’s foreign policy included support for anti-communist movements and governments worldwide, known as the **Reagan Doctrine**, with notable involvement in **Afghanistan**, **Nicaragua**, and **Angola**.

Despite his early confrontational stance, Reagan shifted toward diplomacy in his second term. He developed a working relationship with Soviet leader **Mikhail Gorbachev**, culminating in several high-profile summits. The most significant achievement came in 1987 with the signing of the **Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty**, the first treaty to eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons.

Reagan's foreign policy played a central role in the events that contributed to the **end of the Cold War**, though historians debate the degree of his impact relative to internal Soviet reform.

---

### Domestic Affairs and Social Issues

Reagan's presidency emphasized conservative values and a limited role for the federal government in domestic life. He appointed three justices to the **U.S. Supreme Court**, including **Sandra Day O’Connor**, the first woman to serve on the Court.

The administration’s approach to social issues included opposition to abortion rights, support for school prayer, and resistance to civil rights enforcement in some areas. His response to the **HIV/AIDS epidemic**, particularly in its early years, drew significant criticism for its perceived neglect and slow mobilization of federal resources.

Reagan also faced challenges in urban policy and education, with limited federal intervention. His “**War on Drugs**” significantly expanded law enforcement and incarceration but was later criticized for its disproportionate impact on minority communities.

---

### Iran–Contra Affair

A major scandal during Reagan’s second term was the **Iran–Contra affair**, which involved secret arms sales to Iran in violation of an embargo, with the proceeds funneled to support the **Contras**, a rebel group fighting the leftist Sandinista government in Nicaragua—despite a congressional ban on such funding.

The revelation in 1986 damaged Reagan’s credibility and led to multiple investigations. Several administration officials were indicted, though Reagan himself claimed he had no knowledge of the covert operations. While the scandal tainted his administration, Reagan’s personal popularity remained relatively high.

---

### 1984 Re-Election

Reagan was re-elected in a landslide in **1984**, defeating Democrat **Walter Mondale** with 525 electoral votes to 13, the largest margin in U.S. history. His campaign, emphasizing national optimism under the slogan “**It’s morning again in America**,” capitalized on economic recovery and his broad appeal.

---

### Legacy

Ronald Reagan left office in 1989 with high approval ratings and was succeeded by his vice president, **George H. W. Bush**. His presidency remains one of the most influential in modern American history. Supporters credit him with restoring American confidence, strengthening the economy, and playing a decisive role in ending the Cold War. Critics point to increased national debt, underinvestment in social services, and a mixed legacy on civil rights and public health.

Reagan’s impact on U.S. politics endured long after his presidency, shaping the ideological direction of the Republican Party and becoming a central figure in conservative identity in the 21st century.
