**Norman Stewart Hughson Lamont, Baron Lamont of Lerwick**

**Norman Lamont, Baron Lamont of Lerwick** (born 8 May 1942) is a British Conservative politician who served as Chancellor of the Exchequer from October 1990 to May 1993 under Prime Minister John Major. His tenure encompassed Britain’s dramatic exit from the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) on “Black Wednesday” (16 September 1992), a pivotal moment in modern UK economic policy. Lamont represented the constituency of Kingston upon Thames (and later Kingston and Surbiton) as Member of Parliament from 1972 until 1997 and again from 2001 to 2015.

## Early Life and Pre-Political Career

Born in Stirling, Scotland, Lamont was educated at Fettes College in Edinburgh and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he read History. After graduation, he worked as an articled clerk and later qualified as a chartered accountant, specialising in corporate finance. In the late 1960s, he joined the Conservative Research Department and served as special adviser to Chancellor Anthony Barber (1970–1971), gaining exposure to macroeconomic policymaking during the Heath government’s attempts to curb inflation.

## Parliamentary Career

Lamont first entered the House of Commons at a by-election in Kingston upon Thames on 22 June 1972, retaining the seat for the Conservatives until the 1997 general election. He was re-elected in the newly created constituency of Kingston and Surbiton in 2001 and served until his retirement in 2015. In opposition during the 1979–1990 Thatcher years, Lamont held frontbench roles including Shadow Chief Secretary to the Treasury (1987–1989) and Shadow Financial Secretary (1984–1987), forging his reputation as a fiscal conservative.

## Ministerial Offices

Under Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher, Lamont held junior Treasury posts: Financial Secretary to the Treasury (1989–1990) and Chief Secretary to the Treasury (1989), responsible for public expenditure control during a period of significant privatization and public-sector cuts. His management of spending reviews and advocacy for tighter fiscal discipline positioned him as a leading candidate for the chancellorship upon Thatcher’s departure.

## Chancellor of the Exchequer (1990–1993)

Upon John Major’s succession in October 1990, Lamont was appointed Chancellor. He inherited an economy facing recession, high interest rates, and sterling under speculative pressure. His early years saw him maintain the ERM membership, set by his predecessor, John Major, in October 1990 at a rate of DM 2.95 to £1. Lamont implemented modest tax cuts in the 1991 Budget, including a reduction in the top rate of income tax from 40% to 40% (an increase for higher earners was abandoned), and maintained Margaret Thatcher’s emphasis on controlling public expenditure.

### The ERM Crisis and Black Wednesday

By mid-1992, sterling came under intense speculative attack amid Germany’s sharply rising interest rates following reunification. Lamont’s government sought to defend the pound through interest-rate hikes (reaching 15%) and currency interventions. On 16 September 1992—“Black Wednesday”—the UK Treasury announced withdrawal from the ERM after these measures proved unsustainable. The pound was allowed to float, leading to a rapid fall in its value. Although politically damaging in the short term and undermining Conservative credibility on economic stewardship, the devaluation eventually boosted export competitiveness and is credited with contributing to the mid-1990s economic recovery.

### Post-ERM Policies

Following the ERM exit, Lamont shifted to a more flexible exchange-rate regime and gradually cut interest rates, helping to end the recession. He delivered successive tax cuts in the 1993 Budget, including a 1% reduction in the basic rate of income tax, and further reductions in employer National Insurance contributions. However, the political fallout from Black Wednesday and internal party criticism led to his dismissal at the 1993 Conservative Party conference, replaced by Kenneth Clarke.

## Later Parliamentary Career and Peerage

After losing his seat in the Labour landslide of 1997, Lamont returned to the backbenches in 2001 and served until declining to stand in 2015. He was created a life peer as Baron Lamont of Lerwick in October 2016. In the House of Lords, he has participated in debates on fiscal policy, banking regulation, and the European Union, maintaining his longstanding Eurosceptic views.

## Political Views and Legacy

A proponent of monetarist principles and low-tax conservatism, Lamont advocated for sound public finances, deregulation of financial markets, and a flexible exchange-rate system. His stewardship during the ERM crisis—despite its political cost—has been reappraised by economists and historians as laying the groundwork for the UK’s economic resilience in the late 1990s. Lamont’s career exemplifies the challenges of managing fiscal and monetary policy amid international capital flows and the tensions between political sovereignty and European integration.

## Personal Life

Lamont married Sarah-Jane Rayner in 1976; the couple has two daughters. He holds honorary doctorates from De Montfort University and the University of Stirling. A former member of the InterAction Council’s Commission on Global Financial Security, Lamont continues to comment on economic affairs in media and academic fora.
