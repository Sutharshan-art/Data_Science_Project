**History of the National Hockey League**

The National Hockey League (NHL) is a professional ice hockey league in North America, founded in 1917. Its evolution reflects not only the growth of a major sports enterprise but also broader social, economic, and political developments in Canada and the United States.

---

## Origins and Early Growth (1917–1942)

The NHL was formed on November 26, 1917, in Montreal by owners of the four surviving teams of the defunct National Hockey Association (NHA). Original franchises included the Montreal Canadiens, Montreal Wanderers, Ottawa Senators, and Toronto Arenas. The league’s early years coincided with World War I and the 1918 influenza pandemic, both of which disrupted play and strained resources.

Prohibition in the United States (1920–33) indirectly benefited the NHL by boosting investments in entertainment, including sports arenas. Between 1924 and 1926, the league expanded into U.S. markets—Boston (1924), New York (Rangers, 1926), Chicago (Black Hawks, 1926), and Detroit (Cougars, 1926)—seeking larger revenue bases. This expansion was part of a broader pattern of U.S.–Canada economic integration in the interwar period.

---

## Original Six Era (1942–1967)

By 1942, the NHL had contracted to six teams—the “Original Six”: Montreal Canadiens, Toronto Maple Leafs, Boston Bruins, Chicago Black Hawks, Detroit Red Wings, and New York Rangers. The limited membership fostered intense rivalries and stable revenue sharing, even during the economic strains of World War II. Government rationing and player enlistments reduced rosters, but hockey offered wartime morale and a sense of continuity.

Postwar prosperity in North America fueled higher attendance and the rise of television broadcasting. In Canada, hockey became a pillar of national identity, reinforced by CBC’s **Hockey Night in Canada** (first televised 1952). In the U.S., the NHL maintained niche status compared to baseball and football but laid groundwork for future growth.

---

## First Wave of Expansion and Rival Leagues (1967–1979)

Amid rising U.S. population mobility and civil-rights–era economic optimism, the NHL doubled in size in 1967, adding six new franchises in California, Pennsylvania, Missouri, and Minnesota. This **1967 Expansion** aimed to preempt antitrust challenges by broadening the league’s geographic footprint and securing lucrative television markets.

The World Hockey Association (WHA), founded in 1972, challenged the NHL’s monopoly by offering higher player salaries and signing European and Soviet talent. The resulting talent wars led to landmark antitrust litigation, spurring the NHL to recognize a players’ association and negotiate a **collective bargaining agreement** in 1975 that improved player mobility and compensation. In 1979 the leagues reconciled under an NHL–WHA merger, admitting four WHA teams (Edmonton, Hartford, Quebec, Winnipeg) and formalizing **unrestricted free agency** for veterans.

---

## Labor Relations and Governance (1967–1992)

The NHL Players’ Association (NHLPA), formed in 1967, evolved from a benefit society into a powerful union. Under successive leaders—Alan Eagleson and later Bob Goodenow—the NHLPA secured improvements in pensions, travel, and free agency rights following rulings in U.S. and Canadian courts against restrictive reserve clauses.

League governance adapted through the Board of Governors, comprising team owners who negotiated television contracts (e.g., with ESPN in 1985) and realigned divisions to reflect franchise locations and travel costs. Regulatory scrutiny under U.S. antitrust law and Canadian competition policy shaped draft, salary cap, and revenue-sharing mechanisms, underscoring professional sports’ intersection with public policy.

---

## Internationalization and Cold War Influence

Hockey’s Cold War symbolism reached a zenith in the 1972 Summit Series between Canada and the Soviet Union, viewed as a proxy for ideological competition. In subsequent decades, détente and the collapse of communist regimes enabled NHL recruitment of Eastern Bloc players—Vladislav Tretiak (retired), then pioneers such as Viacheslav Fetisov and Jaromír Jágr—broadening the league’s talent base and global appeal.

---

## Modern Expansion and Challenges (1992–Present)

From 1992 onward, the NHL expanded into the Sun Belt and other nontraditional markets—San Jose (1991), Tampa Bay (1992), Nashville (1998), and Las Vegas (2017)—reflecting demographic shifts and corporate sponsorship trends. Cross-border integration continued, with franchises in Canada and the United States cooperating on broadcast rights, marketing, and arena financing, often involving municipal and state/provincial subsidies.

Labor unrest recurred in 1992 (lockout) and 2004–05 (full‐season cancellation), highlighting tensions over salary caps, revenue sharing, and players’ rights. The resulting 2005 collective bargaining agreement introduced a hard salary cap tied to league revenues, aligning NHL financial management with broader U.S. labor–management norms.

Concussion concerns and player safety have prompted collaboration with government health agencies and sports commissions. Internationally, the NHL participates in the IIHF World Championships and has staged regular-season games in Europe and Mexico City, reinforcing hockey diplomacy and market development.

---

Over more than a century, the NHL’s trajectory—from a four-team Montreal league to a 32-team transnational enterprise—mirrors North American political-economic integration, labor law evolution, and cultural diplomacy. Its history highlights how professional sports operate at the nexus of business strategy, national identity, and public policy.
