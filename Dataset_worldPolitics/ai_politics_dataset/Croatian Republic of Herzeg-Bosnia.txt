**Croatian Republic of Herzeg-Bosnia**

The Croatian Republic of Herzeg-Bosnia (Croatian: Hrvatska Republika Herceg-Bosna) was a self-proclaimed political and territorial entity within the internationally recognized borders of Bosnia and Herzegovina during the Bosnian War (1992–1994). Established by Bosnian Croat leaders, it aimed to safeguard Croat interests and administer Croat-majority areas in central and southern Bosnia and Herzegovina. Although never recognized by the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina or the international community, Herzeg-Bosnia maintained its own political institutions, armed forces, and administrative structures until its integration into the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina under the Washington Agreement of March 1994.

## Historical Background

In the multiethnic context of Bosnia and Herzegovina, tensions among Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims), Serbs, and Croats rose significantly following Bosnia and Herzegovina’s declaration of independence from the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia in March 1992. While Bosniaks and Serbs moved to assert their visions of the state—Bosniaks favoring a unitary republic and Serbs pursuing secession to join Republika Srpska—Bosnian Croats debated their own political future. Influenced by the break-up of Yugoslavia and Croatia’s support for fellow Croats across the border, Bosnian Croat leaders sought to protect Croat-populated areas from perceived marginalization and threat of Serb military advances.

## Formation and Political Structure

On October 18, 1991, the Assembly of the Croatian Community of Herzeg-Bosnia convened in Mostar, declaring a Croatian political and territorial unit within Bosnia and Herzegovina. The body adopted a constitution on August 28, 1993, formally proclaiming the Croatian Republic of Herzeg-Bosnia with Mostar as its capital. Its political organs included a President, a Parliament (Sabor), and a Council of Ministers. Mate Boban, former president of the Croatian Democratic Union of Bosnia and Herzegovina (HDZ BiH), served as the first and principal president, guiding its political agenda toward autonomy for Croat areas.

## Administrative Divisions and Governance

Herzeg-Bosnia was divided into six counties—Herzegovina, West Herzegovina, Central Bosnia, North Herzegovina, Posavina, and Livno—each governed by locally appointed prefects and councils. It established ministries for defense, education, health, finance, and internal affairs, operating parallel to those of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina. A separate judiciary and police force enforced laws promulgated by the Herzeg-Bosnia parliament. The entity maintained its own currency (the Croatian dinar until 1993, then the Bosnia-Herzegovina dinar) and issued license plates marked “HRH.”

## Military Forces

The Croatian Defence Council (Hrvatsko vijeće obrane, HVO), founded in April 1992, acted as the military arm of Herzeg-Bosnia. Initially cooperating with the Army of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina (ARBiH) against Serb forces, the HVO later fought both Bosnian Serb and Bosniak forces in areas of territorial dispute. The HVO organized brigades along territorial lines, recruited locally, and received material support from Croatia. It conducted operations aimed at consolidating Croat-majority zones, but also became implicated in incidents of ethnic cleansing and wartime atrocities against Bosniak civilians during the Croat–Bosniak conflict.

## Role in the Bosnian War

During 1992–1993, Herzeg-Bosnia sought to establish ethnic continuity in Croat regions, often clashing with Bosniak forces over mixed-population towns such as Mostar, Jajce, and Vitez. Political rivalry culminated in open conflict in late 1992, fueled by disagreements over federalization versus centralization of the Bosnian state. International mediation by the United Nations and the Contact Group attempted to resolve disputes but failed until intense U.S. diplomacy in early 1994. Meanwhile, the HVO and ARBiH engaged in sieges, offensives, and counter-offensives, fragmenting local authority and inflicting civilian suffering.

## Washington Agreement and Dissolution

Under mounting pressure from the United States and renewed cooperation against Serb forces, Bosnian Croat and Bosniak leaders signed the Washington Agreement on March 18, 1994. The accord abolished Herzeg-Bosnia as a separate entity and merged Croat- and Bosniak-held territories into the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina, one of two constituent entities of Bosnia and Herzegovina (alongside Republika Srpska). The HVO was integrated into the Federation’s armed forces, and Herzeg-Bosnia’s political institutions were dissolved or absorbed into new federal structures. The agreement established a ten-cantonal administrative system, promoting power-sharing and mixed governance in formerly contested areas.

## Legacy

The Croatian Republic of Herzeg-Bosnia remains a contentious chapter of the Bosnian War. It shaped interethnic relations by institutionalizing Croat autonomy aspirations and contributed to wartime fragmentation. Its dissolution under the Washington Agreement laid the groundwork for the post-Dayton political order, embedding a complex system of ethnic power-sharing. Debates continue over its legacy in contemporary Bosnia and Herzegovina, influencing discussions on federalism, national identity, and the memory of wartime experiences among Croat and Bosniak communities.
