**Vietnam War Draft**

**Overview**
The Vietnam War draft refers to the conscription system employed by the United States government to meet military manpower needs during the conflict in Vietnam from the early 1960s through 1973. Administered under the Selective Service System, the draft became a focal point of political controversy and social unrest, shaping public attitudes toward the war, influencing legislative reform, and ultimately contributing to the transition to an all-volunteer force.

**Legal Foundation**
Conscription in the United States dates to the Selective Service Act of 1917. This framework remained in place—with modifications—through World War II, Korea, and into the Vietnam era. Under the Selective Service Act of 1948 (as amended), all male U.S. citizens and resident aliens aged 18 to 26 were required to register for the draft. Local draft boards, composed of civilian volunteers, evaluated registrants for eligibility and classified them based on criteria such as student status, family dependency, and physical fitness.

**Early Vietnam Draft (1964–1968)**
With U.S. military advisors in Vietnam escalating to combat troops beginning in 1965, President Lyndon B. Johnson relied heavily on conscription to field divisions. In 1965, draft calls peaked at over 300,000 men annually. Draft classifications included:

* **1-A (Available for military service)**
* **1-O (Conscientious objector available for noncombatant service)**
* **2-S (Student deferment)**
* **3-A (Hardship or dependency deferment)**
* **4-F (Physically unfit)**

College enrollment surged as young men sought 2-S deferments, while draft avoidance through medical deferments or relocation to the District of Columbia (whose draft board most rarely called registrants) became widespread. The perceived inequities—where wealthier students and those with connections could delay or avoid service—fueled accusations that the draft disproportionately burdened lower-income and minority communities.

**Nationwide Draft Lottery (1969–1972)**
Facing mounting casualties and waning public support after the Tet Offensive (1968), the Nixon administration initiated a reform to enhance perceived fairness. Public Law 91-379 established a draft lottery based on dates of birth. On December 1, 1969, the first lottery determined the order of call for men born between January and December 1944; subsequent lotteries covered cohorts through 1950. Lottery numbers dictated induction priority: low-numbered birthdays meant earlier call. While intended to equalize risk, the system still permitted deferments and exemptions, and early lottery results (e.g., low numbers drawn for August birthdays) sparked controversy over randomness and procedural transparency.

**Conscientious Objection and Resistance**
The draft system recognized conscientious objectors (COs) who opposed all war. CO applicants (1-O) underwent hearings and, if approved, performed alternative service in hospitals, social agencies, or other civilian roles. However, broader draft resistance emerged:

* **Draft Card Burning:** Beginning in 1965, some registrants publicly burned draft cards as a symbolic protest, risking criminal penalties under the 1965 amendment to the Selective Service Act.
* **Draft Counseling and Evasion:** Organizations such as the National Mobilization Committee to End the War in Vietnam provided legal aid, pamphlets on draft law, and guidance on avoiding induction through deferments or emigration to Canada.
* **Draft Board Sit-ins and Confrontations:** Activists occupied draft board offices to disrupt operations and draw media attention to opposition.

**Political and Social Impact**
By the early 1970s, opposition to the draft had become integral to the broader antiwar movement. Polls showed growing public willingness to accept a draft if direct threats to U.S. territory emerged, but strong rejection of compulsory service for an unpopular foreign war. Draft-related protests contributed to congressional passage in 1971 of legislation lowering the draft age to 18, aligning military eligibility with voting age, in an effort to share burden more evenly. However, this measure also intensified youth activism.

**End of the Draft and Transition to All-Volunteer Force**
On June 30, 1973, Selective Service ceased inductions, marking the end of conscription for the Vietnam War. The all-volunteer force that followed was institutionalized by the Military Selective Service Act’s continuing registration requirement (which remains in place) but without active drafting. In 1973 and 1974, the Nixon and Ford administrations—and subsequently the Carter administration—completed the transition, codifying a volunteer military deeply shaped by lessons learned during Vietnam about recruitment, retention, and public support.

**Legacy**
The Vietnam War draft left enduring marks on American politics and society. It catalyzed debates over civic duty, equity, and executive power, spurring reforms in military manpower policy. The draft’s controversial implementation and public resistance influenced later decisions to rely solely on volunteers, reshaped veterans’ demographics, and underscored the complex relationship between democratic governance and the use of military force abroad.
