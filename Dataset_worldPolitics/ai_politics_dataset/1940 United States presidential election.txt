**1940 United States Presidential Election**

The **1940 United States presidential election** was held on **Tuesday, November 5, 1940**. It was a historically significant contest that resulted in the unprecedented election of **Franklin D. Roosevelt** to a third term as President of the United States. Roosevelt, a Democrat, defeated Republican challenger **Wendell Willkie**, a corporate executive and political newcomer. The election occurred against the backdrop of mounting global tensions as World War II raged in Europe and Asia, and it reflected deep political and ideological debates within the United States over foreign policy, economic recovery, and constitutional norms.

---

### Background and Historical Context

By 1940, Roosevelt had served two terms as president, having first been elected in 1932 during the depths of the Great Depression. His administration had introduced the **New Deal**, a series of economic reforms and public works programs aimed at recovery and reform. While the New Deal had both fervent supporters and sharp critics, Roosevelt remained personally popular with many voters.

A major political question going into the 1940 election was whether Roosevelt would seek a **third term**, something no previous U.S. president had done, following the precedent set by George Washington. As the international situation deteriorated with the rise of fascism in Europe and militarism in Asia, Roosevelt argued that continuity in leadership was essential to maintain U.S. security and prepare for a possible global conflict. This reasoning helped justify his break with tradition.

Meanwhile, isolationist sentiment remained strong in both political parties and among the American public. The **America First movement** opposed involvement in the war and viewed Roosevelt's foreign policy as edging the country toward conflict. The third-term issue and the debate over foreign policy would become central themes of the election.

---

### Democratic Nomination

Despite speculation that he might retire, Roosevelt announced his candidacy for a third term at the **1940 Democratic National Convention** in Chicago. With overwhelming support from the party establishment and grassroots delegates, Roosevelt was renominated on the first ballot. To broaden his appeal and signal continuity in national unity, he replaced his liberal Vice President **John Nance Garner** with **Henry A. Wallace**, his Secretary of Agriculture and a progressive New Dealer.

Roosevelt ran on a platform defending the achievements of the New Deal and emphasizing the need for steady leadership amid the gathering storm of global war.

---

### Republican Nomination

The Republican Party faced a divided field in 1940, with leading candidates including Senators Robert Taft of Ohio and Arthur Vandenberg of Michigan, both staunch isolationists. However, a dark horse emerged in the form of **Wendell Willkie**, a former Democrat and an internationalist businessman from New York. Willkie, a vocal opponent of some New Deal policies, supported aid to Britain and was seen as a moderate alternative to the party’s more conservative factions.

At the **Republican National Convention** in Philadelphia, Willkie's candidacy gained momentum and he secured the nomination on the sixth ballot. His running mate was **Senator Charles L. McNary** of Oregon.

Willkie ran a vigorous campaign criticizing Roosevelt’s concentration of executive power, the expansion of federal government, and his decision to run for a third term. However, Willkie largely aligned with Roosevelt on foreign policy, especially regarding support for the Allies, blunting what could have been a major campaign contrast.

---

### General Election Campaign

The 1940 election was dominated by two major issues: **the question of a third presidential term** and **the debate over American involvement in World War II**.

Roosevelt downplayed war fears by assuring voters that he would not send American troops into foreign wars unless the United States was attacked. Willkie echoed many of Roosevelt’s foreign policy views but criticized the President for allegedly circumventing constitutional checks and weakening democratic traditions.

Domestically, Roosevelt’s New Deal remained a source of partisan division. Republicans warned of “big government” and declining liberties, while Democrats touted economic recovery, expanded social programs, and rural electrification as achievements of Roosevelt’s administration.

---

### Election Results

| Candidate             | Party      | Electoral Votes | Popular Vote | Percentage |
| --------------------- | ---------- | --------------- | ------------ | ---------- |
| Franklin D. Roosevelt | Democratic | 449             | 27,244,160   | 54.7%      |
| Wendell Willkie       | Republican | 82              | 22,305,198   | 44.8%      |

Roosevelt won a decisive victory in both the **Electoral College** and the **popular vote**, carrying 38 of the 48 states. His strongest support came from the South, industrial states in the Northeast and Midwest, and parts of the West. Willkie performed well in the Midwest and the Northeast but was unable to break the Democratic coalition Roosevelt had built since 1932.

---

### Political and Historical Significance

The **1940 election was unprecedented** in U.S. history. Roosevelt’s victory to a third term marked a fundamental shift in the unwritten two-term tradition established by George Washington. While many viewed the third term as a necessary response to global instability, others saw it as a troubling centralization of power. The issue would remain controversial until the ratification of the **22nd Amendment** in 1951, which constitutionally limited presidents to two terms.

The election also cemented Roosevelt’s dominance in American politics and ensured continuity in the executive branch during a period of global crisis. His re-election allowed the administration to pursue greater military preparedness and implement programs like **Lend-Lease**, which positioned the United States as the “arsenal of democracy” prior to entering World War II.

Moreover, Willkie's campaign, despite losing, represented a **moderate and internationalist wing** of the Republican Party that would influence postwar politics, especially under future leaders like Dwight D. Eisenhower.
