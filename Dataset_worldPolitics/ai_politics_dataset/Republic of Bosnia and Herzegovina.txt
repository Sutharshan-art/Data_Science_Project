**Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina**

**Overview**
The Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina (Bosnian/Croatian/Serbian: Republika Bosna i Hercegovina / Република Босна и Херцеговина) was the internationally recognized sovereign state that emerged following Bosnia and Herzegovina’s declaration of independence from the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia in March 1992. Functioning as a multiethnic republic, it existed through the course of the Bosnian War (1992–1995) and continued under its original constitution until the Dayton Peace Agreement redefined its internal structure in December 1995.

**Historical Background**
Under socialist Yugoslavia (1945–1991), Bosnia and Herzegovina was one of six constituent republics, celebrated for its ethnic and religious diversity—principally Bosniak Muslims, Orthodox Serbs, and Catholic Croats. The weakening of central authority in Belgrade and rising nationalist movements across Yugoslavia during the late 1980s spurred competitive assertions of self-determination. In October 1991, following similar referenda in Slovenia and Croatia, Bosnia’s Assembly voted to hold an independence referendum.

**Independence Referendum and Declaration**
From 29 February to 1 March 1992, a popular referendum on sovereignty was held. Despite a boycott by many Bosnian Serb voters, turnout exceeded 60 percent, and over 99 percent of participants voted in favor of independence. On 3 March 1992, the Assembly of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina formally declared independence, and on 6 April 1992 the United States and European Community states recognized the new republic, paving the way for its admission to the United Nations on 22 May 1992.

**Political Institutions**
The 1991 constitution, retained at independence, established a parliamentary-presidential system. The presidency was a collective three-member body, with one representative each for Bosniaks, Serbs, and Croats, elected by popular vote for four-year terms. The unicameral Assembly of Bosnia and Herzegovina (Skupština) comprised delegates from across the country. Ministries overseen day-to-day governance, though wartime exigencies often meant that military and humanitarian priorities superseded civilian administration.

**Wartime Government and Leadership**
Alija Izetbegović, leader of the Party of Democratic Action (SDA), served as head of the Bosniak delegation in the collective presidency and was widely regarded as the republic’s principal leader. His government faced the unprecedented challenge of sustaining sovereignty, organizing defense forces under the Army of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina, and maintaining essential services amid siege warfare, particularly in Sarajevo. Parallel Serb and Croat entities—Republika Srpska and the Croatian Community of Herzeg-Bosnia—challenged the republic’s authority, fracturing its territory and complicating governance.

**International Recognition and Support**
International recognition granted the republic formal legitimacy, but securing viable defense and humanitarian aid proved difficult. The United Nations imposed an arms embargo on all former Yugoslav republics in September 1991, constraining the republic’s armed forces while inadvertently favoring better-armed Serb units. In April 1992, the UN Security Council established UNPROFOR to protect designated “safe areas” and deliver humanitarian relief. Diplomatic initiatives—the Vance–Owen Plan (1993) and the Contact Group’s Carrington proposals—sought to negotiate ceasefires and territorial divisions but ultimately faltered.

**Conflict and Territorial Control**
From 1992 to 1995, the republic’s government controlled enclaves in central and northern Bosnia, including Sarajevo, Tuzla, and Bihać. Bosnian Serb forces, backed by the Yugoslav People’s Army, besieged Sarajevo and seized large swaths of territory early in the war. Bosnian Croat forces vied for control in western and southern regions. In response, the republic’s armed forces conducted defensive and occasional counteroffensive operations to reclaim key areas and safeguard supply lines.

**Dayton Peace Agreement and Aftermath**
In November 1995, U.S.-brokered negotiations at Dayton, Ohio, produced the General Framework Agreement for Peace in Bosnia and Herzegovina. Under its terms, the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina was succeeded by a single sovereign state—Bosnia and Herzegovina—composed of two largely autonomous entities: the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (primarily Bosniak and Croat) and Republika Srpska (Serb). The original republican institutions were restructured to include a central three-member presidency, a bicameral Parliamentary Assembly, and a reinforced Constitutional Court. The transition was formalized with the signing of the accord on 14 December 1995.

**Legacy**
Though the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina existed only for the duration of the Bosnian War, it laid the foundations for the modern state. Its struggle to preserve multiethnic governance under extreme duress underscored the challenges of post–Cold War statehood in ethnically mixed societies. The wartime republic’s experience informed subsequent constitutional reforms, power-sharing arrangements, and international interventions aimed at consolidating peace and promoting democratic governance in Bosnia and Herzegovina. The legacy of its institutions and leadership continues to shape political discourse and interethnic relations in the country today.
