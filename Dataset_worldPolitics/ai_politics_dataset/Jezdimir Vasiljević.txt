**Jezdimir Vasiljević** (born 2 November 1948) is a Serbian businessman and convicted criminal best known for operating one of the largest Ponzi schemes in the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia during the early 1990s. His activities—and their collapse—illustrate the interplay of wartime economies, international sanctions, and criminal finance amid the breakup of Yugoslavia.

### Early Life and Business Ventures

Vasiljević was born in the village of Topolovnik in what was then the People’s Republic of Serbia. Little is publicly documented about his youth, but he later claimed to have worked abroad in sectors ranging from diamond prospecting to commodity trading before returning to Serbia in 1991. He first gained prominence by importing consumer electronics and Japanese automobiles, positioning himself as a flamboyant figure in Serbia’s emerging private sector.

### Formation of Jugoskandik and Economic Context

In 1992, against the backdrop of United Nations sanctions imposed on Serbia and Montenegro for their roles in the Yugoslav Wars, Vasiljević established Jugoskandik Savings Bank—the country’s first private bank. Operating under a license from a Montenegrin institution, Jugoskandik attracted deposits from citizens seeking protection against hyperinflation that reached astronomical levels. Vasiljević pledged to use these funds to import oil through Montenegrin ports and sell it domestically at a significant markup to generate returns for earlier investors, creating the classic structure of a Ponzi scheme.

### High-Profile Chess Match

To bolster his public image and international connections, Vasiljević financed the 1992 rematch between former world chess champion Bobby Fischer and Boris Spassky at the Montenegrin resort of Sveti Stefan. Marketed as the “Match of the Century,” the event drew global media attention during Serbia’s period of isolation. Fischer’s appearance violated U.S. sanctions, leading to his effective exile, and casting further scrutiny on Vasiljević’s influence and willingness to flout international restrictions.

### Collapse and Legal Consequences

By early 1993, Jugoskandik’s liabilities far exceeded its assets. Montenegrin authorities seized thousands of tons of oil that had been held as purported collateral for depositors, revealing the bank’s insolvency. An estimated 80,000 depositors lost their savings when the oil reserves proved insufficient to cover withdrawals. Facing mounting civil and criminal claims, Vasiljević fled Serbia in March 1993. He was later arrested—once in 2000 in Belgrade and again in 2009 in the Netherlands—and extradited back to Serbia. In 2013, he was convicted of embezzlement and fraud and sentenced to five years in prison for causing substantial financial damage to depositors and the national banking system.

### Role in Wartime Economy and Sanctions Evasion

Vasiljević’s scheme flourished amid the wartime economy, characterized by shortages, hyperinflation, and a booming black market. By channeling depositors’ funds into oil imports, he effectively became an intermediary for sanctioned goods, underscoring how private actors could exploit political upheaval to profit from embargoes. His operations exemplify the challenges of enforcing international sanctions and maintaining financial stability during armed conflict.

### Public Persona and Media Appearances

After serving his sentence, Vasiljević re-entered Serbian public life as a television personality. In 2013, he appeared on a reality program in which former Jugoskandik depositors confronted him about their losses, a moment symbolizing Serbia’s ongoing reckoning with the economic collapse of the 1990s. Vasiljević remains a controversial figure: admired by some as an archetype of post-communist entrepreneurial daring and reviled by others as a symbol of greed and impunity in a period of national crisis.

### Legacy and Historical Significance

Jezdimir Vasiljević’s rise and fall highlight the vulnerabilities of societies in political and economic transition. The Jugoskandik scandal eroded public confidence in Serbia’s nascent private banking sector and prompted legal reforms aimed at strengthening financial regulation. Vasiljević’s story serves as a cautionary tale about the intersection of war-time profiteering, inadequate oversight, and the complexities of rebuilding economies in post-conflict environments.
