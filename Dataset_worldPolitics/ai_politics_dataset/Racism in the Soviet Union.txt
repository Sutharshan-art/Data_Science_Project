Racism in the Soviet Union refers to the attitudes, policies, and practices of racial and ethnic discrimination that occurred within the USSR from its establishment in 1922 to its dissolution in 1991. Although Marxist-Leninist doctrine nominally rejected racism as a bourgeois ideology, various forms of xenophobia, ethnic hierarchy, and state-sanctioned discrimination persisted, often in tension with official proclamations of internationalism and equality.

**Historical Context**
The Russian Empire, predecessor to the Soviet state, encompassed dozens of ethnic groups and nationalities, many of whom faced legal and social discrimination. The Bolshevik Revolution of 1917 and subsequent civil war brought these issues to the fore. Early Soviet leaders sought to forge a new socialist identity that would transcend national and racial divisions under the slogan “proletarian internationalism.” In practice, however, policies vacillated between support for minority cultures and suspicion of “bourgeois nationalism” among non-Russian peoples.

**Legal and Constitutional Framework**
The 1918 and 1924 Soviet Constitutions guaranteed equality of all citizens regardless of ethnicity, and the 1936 Constitution reaffirmed these principles, explicitly prohibiting discrimination on racial, national, or religious grounds. Soviet nationality policy included korenizatsiia (“indigenization”), promoting native languages and cultures in non-Russian republics and regions, alongside affirmative action in party and government appointments. Nonetheless, no specific legal codes against “racism” as understood in Western discourse were enacted; rather, discrimination was often pursued under charges of “bourgeois nationalism,” “counter-revolutionary activity,” or “anti-Soviet agitation.”

**Manifestations of Discrimination**
Despite official egalitarianism, ethnic Russians frequently occupied privileged positions within the hierarchy of the Communist Party, the military, and the security services. Minority groups perceived as “foreign” or “backward”—including Central Asian, Caucasian, and Far Eastern communities—faced stereotypes of being uneducated or inflammatory. During the 1930s Great Purge, entire ethnicities (e.g., Poles, Koreans, Germans) were targeted for forced deportation to remote regions under suspicion of espionage or disloyalty. These actions, while politically motivated, carried racialized undertones by conflating ethnicity with treachery.

**Anti-Semitism and the “Doctors’ Plot”**
Although Jews initially experienced a reduction in tsarist-era legal restrictions, anti-Semitic attitudes persisted in society and sometimes in state policy. Under Stalin’s late rule, a campaign culminating in the 1953 “Doctors’ Plot” accused predominantly Jewish physicians of conspiring to poison Soviet leaders. While the campaign was officially halted upon Stalin’s death, it reflected deep-seated biases and instrumentalization of ethnic stereotypes for political purges.

**Treatment of African Students and “Negro Question”**
From the 1950s onward, the USSR welcomed students and workers from Africa, Asia, and Latin America as part of its Cold War outreach. Officially, they were treated as comrades in the global struggle against colonialism and racism. However, foreign students, particularly Black Africans, encountered covert discrimination: difficulty securing housing, social ostracism, and sometimes overt hostility. Soviet propaganda capitalized on showcasing internationalism but often failed to challenge ingrained societal prejudices among ordinary citizens.

**World War II and National Identity**
During the war against Nazi Germany, Soviet leadership emphasized Russian patriotism alongside socialist heroism to motivate the population. While this “Great Patriotic War” narrative bolstered unity, it also re-centered ethnic Russian identity, at times marginalizing non-Slavic peoples. Ethnic minorities served valiantly, yet their contributions were often subsumed under a broader Soviet identity that defaulted to Russian cultural norms in official memorialization.

**Post-Stalin Thaw and Khrushchev Era**
Nikita Khrushchev’s “Thaw” (mid-1950s to early 1960s) saw limited loosening of cultural controls and some rehabilitation of deported peoples, but anti-Semitic policies continued unofficially. Khrushchev criticized certain forms of “Great Russian chauvinism” yet maintained restrictive emigration policies for Jews and suppressed expressions of Jewish identity. Minority languages and cultural institutions persisted, but central planning and Russification in education and administration accelerated.

**Brezhnev Era and Stagnation**
Under Leonid Brezhnev, the Soviet leadership emphasized stability and often ignored ethnic grievances. Local Communist Party bosses wielded considerable power over nationality questions, and interethnic tensions occasionally erupted into violence (e.g., clashes between Russians and Ingush in 1958, or Armenian-Azerbaijani skirmishes in 1968). These incidents, though addressed as local disturbances, revealed latent hostilities and competition for resources framed along ethnic lines.

**Perestroika, Glasnost, and Rise of Ethno-Nationalism**
Mikhail Gorbachev’s reforms in the mid-1980s unleashed pent-up nationalist demands across the USSR’s republics. While aimed at democratization, glasnost encouraged free discussion of previously suppressed grievances, including interethnic relations. Unfortunately, this climate also enabled racist and xenophobic rhetoric to proliferate, fueling ethnic conflicts in the Caucasus and Baltic regions as Soviet cohesion unraveled.

**Legacy and Historiographical Debate**
Historians debate the extent to which racism in the Soviet Union constituted a systemic phenomenon versus episodic deviations from internationalist ideals. While Soviet ideology explicitly condemned racism, its application was inconsistent, often subordinated to political expediency and security considerations. The legacy of Soviet nationality policy continues to shape interethnic relations in successor states, informing contemporary debates on multiculturalism, Russification, and historical memory.
