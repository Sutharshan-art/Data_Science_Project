**Bosnian–Ragusan War of 1403–1404**

The **Bosnian–Ragusan War of 1403–1404** was a brief but significant military conflict between the Kingdom of Bosnia and the Republic of Ragusa (modern-day Dubrovnik). Rooted in the shifting political alliances and territorial ambitions of the late medieval Balkans, the war reflected the complex relations between regional powers competing for influence along the Adriatic coast and within the interior Balkans during the early 15th century.

---

### Historical Background and Political Context

In the late 14th and early 15th centuries, the Kingdom of Bosnia, under the rule of King Tvrtko I and his successors, was a powerful regional state. The Bosnian crown sought to expand and consolidate its authority in the Western Balkans amid the decline of the Serbian Empire and increasing Ottoman incursions.

The Republic of Ragusa was a wealthy maritime city-state strategically located on the Adriatic Sea, renowned for its extensive trade networks and diplomatic skill. Though small in size, Ragusa maintained a high degree of autonomy through skillful navigation between larger powers such as the Kingdom of Hungary, the Venetian Republic, and the Ottoman Empire.

The relationship between Bosnia and Ragusa was shaped by economic interdependence and political tension. Ragusa relied on Bosnian hinterlands for resources and overland trade routes, while Bosnia sought control over coastal territories and influence over regional commerce.

---

### Causes of the War

The war was triggered by shifting allegiances following the death of King Tvrtko I in 1391 and the succession struggles that ensued. In 1403, the Hungarian King Sigismund supported Ladislaus of Naples’ claim to the Hungarian throne, opposing King Sigismund’s own rule. This dynastic conflict affected neighboring states, including Bosnia and Ragusa.

Bosnia, led by King Stjepan Ostoja, allied with Ladislaus of Naples, while Ragusa sided with King Sigismund. This alignment deepened existing tensions over territorial disputes, especially regarding control of key trading routes and border fortifications.

The immediate cause of hostilities was Ragusa’s refusal to accept Bosnian control over certain territories and trade privileges, leading to Bosnian military actions against Ragusan interests.

---

### Course of the War

The conflict lasted from 1403 to 1404 and primarily involved skirmishes and sieges rather than large-scale battles. Bosnian forces launched raids into Ragusan territory and attempted to assert dominance over border areas.

Ragusa responded with diplomatic appeals to King Sigismund and sought support from Venice and other regional powers. Despite its smaller military capacity, Ragusa utilized its strategic position and alliances to resist Bosnian advances.

The war did not result in decisive territorial changes but highlighted the fragile balance of power in the region and the limits of Bosnian expansionist ambitions along the Adriatic coast.

---

### Resolution and Aftermath

The Bosnian–Ragusan War ended with negotiated settlements restoring the pre-war status quo. Both parties recognized the benefits of trade and coexistence over prolonged conflict.

Ragusa maintained its autonomy and continued to flourish as a maritime republic, while Bosnia retained influence inland but refrained from further attempts to seize Ragusan territories.

The conflict underscored the importance of diplomacy and shifting alliances in the medieval Balkans, with Ragusa exemplifying how small states could survive amid larger rivals.

---

### Significance

Though relatively short and limited in scope, the war illustrated broader themes of medieval Balkan politics, including the interaction between land-based kingdoms and coastal city-states, the impact of dynastic struggles on regional stability, and the role of economic interests in shaping conflict.

The war also foreshadowed future Bosnian interactions with external powers, especially as the Ottoman Empire’s influence expanded in the 15th century.

---

### Conclusion

The Bosnian–Ragusan War of 1403–1404 was a conflict rooted in dynastic rivalries and economic competition during a volatile period in Balkan history. Its resolution through diplomacy rather than conquest reflected the pragmatic approaches necessary for survival in a fragmented political landscape. The episode remains a notable example of the complex relations between medieval Balkan states and the enduring importance of Ragusa as a regional actor.
