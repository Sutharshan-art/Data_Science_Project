**Lyndon B. Johnson**

**Lyndon Baines Johnson** (August 27, 1908 – January 22, 1973) was the 36th president of the United States, serving from 1963 to 1969, after previously holding the office of vice president under John F. Kennedy. A Democrat from Texas, Johnson served in both houses of Congress for nearly three decades before assuming the presidency following Kennedy’s assassination. His administration is known for sweeping domestic reforms under the banner of the “Great Society” and for a deeply controversial escalation of U.S. involvement in the Vietnam War.

Johnson’s political legacy is marked by a mix of landmark civil rights achievements and divisive foreign policy decisions, making him one of the most consequential and complex figures in 20th-century American politics.

---

### Early Political Career

Born in rural Texas, Johnson graduated from Southwest Texas State Teachers College (now Texas State University) and worked briefly as a teacher before entering politics. In 1937, he won a seat in the U.S. House of Representatives, and in 1948, he was elected to the U.S. Senate. Known for his forceful personality and mastery of legislative procedure, Johnson quickly rose through the ranks, becoming Senate Majority Leader in 1955.

As a legislator, Johnson was instrumental in passing infrastructure and rural electrification initiatives, though he maintained a centrist stance on civil rights to align with his Southern Democratic base. His leadership style, often called the “Johnson treatment,” combined personal persuasion and political pressure to achieve his legislative goals.

---

### Vice Presidency

Johnson was selected as John F. Kennedy’s running mate in the 1960 presidential election to balance the ticket geographically and ideologically. As vice president (1961–1963), Johnson had limited influence over administration policy, particularly in foreign affairs, though he remained involved in space exploration and poverty initiatives.

His political experience and connections in Congress proved invaluable, however, and his presence on the ticket helped Kennedy secure critical Southern votes during the election.

---

### Presidency (1963–1969)

Johnson assumed the presidency on November 22, 1963, following President Kennedy’s assassination in Dallas, Texas. He immediately sought to unite a grieving nation and move forward with Kennedy’s legislative agenda.

#### The Great Society

Johnson’s most enduring domestic legacy is the **Great Society**, a series of social and economic programs aimed at eliminating poverty and racial injustice. Major legislative achievements during his presidency include:

* **The Civil Rights Act of 1964**, which outlawed segregation and employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin.
* **The Voting Rights Act of 1965**, aimed at eliminating barriers to voting for African Americans in the South.
* **Medicare and Medicaid**, which provided health insurance to the elderly and low-income populations.
* **The Elementary and Secondary Education Act**, expanding federal funding for public schools.
* **The War on Poverty**, which included programs such as Head Start, Job Corps, and food stamps.

These initiatives reshaped the role of the federal government in American life and significantly reduced poverty levels during the 1960s. However, they also led to increased government spending and opposition from conservatives who viewed the programs as federal overreach.

#### Vietnam War

Despite his domestic achievements, Johnson’s presidency was increasingly dominated by the **Vietnam War**. Committed to containing communism, Johnson authorized a major escalation of U.S. military involvement in Vietnam following the 1964 Gulf of Tonkin incident. By 1968, over 500,000 American troops were stationed in Southeast Asia.

The war’s growing unpopularity, rising casualties, and lack of clear progress led to widespread protests and political unrest at home. Johnson’s credibility suffered following the 1968 Tet Offensive, and he announced in March of that year that he would not seek re-election.

---

### Political Decline and Legacy

Johnson left office in January 1969 with his presidency deeply divided between success in domestic policy and failure in foreign policy. Though his approval ratings plummeted due to the war, his legislative record—particularly on civil rights and poverty—profoundly transformed American society.

In retirement, Johnson returned to his Texas ranch and published his memoirs. He died of a heart attack in 1973 at the age of 64.

---

### Historical Significance

Lyndon B. Johnson is remembered as a paradoxical figure: a Southern politician who championed civil rights, a legislative genius who miscalculated a foreign war, and a president whose ambitious vision reshaped the social contract between Americans and their government. His presidency marked the peak of mid-20th-century liberalism and the beginning of political realignment in the United States, as disaffected white Southerners began shifting toward the Republican Party in response to his civil rights agenda.

Modern assessments of Johnson continue to reflect this dual legacy, positioning him as both a transformative reformer and a cautionary figure in the use of American military power abroad.
