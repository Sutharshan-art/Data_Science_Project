**United States Prisoners of War during the Vietnam War**

**Overview**
During the Vietnam War (1955–1975), approximately 766 United States military personnel became prisoners of war (POWs) held by the Democratic Republic of Vietnam (North Vietnam). Captured in air, land, and riverine operations, these service members endured varied detention conditions until their release following the January 1973 Paris Peace Accords. The treatment and fate of U.S. POWs became a central political issue in American society, influencing public opinion, military policy, and postwar efforts to account for those missing in action (MIA).

**Capture and Numbers**
U.S. POWs in Vietnam were captured under diverse circumstances: downed aircraft, ambushes of ground patrols, and navy and army riverine operations in the Mekong Delta and along the Ho Chi Minh Trail. Of the 766 held, 94 were captured before the major U.S. ground buildup of 1965, with the remainder taken between 1965 and 1972. The majority—nearly 600—were U.S. Air Force and Navy aviators shot down over North Vietnam during bombing missions. Army and Marine Corps losses accounted for the rest, often captured in isolated firefights or during the evacuation of embattled positions.

**Detention Facilities and Conditions**
North Vietnamese detention facilities varied in security, overcrowding, and harshness of treatment. The most infamous was Hỏa Lò Prison in Hanoi, dubbed the “Hanoi Hilton” by American prisoners. Others included the “Zoo” in Hàm Tân, the Barton Field facility near Đồng Hới, and various jungle camps. Conditions generally featured inadequate shelter, scant medical care, meager rations, and hard labor. POWs endured extreme heat, monsoon rains, and endemic diseases such as malaria and dysentery. Torture—particularly during interrogation of downed pilots—took many forms, from prolonged solitary confinement in dark cells to beatings and stress positions intended to extract military intelligence or propaganda statements.

**The “Code of Conduct” and Resistance**
In response to the prospect of capture, President Dwight D. Eisenhower in 1955 promulgated a military “Code of Conduct” for members of the armed forces, emphasizing honor, resistance to enemy demands, and solidarity with fellow POWs. This code was revised in 1977 to reflect lessons from Vietnam. Many POWs at the Hanoi Hilton organized clandestine communication methods—such as the “tap code” spelling messages through cell walls—and engaged in collective resistance, including refusal to sign coerced confessions or participation in propaganda broadcasts.

**Casualties and Deaths**
Of the 766 U.S. POWs held in North Vietnam, 148 died in captivity, representing a mortality rate of nearly 19 percent—roughly double that of POWs in World War II and Korea. Official North Vietnamese records cited deaths from wounds sustained at capture, disease, malnutrition, and maltreatment. Some remains were repatriated in 1973; others were declared “presumed dead” and memorialized as missing. Families of MIAs pressed for full accounting, spurring decades-long investigations by the Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency.

**Repatriation and the “Operation Homecoming”**
Following the signing of the Paris Peace Accords on 27 January 1973, North Vietnam released 591 U.S. POWs in “Operation Homecoming.” Between 12 and 14 February, five C-141 transport aircraft flew 78 POWs to Clark Air Base in the Philippines for medical evaluation before returning them to U.S. military hospitals. Subsequent flights repatriated the remainder over the next week. Camps such as Hỏa Lò were evacuated or turned over to the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), which monitored compliance with the accords’ provisions on POW treatment.

**Domestic Political Impact**
The plight of POWs—and uncertainty over MIAs—became a potent political symbol in the United States. Families, veterans’ groups, and religious organizations mounted public campaigns demanding accounting for missing personnel. In 1976, Congress established the Select Committee on POW/MIA Affairs, whose 1993 report concluded that “no compelling evidence” of live MIAs remained in Southeast Asia. Nonetheless, the POW/MIA issue persisted as a cultural and political touchstone, reflecting broader debates over the war’s legacy, government transparency, and veterans’ rights.

**Veteran Reintegration and Aftercare**
Returned POWs faced significant physical and psychological challenges. Many suffered from post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), chronic health conditions, and difficulties reintegrating into civilian life. The Department of Veterans Affairs and the Department of Defense established specialized medical and mental‐health programs, including residential rehabilitation and family counseling. High‐profile POWs—such as U.S. Senator John McCain, Admiral James Stockdale, and Senator Jeremiah Denton—became advocates for veterans and public awareness of POW‐related issues.

**Legacy**
U.S. prisoners of war during the Vietnam War profoundly influenced American military doctrine, veteran care, and public expectations regarding the treatment of captured service members. The Vietnam experience led to enhanced training on survival, evasion, resistance, and escape (SERE), revisions to the Code of Conduct, and improvements in battlefield medical evacuation. The enduring pursuit of accounting for MIAs has informed U.S. policy on missing personnel in subsequent conflicts, embodying the principle that no service member should be left behind.
