**State Anthem of the Soviet Union**

The **State Anthem of the Soviet Union** was the national anthem of the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR)** from 1944 until the country’s dissolution in 1991. It replaced "The Internationale" as the official anthem during World War II and was composed by **Alexander Alexandrov** with lyrics originally written by **Sergei Mikhalkov** and **Gabriel El-Registan**. The anthem became a powerful symbol of Soviet identity, ideology, and unity, and its use and meaning evolved in accordance with the shifting political landscape of the USSR.

After the fall of the Soviet Union, the anthem’s melody was adopted by the **Russian Federation** in 2000, with new lyrics once again penned by Mikhalkov. The anthem thus holds a unique place in both Soviet and post-Soviet political culture, reflecting the continuity and transformation of state symbolism.

---

### Origins and Composition

Prior to 1944, the Soviet Union used "The Internationale," a socialist anthem associated with the global workers’ movement, as its national anthem. However, by the early 1940s, Soviet leadership sought a new anthem that would reflect the Soviet Union’s status as a powerful nation-state rather than a revolutionary movement.

During **World War II**, the Soviet government initiated a competition to select a new national anthem that could inspire patriotism and unity. The winning composition was created by **Alexander Alexandrov**, founder of the Red Army Choir. His composition was originally intended for the "Hymn of the Bolshevik Party" and was repurposed as the national anthem.

The original lyrics were authored by **Sergei Mikhalkov** and **Gabriel El-Registan**, emphasizing themes of Soviet unity, Leninist ideology, and the leadership of **Joseph Stalin**. The anthem was officially adopted on **March 15, 1944**, during the height of the Great Patriotic War (as World War II was known in the USSR), and served as a powerful rallying symbol.

---

### Political Symbolism and Ideological Content

The anthem’s original lyrics explicitly praised Stalin as the "wise leader" and celebrated the Soviet Union's victory, strength, and ideological supremacy. It emphasized the unity of the republics under the Communist Party and the guiding hand of Lenin and Stalin.

The political function of the anthem was to project the USSR as a harmonious federation of equal nations led by a strong and central authority. It was also used to assert Soviet distinction from other socialist movements globally, particularly as Stalin moved to solidify the USSR’s global influence during and after World War II.

During public events, state broadcasts, party congresses, and international meetings, the anthem reinforced Soviet identity and its revolutionary narrative. It played a central role in state ceremonies and was taught to schoolchildren throughout the republics.

---

### De-Stalinization and Lyric Revisions

Following Stalin's death in 1953 and the onset of **Nikita Khrushchev’s de-Stalinization campaign**, the political content of the anthem came under scrutiny. In 1956, during Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" denouncing Stalin’s cult of personality, the Soviet leadership discontinued the use of the anthem’s lyrics due to their glorification of Stalin.

From **1956 to 1977**, the anthem was performed in an **instrumental-only version**, with its original lyrics effectively censored from public use. This period coincided with broader efforts to reinterpret Soviet history and distance the regime from Stalinist repression.

In **1977**, as part of the celebrations for the **60th anniversary of the October Revolution**, the Soviet government reintroduced the anthem with **new lyrics** by Sergei Mikhalkov. The revised version removed references to Stalin and emphasized Lenin, the Communist Party, and the unity of the Soviet peoples. This restored version remained in use until the dissolution of the Soviet Union.

---

### Use During the Dissolution and Aftermath

As the Soviet Union entered a period of political and economic crisis in the late 1980s, the anthem retained its official status, though it became increasingly controversial. During **Mikhail Gorbachev’s perestroika and glasnost** reforms, debates emerged about the symbolism of Soviet emblems, including the anthem.

After the **official dissolution of the USSR** on **December 26, 1991**, the Soviet anthem ceased to be used. In the newly independent **Russian Federation**, it was temporarily replaced with **“The Patriotic Song”** by Mikhail Glinka, which lacked official lyrics and did not achieve widespread popularity.

---

### Reuse in Post-Soviet Russia

In **2000**, under **President Vladimir Putin**, the Russian government reintroduced Alexandrov’s melody as the official anthem of Russia, this time with new lyrics written again by **Sergei Mikhalkov**. The decision was politically significant: it signaled a desire to reconcile Soviet heritage with modern Russian identity, combining symbols of past strength with a vision of national unity.

The re-adoption of the Soviet-era anthem music sparked controversy within Russia and among its former Soviet neighbors. Critics viewed it as a regression to authoritarian symbolism, while supporters argued it provided continuity and evoked national pride.

---

### Legacy

The State Anthem of the Soviet Union remains one of the most recognized national anthems globally. Its stirring melody and association with key moments in 20th-century history ensure its continued cultural and political relevance.

In former Soviet republics, its legacy is mixed—revered by some for its historical grandeur and resisted by others as a symbol of past oppression. In Russia, it continues to play a prominent role in state functions, sports events, and commemorations, serving as a reminder of the USSR’s enduring impact on Russian national identity.

The anthem, in its various forms, offers a lens into the complex evolution of Soviet and post-Soviet political culture, illustrating how music and symbolism are deployed to shape and reflect national narratives.
