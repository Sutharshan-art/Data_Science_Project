**Air Force History of Iran**

The **air force history of Iran** encompasses the development, transformation, and political role of Iranian air power from the early 20th century through the modern Islamic Republic. Shaped by geopolitical shifts, revolutions, and regional conflicts, Iran’s air force has evolved from a Western-trained institution under the monarchy to a domestically oriented force driven by self-reliance following the 1979 Islamic Revolution. The air force has played a critical role in Iran’s national defense strategy and continues to reflect the broader political trajectory of the Iranian state.

### Origins and Early Development

Iran’s air force origins date back to the **Pahlavi dynasty**, when efforts to modernize the military included the creation of an air arm. The **Imperial Iranian Air Force (IIAF)** was formally established in the **1920s**, with the acquisition of aircraft from European countries, notably France and Germany. Under **Reza Shah Pahlavi**, Iran’s military modernization was a central aspect of state-building, and air power was seen as a symbol of national modernization.

After **World War II**, Iran shifted its military alignment toward the **United States**, especially under **Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi**, Reza Shah’s son. The Cold War context, coupled with Iran’s strategic location, made the country a key U.S. ally in the Middle East. This alliance led to rapid expansion and professionalization of the IIAF during the 1950s–1970s.

By the mid-1970s, the Imperial Iranian Air Force had become one of the most advanced in the region, equipped with cutting-edge American aircraft such as the **F-4 Phantom II**, **F-5 Freedom Fighter**, and the **F-14 Tomcat**, making Iran the only country outside the United States to operate the F-14. The IIAF had a close operational relationship with the U.S. Air Force, and American advisers played a major role in training and logistics.

### The Islamic Revolution and Restructuring

The **1979 Islamic Revolution** marked a turning point in Iran’s military structure and political ideology. The fall of the Pahlavi monarchy and the rise of the **Islamic Republic** under **Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini** led to a purge of many officers associated with the Shah. The revolutionary leadership viewed the air force with suspicion, considering it a symbol of Western influence and royalist power.

The newly renamed **Islamic Republic of Iran Air Force (IRIAF)** faced major operational and organizational challenges. Many senior pilots and engineers were executed, imprisoned, or exiled, leading to a significant loss of expertise. Furthermore, the breakdown in relations with the United States following the **U.S. Embassy hostage crisis** resulted in a halt to spare parts and maintenance support, severely affecting Iran’s advanced aircraft fleet.

Despite these setbacks, the IRIAF remained a crucial element of Iran’s defense strategy. Efforts were made to restore operational capabilities through indigenous maintenance programs and reliance on clandestine procurement channels, often involving third-party countries.

### Iran–Iraq War and Air Force Resilience

The **Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988)** was the most significant military engagement for the IRIAF in the post-revolution period. Iraq’s invasion in September 1980 forced Iran to mobilize all branches of its military, including the air force, which played a key role in strategic bombing, air defense, and ground support operations.

Despite being constrained by limited supplies and an aging fleet, the IRIAF managed to mount an effective defense. Notable operations included early strikes on Iraqi airfields and industrial infrastructure, as well as support for ground forces during key battles. The F-14s, in particular, proved instrumental in maintaining air superiority, even with dwindling resources.

The war also led to the creation and expansion of the **Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Aerospace Force (IRGC-AF)**, a parallel air power institution that would eventually focus on ballistic missile development and drone operations, reinforcing the bifurcation of Iran’s military structure.

### Post-War Developments and Self-Reliance

Following the end of the war, Iran placed renewed emphasis on **military self-sufficiency**, particularly in the aviation sector. Under the supervision of the **Ministry of Defense and Armed Forces Logistics (MODAFL)** and the **Iran Aviation Industries Organization (IAIO)**, Iran began developing and producing indigenous aircraft, reverse-engineering foreign models, and expanding its drone and missile capabilities.

In the 1990s and 2000s, Iran turned to alternative partners such as **Russia**, **China**, and **North Korea** to acquire spare parts and new platforms. Iran received **MiG-29s**, **Su-24s**, and **Il-76s** from Russia, while also absorbing over **100 Iraqi aircraft** that fled to Iran during the 1991 Gulf War.

Domestically developed aircraft, such as the **Azarakhsh**, **Saeqeh**, and **Kowsar**, were introduced with limited capabilities, serving mostly symbolic and tactical purposes. The IRIAF also focused on expanding pilot training and domestic radar and avionics systems.

### Modern Role and Strategic Significance

In the 21st century, the Iranian air force plays a largely defensive and deterrent role, with limited offensive reach. Its capabilities are constrained by aging equipment, limited access to international arms markets due to **UN sanctions** and **U.S.-led embargoes**, and internal political divisions over military doctrine.

Nevertheless, the IRIAF remains central to Iran’s **integrated air defense system**, coordinating with the **IRGC Aerospace Force**, **civil aviation**, and air defense units. In recent years, Iran has emphasized the use of **unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)** and **ballistic missiles** to project power, particularly in regional conflicts such as Syria and in tensions with the United States and Israel.

### Conclusion

The air force history of Iran reflects the broader political evolution of the Iranian state, from pro-Western monarchy to revolutionary theocracy. Despite external isolation and internal upheaval, Iran’s air force has demonstrated resilience and adaptability, maintaining a significant if limited role in national defense. Its trajectory continues to shape Iran’s military posture, regional influence, and engagement with global powers.
