**Iraq–Yemen Relations**

Iraq–Yemen relations encompass the diplomatic, political, and economic interactions between the Republic of Iraq and the Republic of Yemen. These ties have been shaped by shared membership in Arab and Islamic organizations, pan-Arab and Baʿthist ideological currents, Cold War alignments, and regional upheavals—most notably the Gulf Wars and the Yemeni civil conflict. Relations have evolved through distinct phases: the pre-unification era when North and South Yemen maintained separate ties; the post-1990 unification period; and the post-2003 realignment in Iraq and the ongoing crisis in Yemen.

### Early Contacts and Pan-Arab Context

In the 1960s, both Yemeni states and Iraq pursued pan-Arab goals, but on divergent paths. The Yemen Arab Republic (North Yemen), established after the 1962 revolution, maintained cautious relations with Iraq’s Hashemite monarchy and, following the 1958 Baʿthist coup in Iraq, developed ties with the Arab nationalist Baʿth Party. Meanwhile, the People’s Democratic Republic of Yemen (South Yemen), founded in 1967, aligned with the Soviet Union and supported Marxist-Leninist movements. Baghdad under Saddam Hussein’s Baʿth regime (from 1968) offered diplomatic recognition and limited economic cooperation to both Yemeni governments, balancing ideological affinity with strategic interests in the Red Sea corridor.

### Baʿthist Solidarity and Ideological Alignment

During the 1970s and 1980s, Iraq extended financial and military aid to like-minded regimes in the region. North Yemen’s government sought arms and training from Iraq to counter royalist insurgencies and South Yemeni guerrillas along the border. Conversely, South Yemen provided sanctuary to Iraqi Baʿthist opponents and facilitated Soviet naval visits to Aden, complicating Baghdad’s calculations. Despite these tensions, Iraq and North Yemen signed a friendship and cooperation treaty in 1977, pledging mutual defense and closer economic integration, reflecting Saddam’s ambition to lead the Arab nationalist bloc.

### Yemeni Unification and Gulf War Aftermath (1990s)

The unification of North and South Yemen on 22 May 1990 into the Republic of Yemen occurred days before Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait. Yemen’s decision—under President Ali Abdullah Saleh—to oppose the U.S.-led coalition and call for Arab mediation led to Riyadh expelling half a million Yemeni workers and suspending aid, severely weakening Yemen’s economy. Iraq, by contrast, welcomed Yemen’s at-least tacit endorsement of its position, sending humanitarian relief and diplomatic support in Arab forums. Following Iraq’s expulsion from Kuwait in early 1991, Baghdad faced UN sanctions; Yemen, burdened by its losses, was among the Arab states most critical of the embargo, further binding Yemen to Iraq amid shared grievances.

### Diplomatic Reengagement Post-2003

The 2003 U.S. invasion of Iraq and the overthrow of Saddam Hussein ended the Baʿthist era. Iraq’s subsequent transitional governments sought to reestablish relations across the Arab world. In 2004, Iraq and Yemen exchanged ambassadors and signed agreements on cultural, technical, and trade cooperation. Both countries joined the Arab Cooperation Council and participated in joint initiatives within the Arab League and the Organization of Islamic Cooperation. Yemeni pilgrims to Shiʿa holy sites in Iraq increased after the fall of Saddam, reflecting new sectarian openness, while Iraqi businessmen explored Yemen’s port facilities in Aden and Al-Mukalla.

### Economic and Energy Cooperation

Bilateral trade remained modest due to security challenges and underdeveloped infrastructure. Iraq expressed interest in Yemeni crude oil exports to compensate for its own production shortfalls during reconstruction, while Yemen looked to Iraqi expertise in oil refining and natural gas development. In 2007, state energy firms held preliminary talks on joint ventures, although progress stalled amid Yemen’s domestic unrest and Iraq’s security priorities tied to counter-insurgency operations against al-Qaeda affiliates.

### Impact of the Yemeni Civil War (2014–Present)

The outbreak of civil war in Yemen in 2014, pitting the Houthi-Salafi coalition against the internationally recognized government of President Abdrabbuh Mansur Hadi, added complexity to Iraq–Yemen relations. Unlike the Saudi-led coalition—which Iraq officially declined to join—Baghdad maintained diplomatic neutrality, calling for dialogue and respect for Yemen’s sovereignty. Iraq’s foreign ministry issued statements condemning external military intervention and humanitarian suffering, reflecting its own war-weary population’s aversion to foreign entanglements. Baghdad also provided limited humanitarian aid through UN channels but avoided aligning with either Saudi Arabia or Iran in the proxy aspects of Yemen’s conflict.

### Political and Sectarian Dimensions

The rise of Iran’s regional influence—in both Baghdad and among Yemen’s Houthi movement—has introduced sectarian overtones into Iraq–Yemen ties. Iraqi Shia militias and political parties, some with close Tehran links, have expressed ideological solidarity with Yemen’s Zaidi Shia community, while Sunni political actors in Iraq have sympathized with Yemen’s Sunni factions. The Iraqi government, however, has officially pursued a policy of non-alignment, seeking to preserve unity among its diverse domestic and external partners.

### Humanitarian and Cultural Exchange

Prior to the civil war, Iraq and Yemen facilitated student exchanges and cultural delegations, emphasizing shared Arab heritage and joint archaeological interests along the ancient incense route. Since 2015, Iraq contributed modest funding to UN humanitarian appeals for Yemen, and Iraqi NGOs have sent medical teams to assist displaced populations in Aden and Taiz. Yemeni refugees have found temporary refuge in Iraq, although numbers remained small compared to flows elsewhere in the region.

### Future Prospects

The eventual resolution of Yemen’s conflict will determine the trajectory of Iraq–Yemen relations. Stabilization in Yemen could open opportunities for reconstruction cooperation, port and logistics collaboration, and renewed energy partnerships. For Iraq, deepening ties with Yemen would support its broader Arab integration strategy and diversify its diplomatic portfolio. Both countries’ shared experiences of war, sanctions, and internal division may provide a basis for pragmatic solidarity, even as regional rivalries continue to test their bilateral rapport.
