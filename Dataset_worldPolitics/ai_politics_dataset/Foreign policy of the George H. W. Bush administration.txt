**Foreign Policy of the George H. W. Bush Administration**

The foreign policy of the George H. W. Bush administration (1989–1993) was defined by the end of the Cold War, the collapse of the Soviet Union, and the administration’s management of the first Gulf War. President Bush guided the United States through a period of rapid geopolitical change, emphasizing coalition-building, diplomatic engagement, and cautious use of military power.

### End of the Cold War and German Reunification

Upon taking office in January 1989, Bush inherited the latter stages of the Cold War and a Soviet Union under Mikhail Gorbachev pursuing glasnost and perestroika. The administration supported these reforms, combining encouragement of human rights with assurances that NATO would not exploit Soviet weakness. During the fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989, the administration worked closely with West German Chancellor Helmut Kohl and European partners to manage German reunification. Through the “Two-Plus-Four” negotiations (the two Germanys plus the four Allied powers), Bush secured Soviet acquiescence to a unified Germany’s NATO membership, which was formalized in 1990.

### Dissolution of the Soviet Union

As republics declared independence in 1990–1991, Bush balanced support for self-determination with the continuity of arms control. The administration negotiated the Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (START I), signed in July 1991, reducing the U.S. and Soviet strategic arsenals. After the failed August 1991 coup against Gorbachev, Bush recognized the independence of the Baltic states and, following the December 1991 dissolution of the USSR, established diplomatic relations with the newly independent republics. The administration launched assistance programs (e.g., the Freedom Support Act) to aid their transition to market economies and democratic governance.

### Persian Gulf War

The defining military engagement of Bush’s presidency was the 1990–1991 Persian Gulf War. After Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait in August 1990, Bush secured U.N. Security Council resolutions demanding withdrawal and authorizing force. He assembled a 34–nation coalition—including Arab and Western states—and undertook Operation Desert Shield to defend Saudi Arabia. On 17 January 1991, Operation Desert Storm commenced, employing overwhelming air power and a ground assault that liberated Kuwait within 100 hours. The decisive victory reinforced U.S. leadership in the post–Cold War order but left Saddam Hussein in power in Iraq, setting the stage for future conflicts.

### Latin America and the Caribbean

Bush continued the Cold War–era focus on hemispheric security. In December 1989, the administration launched Operation Just Cause, a U.S. invasion of Panama to depose General Manuel Noriega, citing the defense of the Panama Canal Treaties, protection of U.S. citizens, and drug trafficking concerns. Noriega was captured and later tried in the United States. The administration also deepened engagement with Mexico on trade liberalization, laying groundwork for the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), signed under Bush in November 1992 (though ratified later).

### Asia and the Pacific

In East Asia, Bush sought to maintain stability amid China’s rise and the waning of Soviet influence. Following the 1989 Tiananmen Square crackdown, he balanced human rights criticism with strategic engagement, continuing arms sales and high-level contacts to keep lines of communication open. The administration strengthened alliances with Japan, South Korea, and Australia, and supported ASEAN’s regional role. In 1991, Bush recognized the end of the Vietnam War’s legacy by normalizing relations with Vietnam, subject to human rights progress.

### Europe and NATO Expansion

Beyond German reunification, Bush reinforced U.S. commitments to NATO and European integration. He supported the enlargement of the European Community (later the European Union) to include Spain and Portugal, and boosted NATO’s “Partnership for Peace” program to integrate Central and Eastern European nations without immediate alliance membership. His administration’s approach combined security guarantees with political and economic incentives, setting the stage for post–Bush EU and NATO enlargements.

### Middle East Peace Efforts

Bush inherited the Reagan administration’s role in the Arab–Israeli peace process. In October 1991, he convened the Madrid Conference—co-sponsored with the Soviet Union and involving Israel, Palestine Liberation Organization observers, and neighboring Arab states—to launch bilateral peace talks. While comprehensive peace remained elusive, the framework established during Bush’s term facilitated later agreements such as the Oslo Accords.

### Humanitarian and Multilateral Engagement

The Bush administration emphasized multilateralism through the United Nations and regional organizations. It supported U.N. peacekeeping in Namibia and Cambodia, and provided substantial humanitarian assistance following the 1991 Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan and the Somali famine crisis. Bush also championed global nonproliferation, securing the 1991 ratification of the Chemical Weapons Convention and negotiating the 1992 extension of the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty.

### Legacy and Assessment

George H. W. Bush’s foreign policy is often praised for its prudent leadership during a transformative era. His administration managed the peaceful conclusion of the Cold War, prevented wider regional conflagrations through coalition diplomacy, and maintained American credibility by securing swift victory in the Gulf. Critics, however, note missed opportunities in the Arab–Israeli conflict and the decision not to overthrow Saddam Hussein, which some argue contributed to future instability. Nonetheless, Bush’s emphasis on alliances, measured use of force, and multilateral institutions influenced U.S. strategy into the 21st century.
