**Clinton–Lewinsky Scandal**

The **Clinton–Lewinsky scandal** was a political and legal controversy in the United States that emerged in 1998 involving then-President **Bill Clinton** and **Monica Lewinsky**, a 22-year-old White House intern. The scandal centered on allegations of a sexual relationship between Clinton and Lewinsky, subsequent efforts by Clinton to conceal the affair, and accusations that he committed perjury and obstructed justice. It ultimately led to Clinton’s impeachment by the U.S. House of Representatives, though he was acquitted by the Senate and remained in office.

The scandal had significant implications for American politics, media, and law. It fueled intense partisan debate, raised questions about personal conduct in public office, and contributed to broader cultural discussions about privacy, power, and accountability in the late 1990s.

---

### Background

Monica Lewinsky began working as an unpaid intern in the White House Office of Legislative Affairs in 1995 and was later hired as a paid staffer in the Office of the Chief of Staff. During her time in the White House, she and President Clinton engaged in what both parties later acknowledged was a consensual sexual relationship, which reportedly occurred between 1995 and 1997.

The affair became politically consequential when Lewinsky confided in **Linda Tripp**, a Pentagon employee and former White House colleague, who secretly recorded their conversations. Tripp turned the tapes over to **Independent Counsel Kenneth Starr**, who was already investigating Clinton’s involvement in the Whitewater real estate controversy and other matters.

---

### Legal and Political Developments

In January 1998, media reports began circulating that President Clinton had engaged in an affair with a former intern and encouraged her to lie under oath. The story gained national attention after it was reported by the **Drudge Report**, followed by coverage in mainstream outlets such as *The Washington Post* and *Newsweek*.

Clinton initially denied the allegations, most famously declaring during a White House press conference: *"I did not have sexual relations with that woman, Miss Lewinsky."* However, forensic evidence, including a blue dress belonging to Lewinsky that contained traces of Clinton’s DNA, eventually contradicted his public denials.

Clinton was questioned under oath in a deposition related to a sexual harassment lawsuit filed by **Paula Jones**, a former Arkansas state employee. During the deposition, he denied having a sexual relationship with Lewinsky. When Starr obtained evidence suggesting otherwise, he expanded his investigation to include possible perjury and obstruction of justice.

In August 1998, Clinton testified before a grand jury, becoming the first sitting U.S. president to do so. Later that day, he addressed the nation and admitted to an “inappropriate relationship” with Lewinsky.

---

### Impeachment Proceedings

The controversy culminated in the impeachment of President Clinton by the **U.S. House of Representatives** on **December 19, 1998**. The House approved two articles of impeachment:

1. **Perjury before a grand jury**
2. **Obstruction of justice**

Two additional articles—another perjury charge and abuse of power—were rejected.

The impeachment trial was held in the **U.S. Senate** in January 1999. After weeks of proceedings, the Senate acquitted Clinton on both charges on **February 12, 1999**. The perjury charge failed by a vote of 45–55, and the obstruction of justice charge failed 50–50, both well short of the two-thirds majority required for conviction and removal from office.

Clinton completed his term, leaving office in January 2001 with high approval ratings despite the scandal.

---

### Impact and Legacy

The Clinton–Lewinsky scandal had far-reaching consequences for American political culture, legal precedent, and media practices:

* **Partisan Polarization**: The scandal deepened partisan divisions in Washington. Republicans accused Clinton of undermining the rule of law, while many Democrats defended him, arguing that his private misconduct did not warrant removal from office.

* **Rise of Political Media**: The scandal marked a turning point in media coverage of politics, with 24-hour cable news channels and emerging online outlets playing a significant role in shaping public opinion.

* **Legal and Ethical Debates**: The case raised questions about the boundaries of executive privilege, the role of independent counsels, and the definition of perjury and obstruction in the context of private conduct.

* **Effect on Hillary Clinton**: Then-First Lady **Hillary Clinton** became a prominent public figure in the scandal’s aftermath. She stood by her husband during the ordeal and later launched a successful political career, serving as U.S. Senator, Secretary of State, and Democratic presidential nominee in 2016.

* **Cultural Discourse**: The scandal sparked broader societal debates over workplace relationships, sexual misconduct, and abuse of power. Lewinsky, once vilified in public discourse, later re-emerged as an anti-bullying advocate and writer, reshaping her public image in the context of evolving cultural norms.

---

### Conclusion

The Clinton–Lewinsky scandal was a defining political event of the late 20th century, intertwining issues of law, morality, partisanship, and media sensationalism. While President Clinton survived impeachment and remained popular with much of the public, the episode underscored the vulnerability of political institutions to personal scandal and marked a shift in the tone and intensity of political conflict in the United States. The scandal’s legacy continues to influence American political discourse, particularly in discussions about presidential accountability and media scrutiny.
