**History of the Soviet Union**

The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) existed from 1922 to 1991 as a federal socialist state spanning Eastern Europe and northern Asia. Born of the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution, the Soviet Union became a global superpower, undergoing radical political, economic, and social transformations under communist rule.

---

## Origins and Formation (1917–1924)

Following the February 1917 overthrow of the Romanov monarchy, a Provisional Government shared power with workers’ and soldiers’ councils, or Soviets. In October 1917, Lenin’s Bolsheviks seized control, withdrew from World War I via the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (1918), and fought a multi-front civil war (1918–1921) against White forces and foreign interventionists. In December 1922, the Russian, Ukrainian, Byelorussian, and Transcaucasian Soviet republics united under the USSR’s founding treaty, establishing a federal constitution and enshrining the Communist Party’s political monopoly.

---

## Lenin’s New Economic Policy and Party Consolidation (1921–1928)

The **New Economic Policy (NEP)**, introduced in 1921, reintroduced limited private trade and small-scale private ownership to revive a war-ravaged economy. Under this mixed system, heavy industry and banking remained state-controlled, while peasants could sell surplus grain. Concurrently, intra‐party struggles began; after Lenin’s **death in 1924**, Joseph Stalin outmaneuvered rivals—Trotsky, Zinoviev, Bukharin—to consolidate power by 1928.

---

## Stalinism: Industrialization, Collectivization, and the Great Purge (1928–1939)

In 1928, Stalin replaced NEP with the **First Five-Year Plan**, prioritizing rapid industrialization and forced **collectivization** of agriculture. State targets sought dramatic increases in steel, coal, and machinery output; however, agricultural requisitions and dekulakization triggered widespread famine, notably the Holodomor in Ukraine (1932–33), causing millions of deaths.

Simultaneously, political repression intensified. The **Great Purge** (1936–38) saw show trials of Old Bolsheviks and mass arrests by the NKVD, resulting in hundreds of thousands of executions and millions deported to Gulag labor camps. These campaigns eliminated perceived “enemies of the people” and solidified Stalin’s absolute authority.

---

## World War II and Emergence as Superpower (1939–1953)

The 1939 **Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact** initially aligned the USSR with Nazi Germany, allowing Soviet annexations in Eastern Europe. Germany’s 1941 invasion (Operation Barbarossa) provoked a massive Soviet war effort. After staggering early losses, Red Army victories at Stalingrad (1942–43) and Kursk (1943) turned the tide. The USSR bore the brunt of Allied casualties—over 20 million—and emerged in 1945 as a victor occupying Eastern Europe.

Postwar, Stalin established Soviet‐aligned governments across the former German-occupied zone, laying the groundwork for the Cold War. Reconstruction under the **Fourth Five-Year Plan** emphasized heavy industry over consumer goods, while political conformity was enforced through renewed purges and censorship. Stalin’s death in 1953 triggered a brief power struggle, ultimately leading to Nikita Khrushchev’s leadership.

---

## De-Stalinization and the Khrushchev Thaw (1953–1964)

Khrushchev’s 1956 “Secret Speech” denounced Stalin’s cult of personality and abuses, inaugurating **de-Stalinization**. The **Khrushchev Thaw** relaxed censorship, released political prisoners, and allowed greater cultural and intellectual freedom. Economically, Khrushchev sought to improve living standards through investments in agriculture and housing. Foreign‐policy initiatives included the **peaceful coexistence** doctrine, Sputnik (1957) in the Space Race, and support for decolonizing movements in the Global South.

---

## Brezhnev Era and Stagnation (1964–1982)

In 1964, Leonid Brezhnev replaced Khrushchev, ushering in a period of political stability often termed the “Era of Stagnation.” Economic growth slowed as bureaucratic inertia stifled innovation; widespread corruption and the **nomenklatura** system entrenched elite privilege. The **Prague Spring** (1968) and its suppression by Warsaw Pact forces underscored limits on political liberalization. Meanwhile, détente with the United States produced arms-control treaties (SALT I, 1972) and increased trade, even as the **Soviet–Afghan War** (1979–1989) drained resources and tarnished the USSR’s international standing.

---

## Gorbachev’s Reforms and Dissolution (1985–1991)

Mikhail Gorbachev became General Secretary in 1985, launching **perestroika** (economic restructuring) and **glasnost** (political openness) to revive the system. Market‐oriented reforms and reduced censorship empowered public debate, but also unleashed nationalist movements in the Baltic states, Ukraine, and the Caucasus. Gorbachev’s withdrawal from Afghanistan (completed 1989) and acquiescence to Eastern European independence (1989) marked the end of Soviet hegemony in the Eastern Bloc.

A failed hard‐line **coup in August 1991** weakened Gorbachev. Republican leaders, notably Boris Yeltsin in Russia, asserted sovereignty. In December 1991, the **Belovezha Accords** formally dissolved the USSR, establishing the Commonwealth of Independent States in its place and ending the Soviet experiment.

---

Over seven decades, the Soviet Union transformed from a revolutionary ideal into a global superpower and ultimately collapsed under the weight of economic inefficiency, political repression, and nationalist ferment. Its legacy continues to shape post‐Soviet states and global geopolitics.
