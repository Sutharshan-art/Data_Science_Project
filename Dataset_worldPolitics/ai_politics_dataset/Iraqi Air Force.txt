**Iraqi Air Force**

The Iraqi Air Force (IQAF) is the aerial warfare branch of the Iraqi Armed Forces. Established in the 1930s under the Kingdom of Iraq, it has undergone multiple reorganizations reflecting the country’s shifting political landscape—from monarchy to military republic to Baʿthist dictatorship and, after 2003, a democratically elected government. Throughout its history, the IQAF has played a central role in internal power struggles, regional conflicts, and efforts to project state authority.

### Origins and Early Development (1931–1958)

The roots of the IQAF date to April 1931, when Iraq ordered its first aircraft—six de Havilland D.H.9A biplanes—from Britain. Formally established as the Royal Iraqi Air Force in 1933, it initially operated under British officers seconded to train Iraqi pilots and ground crews. During World War II, the RIrAF supported the Allied occupation of Iraq to prevent a pro-Axis coup, then participated in operations against Vichy French forces in Syria. Postwar expansion included acquisition of British-built Hawker Audax and Gloster Gladiator fighters.

In 1955 Iraq joined the pro-Western Baghdad Pact, receiving jet aircraft such as the de Havilland Vampire. By the late 1950s the force numbered some 750 personnel and a mix of piston and early jet types, reflecting Iraq’s role as a Western-aligned kingdom.

### Republican Era and Baʿthist Consolidation (1958–1979)

The military coup of July 1958 abolished the monarchy and inaugurated a republic. The newly styled Iraqi Air Force expanded rapidly under successive regimes, acquiring United States–built Lockheed T-33 trainers and Martin B-57 Canberra bombers by the early 1960s. Under the Baʿth Party’s rise to power in 1968, the IQAF became both an instrument of external defense and a pillar of regime security. Political reliability of senior officers was ensured through loyalty screenings and party indoctrination.

In the 1970s, a turning point came with large-scale arms purchases from the Soviet Union and France, including MiG-21 fighters, MiG-23 interceptors, Sukhoi Su-22 ground-attack jets, and Dassault Mirage F1 fighters. By 1979, the IQAF numbered over 80 combat aircraft squadrons, reflecting Iraq’s ambition to become the dominant air power in the Persian Gulf.

### Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988)

During the Iran–Iraq War, the IQAF conducted offensive and defensive operations in support of ground campaigns. Early strikes targeted Iranian airfields and oil facilities, while subsequent engagements focused on air superiority over the Shatt al-Arab and close air support. Iraqi pilots flying MiG-23s and Mirage F1s claimed several air-to-air victories, though the conflict also exposed limitations in pilot training and maintenance logistics under wartime attrition. The IQAF’s extensive use of French-built Super Étendard aircraft armed with Exocet anti-ship missiles in the “Tanker War” phase demonstrated its evolving maritime strike capability.

### Gulf War and Sanctions (1990–2003)

Following Iraq’s 1990 invasion of Kuwait, coalition air campaigns in Operation Desert Storm inflicted heavy damage on IQAF infrastructure. Most frontline aircraft were destroyed on the ground or in air-to-air combat with coalition fighters. In the war’s aftermath, UN Security Council resolutions imposed no-fly zones over northern and southern Iraq, enforced by U.S., British, and French aircraft to protect Kurdish and Shia populations. Economic sanctions and weapon embargoes severely constrained the IQAF’s ability to modernize or maintain its surviving fleet, reducing it to a largely defensive, limited-capability force by the early 2000s.

### Post-2003 Reconstitution and Modernization

The U.S.-led invasion of Iraq in 2003 resulted in the dissolution of the Iraqi Armed Forces, including the IQAF. Reconstruction began under the Coalition Provisional Authority, with new recruitment and training programs. In 2005, the IQAF was formally reestablished, initially operating legacy aircraft still serviceable from the Baʿthist-era inventory, including MiG-21s and Cessna 172 reconnaissance planes.

From 2008 onward, Iraq signed contracts to modernize its air arm, acquiring L-159 Alca light combat jets from the Czech Republic; F-16 Fighting Falcons from the United States; and AgustaWestland AW139 helicopters for transport and search-and-rescue. Training partnerships with Italy, the United States, and Poland facilitated pilot conversion to Western platforms. By the mid-2010s, the IQAF had achieved modest capability in air policing, border surveillance, and limited close air support against internal insurgent threats.

### Role in Counter-Insurgency and Counter-IS Operations

Facing the rise of the Islamic State (IS) from 2014, the IQAF deployed transport and attack helicopters, as well as L-159 jets, to support Iraqi Army and Kurdish Peshmerga offensives. While its early performance was hampered by limited precision-strike munitions, the subsequent delivery of GBU-38 GPS-guided bombs and Paveway II laser-guided bombs increased effectiveness in urban combat. By 2017, IQAF close air support missions were integral to recapturing Mosul and other IS-held territories, demonstrating the air arm’s renewed operational relevance.

### Organizational Structure and Bases

Headquartered at Baghdad International Airport, the IQAF is organized into three regional air commands—Northern, Central, and Southern—each overseeing fighter, transport, and helicopter wings. Key air bases include Balad Air Base (north of Baghdad), Al Masbah Air Base near Basra, and Ali Air Base in the west. A training wing at Taji Air Base hosts initial flight screening and basic instructor courses, while maintenance depots are spread across former Baʿthist-era facilities.

### Challenges and Future Prospects

Sustaining pilot proficiency, maintenance throughput, and logistics for a mixed fleet remain enduring challenges. Corruption, political interference in procurement, and periodic budget shortfalls have hindered expansion plans. Nonetheless, ongoing acquisition of UH-60M Black Hawk helicopters and proposals for new multirole fighters suggest a continued modernization trajectory. Deepening cooperation with U.S. and European air forces through joint exercises and liaison officers aims to further professionalize the IQAF and integrate it into regional security frameworks.

Throughout its history, the Iraqi Air Force has mirrored Iraq’s political transformations—from monarchy to republic to authoritarianism and democratic transition—serving both as a tool of regime power and as a key instrument of national defense. Its evolving role today reflects Iraq’s efforts to rebuild sovereign capability and restore stability in a challenging regional environment.
