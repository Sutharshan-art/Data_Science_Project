**Manuel Noriega**

**Manuel Antonio Noriega Moreno** (February 11, 1934 – May 29, 2017) was a Panamanian military officer and de facto ruler of Panama from 1983 to 1989. Initially a close ally of the United States and a key player in regional intelligence operations during the Cold War, Noriega later became an adversary of the U.S. government, culminating in his ouster following the U.S. invasion of Panama in December 1989. His rule was characterized by authoritarian governance, electoral manipulation, suppression of dissent, and his entanglement in international drug trafficking.

Noriega’s rise and fall exemplify the complex intersection of U.S. foreign policy, Cold War strategy, and Latin American military politics. Though once regarded as a strategic asset by Washington, Noriega’s actions eventually led to a dramatic rupture in relations with the United States and his prosecution on multiple charges.

---

### Early Life and Military Career

Manuel Noriega was born in Panama City and raised in poverty. He received a military education in Peru and was later trained at the U.S.-run **School of the Americas** in the Panama Canal Zone, where he specialized in intelligence and psychological operations. Noriega’s early career was marked by his alignment with Panama’s National Guard and his support for General **Omar Torrijos**, who seized power in a 1968 coup.

By the 1970s, Noriega had risen to prominence within the Panamanian military and was appointed chief of military intelligence. During this period, he began cooperating with U.S. intelligence services, particularly the **Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)**, which viewed him as a useful intermediary in regional affairs.

---

### Relationship with the United States

Throughout the 1970s and early 1980s, Noriega served as a valued asset to U.S. intelligence agencies. He provided assistance on matters related to Cuba, Nicaragua, and Colombia, and was instrumental in supporting U.S. interests in Latin America. Despite his known involvement in illicit activities, including drug trafficking and arms smuggling, Noriega received funding and protection from U.S. authorities due to his strategic utility.

Noriega maintained a delicate balancing act, cultivating ties with both U.S. officials and regional actors such as Colombia’s **Medellín cartel**. His increasing authoritarianism and criminal activity eventually led to growing tensions with the United States, especially after the death of Torrijos in 1981 and Noriega’s formal assumption of power in 1983 as head of the **Panama Defense Forces (PDF)**.

---

### De Facto Rule and Domestic Politics

Although Panama remained nominally democratic during Noriega’s tenure, he exercised near-total control through his command of the military and manipulation of state institutions. Opposition parties were repressed, journalists were harassed, and political rivals were frequently imprisoned or exiled.

In 1984, Noriega orchestrated a controversial presidential election in which his preferred candidate narrowly won amid allegations of fraud. Public dissatisfaction grew throughout the 1980s, culminating in widespread protests and international condemnation.

Noriega also used Panama as a transit point for narcotics trafficking, allegedly profiting personally from his ties with drug cartels. His involvement in money laundering, illegal arms trade, and internal repression led to the imposition of U.S. sanctions and a sharp deterioration in U.S.-Panama relations.

---

### U.S. Invasion and Capture

By 1988, U.S. federal courts had indicted Noriega on drug trafficking charges. Diplomatic efforts to persuade him to resign failed, and tensions escalated. In December 1989, following the killing of a U.S. Marine officer in Panama and other provocations, President **George H. W. Bush** ordered **Operation Just Cause**, the largest U.S. military intervention since Vietnam.

The invasion resulted in the defeat of the Panamanian military and the installation of a U.S.-backed civilian government. Noriega took refuge in the **Vatican diplomatic mission** in Panama City but eventually surrendered in January 1990. He was taken to the United States and stood trial in Miami.

---

### Imprisonment and Extraditions

In 1992, Noriega was convicted in a U.S. court on charges of drug trafficking, racketeering, and money laundering. He was sentenced to 40 years in prison, later reduced for good behavior and cooperation. After serving 17 years, he was extradited to France in 2010, where he was convicted on additional charges related to money laundering.

In 2011, France agreed to extradite Noriega to Panama, where he faced multiple convictions in absentia for human rights abuses, including the torture and killing of political opponents. He remained imprisoned in Panama until his health declined.

---

### Death and Legacy

Manuel Noriega died on May 29, 2017, at the age of 83, following complications from brain surgery. His death marked the end of one of the most controversial chapters in Panamanian and Latin American political history.

Noriega’s legacy remains deeply contested. While some in Panama remember him as a nationalist who defended the country’s sovereignty—especially during negotiations over the Panama Canal—many others view his rule as a dark period marked by corruption, repression, and subservience to foreign interests.

In U.S. history, Noriega is emblematic of the ethical compromises made during the Cold War, when strategic alliances often trumped democratic principles. His story illustrates the perils of intelligence alliances with authoritarian leaders and the unintended consequences of militarized foreign policy in Latin America.
