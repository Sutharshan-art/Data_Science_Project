**Serbian War Crimes**

**Serbian war crimes** refer to acts committed by Serbian military forces, paramilitaries, police units, and other associated actors during the conflicts that followed the disintegration of Yugoslavia in the 1990s, particularly during the wars in Croatia (1991–1995), Bosnia and Herzegovina (1992–1995), and Kosovo (1998–1999). These crimes include ethnic cleansing, mass killings, forced deportations, torture, and sexual violence against civilian populations, primarily targeting non-Serb communities such as Croats, Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims), and Kosovo Albanians.

The prosecution of these crimes became a major focus of international law and post-conflict justice, especially through the establishment of the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) by the United Nations in 1993.

### Background and Historical Context

The collapse of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia in the early 1990s led to a series of ethnically charged conflicts. Serbian political and military leaders pursued efforts to create a “Greater Serbia” by incorporating Serb-inhabited areas of other Yugoslav republics, particularly Croatia and Bosnia and Herzegovina, into a new Serbian-dominated state.

This nationalist agenda led to violent confrontations and widespread atrocities, as Serbian forces, often in cooperation with local Serb militias, carried out operations aimed at expelling non-Serb populations from territories they sought to control. The resulting wars were marked by systematic campaigns of violence against civilians, including acts that have been classified as war crimes and crimes against humanity.

### Croatia (1991–1995)

During the Croatian War of Independence, Serbian forces, including the Yugoslav People's Army (JNA) and local Serb paramilitary groups, were responsible for several war crimes. One of the most well-known incidents was the **Vukovar massacre** in November 1991, in which approximately 260 Croatian prisoners of war and civilians were executed after the fall of the town of Vukovar.

Other crimes included the shelling of civilian areas, forced displacement of non-Serbs, and destruction of cultural and religious sites. Serbian forces controlled about one-third of Croatian territory at the height of the conflict, with reports of systematic abuses committed in these occupied areas.

### Bosnia and Herzegovina (1992–1995)

The Bosnian War saw some of the worst atrocities committed during the Yugoslav wars. The conflict involved Bosniak, Croat, and Serb factions, but the scale and systematic nature of crimes committed by Bosnian Serb forces drew particular international scrutiny. These forces were supported politically and militarily by authorities in Serbia and the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia.

The most notorious war crime was the **Srebrenica massacre** in July 1995, when over 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were executed by units under the command of General Ratko Mladić. This event was later classified as genocide by the ICTY and the International Court of Justice.

Other war crimes included the prolonged **Siege of Sarajevo**, during which thousands of civilians were killed by shelling and sniper fire; ethnic cleansing campaigns in regions such as Prijedor and Foča; and the establishment of detention camps where torture and sexual violence were widespread.

### Kosovo (1998–1999)

During the conflict in Kosovo, Serbian forces and associated paramilitaries engaged in a campaign against the Kosovo Albanian population that involved widespread abuses. The war began as a counterinsurgency operation against the Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA) but escalated into a broader campaign of repression.

Notable incidents include the **Račak massacre**, where 45 Albanian civilians were killed, and the **mass deportations** during NATO’s 1999 air campaign, in which hundreds of thousands of Kosovo Albanians were forcibly displaced. Numerous mass graves were later uncovered across Serbia and Kosovo, containing the remains of civilian victims.

The estimated civilian death toll from Serbian actions in Kosovo ranges from several thousand to over 10,000. Human Rights Watch and other organizations documented evidence of summary executions, sexual violence, and destruction of homes and cultural sites.

### Legal Proceedings and Accountability

The **International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY)** played a central role in prosecuting individuals responsible for war crimes. High-profile figures convicted by the tribunal include:

* **Slobodan Milošević**, President of Serbia and the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, who was indicted for crimes in Bosnia, Croatia, and Kosovo. He died in 2006 before the conclusion of his trial.

* **Radovan Karadžić**, President of Republika Srpska, convicted of genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity.

* **Ratko Mladić**, former commander of the Bosnian Serb Army, sentenced to life imprisonment for genocide and other crimes.

Numerous other military and political leaders, as well as lower-ranking individuals, were prosecuted and convicted for their roles in atrocities. While Serbia has conducted some domestic trials, international organizations have criticized the pace and scope of domestic prosecutions.

### Political Repercussions and Legacy

The legacy of war crimes continues to shape political discourse in Serbia and the wider Balkans. While some segments of Serbian society and political leadership have acknowledged past crimes, others continue to deny or minimize them. The glorification of convicted war criminals in parts of the public and political sphere has drawn criticism from international observers.

Efforts at regional reconciliation remain challenged by competing national narratives, contested interpretations of history, and unresolved issues related to justice, reparations, and the fate of missing persons. The prosecution and acknowledgment of war crimes remain central to Serbia’s relations with neighboring countries and its aspirations for European Union membership.
