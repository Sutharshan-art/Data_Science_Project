**Psychic TV Discography**

**Psychic TV** is a British experimental video art and music collective formed in 1981 by performance artist Genesis P-Orridge and video director Peter Christopherson. Originally conceived as a continuation of the work of Throbbing Gristle and the radical art group COUM Transmissions, Psychic TV became known for its prolific and eclectic output spanning genres such as industrial, psychedelic rock, acid house, and electronic music. The group's discography reflects not only a wide range of musical experimentation but also a complex interplay of political, cultural, and esoteric themes.

While primarily known as a musical project, Psychic TV was also the audio-visual arm of **Thee Temple ov Psychick Youth (TOPY)**, a countercultural network promoting spiritual exploration, social critique, and individual liberation. Their recorded output, therefore, often functioned as both artistic statement and political provocation, challenging mainstream norms through sound, symbolism, and subversion.

---

### Overview of Discography

Psychic TV's discography is vast and diverse, consisting of studio albums, live recordings, compilations, and video releases. Over the course of the 1980s and 1990s, the band was recognized for releasing more albums in a single year than any other group, an achievement noted by the Guinness Book of World Records.

The group's musical style evolved significantly over time. Their early work (1981–1985) leaned toward post-industrial soundscapes and spoken word, before transitioning into psychedelic rock, acid house, and techno influences in the late 1980s and early 1990s. The project went on hiatus in the mid-1990s but was revived in the 2000s under the name **PTV3**, with a new lineup and musical direction.

---

### Notable Studio Albums

1. **Force the Hand of Chance (1982)** – Psychic TV’s debut studio album, blending industrial soundscapes, orchestral arrangements, and occult themes. It set the tone for the group’s early direction and included contributions from notable collaborators such as Alex Fergusson and Paula P-Orridge.

2. **Dreams Less Sweet (1983)** – Featuring advanced stereo recording techniques and thematically addressing death, ritual, and psychic phenomena. It is often praised for its ambitious sonic production and artistic complexity.

3. **Allegory and Self (1988)** – Marked a shift toward psychedelic pop and featured the track “Godstar,” a homage to Rolling Stones guitarist Brian Jones. The album reflected the group's growing interest in rock-oriented sounds.

4. **Towards Thee Infinite Beat (1990)** – A pivotal album in Psychic TV's acid house phase, aligning with the rave and club scenes in the UK and U.S. The music featured prominent electronic rhythms and minimalistic vocals.

5. **Trip Reset (1996)** – Released during a period of stylistic transformation, this album blended electronic experimentation with ambient and pop textures. It signaled the end of the second major phase of the group before their extended hiatus.

6. **Hell Is Invisible... Heaven Is Her/e (2007)** – The first release by PTV3, showcasing a revitalized band under Genesis P-Orridge's leadership. The album combined rock, avant-garde, and spiritual motifs and reflected the group’s evolving approach to identity and performance.

---

### Live and Compilation Releases

Psychic TV was known for its extensive live recordings, which often featured unique arrangements and spontaneous performances. Many of these were released as part of limited-edition series in the 1980s, including the infamous **“Live Albums Series”**, which contributed to the band’s Guinness record.

Compilations such as **“A Pagan Day” (1984)** and **“Godstar: Thee Director’s Cut” (1992)** provided retrospective collections of rarities, demos, and remixes that served to recontextualize the group’s earlier work.

---

### Political and Cultural Context

Psychic TV's discography cannot be separated from its political and cultural context. The group was deeply influenced by avant-garde art movements, anarchist philosophy, and anti-establishment ideologies. The use of media as a tool for consciousness alteration was central to their mission, and many releases were deliberately designed to challenge conventional morality, sexual norms, and consumer culture.

The band’s association with Thee Temple ov Psychick Youth brought additional controversy and scrutiny, particularly in the late 1980s, when Genesis P-Orridge faced media allegations of ritual abuse—a claim that led to police raids and P-Orridge's eventual self-imposed exile from the UK. These events deeply impacted the group's output and public reception during the 1990s.

Despite the controversies, Psychic TV remained committed to pushing boundaries through sound and performance. Their work intersected with emerging subcultures such as punk, goth, rave, and queer activism, and their use of gender-fluid imagery and themes of transformation resonated with later developments in cultural theory and identity politics.

---

### Legacy

Psychic TV's discography stands as a testament to the power of music as a vehicle for experimentation, resistance, and redefinition. Their refusal to conform to genre expectations or commercial formats made them both influential and marginal in the broader musical landscape.

Their recordings, reissued periodically by labels like Cold Spring and Sacred Bones, continue to influence artists across electronic, industrial, and experimental genres. The group’s use of symbolism, mysticism, and radical politics places them within a lineage of countercultural art that remains relevant to discussions on the intersection of music, media, and ideology.

Following the death of Genesis P-Orridge in 2020, Psychic TV’s body of work remains a provocative archive of audio-visual resistance, documenting over three decades of cultural dissent.
