**Cold War (1985–1991)**

The period from **1985 to 1991** marks the final phase of the **Cold War**, culminating in the **dissolution of the Soviet Union** and the end of the decades-long geopolitical and ideological rivalry between the **United States** and the **Soviet Union**. This era was defined by significant political reforms in the USSR under **Mikhail Gorbachev**, a transformation in U.S.–Soviet relations, and the collapse of communist regimes across **Eastern Europe**.

The period witnessed a dramatic thaw in superpower tensions, the signing of landmark arms control agreements, and the rollback of Soviet influence globally. By 1991, the Cold War structure that had dominated world politics since the late 1940s had been fundamentally dismantled.

---

### Gorbachev and Soviet Reforms

In **March 1985**, **Mikhail Gorbachev** became General Secretary of the **Communist Party of the Soviet Union**. Recognizing the stagnation of the Soviet economy and the unsustainable burden of Cold War competition, Gorbachev introduced two key domestic reform policies:

* **Perestroika (restructuring)**: Economic reforms aimed at decentralizing control, introducing limited market mechanisms, and increasing productivity.
* **Glasnost (openness)**: A political reform initiative promoting transparency, freedom of expression, and public debate.

These reforms were intended to revitalize the Soviet system but instead accelerated its destabilization by exposing corruption, economic inefficiency, and long-suppressed dissent.

---

### U.S.–Soviet Rapprochement

Under **U.S. President Ronald Reagan**, who initially pursued a hardline stance against the Soviet Union, a significant shift occurred in the late 1980s. Gorbachev’s reforms and diplomatic outreach were met with cautious optimism. A series of **summit meetings** between Reagan and Gorbachev, including:

* **Geneva Summit (1985)**
* **Reykjavík Summit (1986)**
* **Washington Summit (1987)**
* **Moscow Summit (1988)**

These meetings led to a breakthrough in arms control and mutual understanding. The most significant outcome was the **Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty**, signed in 1987, which eliminated an entire class of nuclear weapons and included unprecedented verification measures.

Gorbachev’s foreign policy, branded as the **“New Thinking,”** emphasized global interdependence, cooperative security, and the abandonment of class conflict as the basis of international relations.

---

### Decline of Soviet Influence in Eastern Europe

By the late 1980s, Soviet control over its Eastern European satellite states began to erode. Gorbachev renounced the **Brezhnev Doctrine**, which had justified Soviet intervention in socialist countries, and signaled that Eastern Bloc governments were responsible for their own affairs.

This shift allowed for the **peaceful revolutions of 1989**, during which communist regimes across Eastern Europe collapsed:

* **Poland** legalized the opposition Solidarity movement and held partially free elections.
* **Hungary** dismantled its border fence with Austria, creating a hole in the Iron Curtain.
* **East Germany** saw mass protests culminating in the **fall of the Berlin Wall** on November 9, 1989.
* **Czechoslovakia**, **Bulgaria**, and **Romania** experienced regime changes, with Romania’s being the only violent transition.

These events marked the effective **end of the Eastern Bloc** and the symbolic disintegration of the Cold War divide in Europe.

---

### German Reunification and NATO

The fall of the Berlin Wall paved the way for **German reunification** in **1990**. While the prospect of a unified Germany raised concerns in Moscow, Gorbachev ultimately accepted it in return for economic aid and assurances that NATO forces would not expand eastward immediately. The **Two Plus Four Agreement** (1990) formalized the terms of reunification and Germany’s full membership in NATO, highlighting the diminishing strategic leverage of the Soviet Union.

---

### Final Collapse of the Soviet Union

Domestically, Gorbachev’s reforms led to political fragmentation and economic turmoil. Nationalist movements surged across the Soviet republics, demanding autonomy or independence. The creation of a more open political environment, including the establishment of a **Congress of People’s Deputies** and the introduction of competitive elections, further undermined the Communist Party’s monopoly on power.

In **August 1991**, a group of hardline communists launched a failed **coup d'état** in Moscow. The coup’s collapse discredited the party and accelerated the unraveling of the Soviet state. By December 1991:

* **Ukraine**, **Belarus**, and **Russia** declared independence.
* The **Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS)** was formed.
* **Gorbachev resigned** as president of the Soviet Union.
* On **December 26, 1991**, the **USSR was officially dissolved**.

---

### End of the Cold War

The dissolution of the Soviet Union marked the definitive **end of the Cold War**. The **United States** emerged as the world’s sole superpower, while Russia, the USSR’s largest successor state, inherited its UN Security Council seat and nuclear arsenal but faced severe internal challenges.

The end of the Cold War also ushered in a period of **U.S. global dominance**, efforts at European integration, and the reorientation of former Eastern Bloc states toward liberal democracy and market economies. However, the legacy of Cold War institutions, alliances, and regional conflicts continued to influence international politics.

---

### Conclusion

The **1985–1991** phase of the Cold War represents a period of rapid transformation, defined by reform, diplomacy, and ultimately collapse. Mikhail Gorbachev’s leadership, combined with a shift in U.S. policy and popular movements across Eastern Europe, brought an end to a conflict that had shaped global affairs for over four decades. Though largely peaceful, the end of the Cold War set in motion new political, economic, and security challenges that would shape the post-Cold War world.
