**Vietnam War**

**Introduction**
The Vietnam War was a protracted conflict in Southeast Asia that lasted from November 1, 1955, to April 30, 1975. It pitted the communist government of North Vietnam and its southern allies, the Viet Cong, against the government of South Vietnam, supported primarily by the United States alongside other anti-communist partners. Emerging from the post-World War II decolonization process, the war evolved into a focal point of Cold War tensions, shaping domestic politics and international relations for two decades.

**Historical Background**
Following the end of Japanese occupation in 1945, Vietnamese nationalist movements sought independence from French colonial rule. The First Indochina War (1946–1954) culminated in the 1954 Geneva Accords, which provisionally divided Vietnam along the 17th parallel. The accords envisioned nationwide elections in 1956 to reunify the country, but these elections were never held. North Vietnam, led by Ho Chi Minh and the Communist Party, consolidated authority in Hanoi, while South Vietnam, under President Ngo Dinh Diem, received backing from the United States and other Western nations.

**U.S. Involvement and Escalation**
Early American assistance to South Vietnam began with economic aid and military advisers under President Dwight D. Eisenhower. Concerned by the “domino” theory―the belief that communist victory in one state would trigger further spread―successive administrations expanded their commitment. Under President John F. Kennedy, U.S. military advisers doubled in number, and South Vietnamese counterinsurgency operations intensified. Following the Gulf of Tonkin incident in August 1964, President Lyndon B. Johnson secured congressional authorization to employ conventional U.S. forces. Ground combat units arrived in March 1965, marking a major escalation in both troop levels and aerial bombardment campaigns.

**Major Campaigns and Tactics**
American strategy centered on attrition, aiming to erode enemy forces through superior firepower. Key operations included Rolling Thunder, an extensive bombing campaign against North Vietnamese infrastructure, and search-and-destroy missions in rural areas of South Vietnam. The Viet Cong and North Vietnamese Army employed guerrilla tactics, leveraging knowledge of the terrain and elaborate tunnel systems. Tet Offensive in January 1968, coordinated attacks on more than 100 cities and outposts, dealt a psychological blow to U.S. perceptions of imminent victory, despite heavy losses inflicted on communist forces. Subsequent operations such as Linebacker I and II in 1972 targeted supply routes and strategic targets in the North.

**Domestic Political Context**
The war provoked intense debate within the United States. Early broad public support gave way to growing antiwar sentiment by the late 1960s, fueled by rising American casualties, televised battlefield imagery, and revelations of wartime atrocities. The civil rights movement and student activism converged with antiwar protests, generating mass demonstrations on college campuses and in major cities. President Richard Nixon sought a “Vietnamization” policy, transferring combat responsibilities to South Vietnamese forces while gradually withdrawing U.S. troops. His administration also conducted secret bombing operations in neighboring Cambodia and Laos, sparking further controversy.

**International Dynamics**
Beyond U.S. and Vietnamese actors, the conflict drew in regional and global powers. The Soviet Union and the People’s Republic of China provided military and economic assistance to North Vietnam, while Australia, South Korea, Thailand, and the Philippines contributed troops to the U.S.-led coalition. Diplomatic efforts, including the Paris Peace Talks initiated in 1968, sought a negotiated settlement. The 1973 Paris Peace Accords stipulated a ceasefire, exchange of prisoners, and the withdrawal of U.S. forces, though fighting between North and South Vietnam continued.

**Conclusion of the Conflict**
After U.S. forces withdrew in March 1973, South Vietnam struggled to maintain stability amid dwindling American aid. In early 1975, North Vietnamese forces launched a final offensive. The rapid collapse of major cities culminated in the fall of Saigon on April 30, 1975, leading to the reunification of Vietnam under communist rule.

**Legacy and Impact**
The Vietnam War had profound consequences. In the United States, it catalyzed shifts in foreign policy doctrine, notably the War Powers Act of 1973, aimed at checking executive authority. The conflict left deep societal scars, including veterans’ health issues and a reassessment of military interventions. For Vietnam, reunification brought reconstruction challenges and economic hardship, later addressed by Đổi Mới reforms beginning in 1986. The war also influenced popular culture, journalism, and academic studies of counterinsurgency and guerrilla warfare, leaving a lasting imprint on international relations and collective memory.
