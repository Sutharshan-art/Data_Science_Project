**Nelson Rolihlahla Mandela** (18 July 1918 – 5 December 2013) was a South African anti-apartheid revolutionary, political leader, and philanthropist who served as the country’s first Black president from 1994 to 1999. A central figure in the struggle against institutionalized racial segregation and discrimination, Mandela’s life and career were deeply intertwined with South Africa’s transition from white minority rule to a multiracial democracy.

## Early Life and Education

Born into the Thembu royal family in the village of Mvezo in the Eastern Cape, Mandela was the son of a local chief and counselor to the monarch. He was educated at Methodist mission schools and at the University of Fort Hare, where he studied law but was expelled for participating in a student protest. Completing his degree through correspondence with the University of South Africa, he became one of the first Black lawyers in Johannesburg. His upbringing and education exposed him to both Xhosa traditions and the inequalities entrenched by British colonial and Afrikaner nationalist rule.

## Entry into Politics and Anti-Apartheid Activism

In 1943, Mandela joined the African National Congress (ANC), which had formed in 1912 to oppose racial discrimination. By the 1950s, the ruling National Party had implemented apartheid—a system of legal segregation and political disenfranchisement of non-white populations. Mandela helped found the ANC Youth League in 1944, advocating more confrontational tactics. He played a leading role in the 1952 Defiance Campaign, orchestrated civil disobedience against apartheid laws, and co-authored the 1955 Freedom Charter, which articulated a vision of non-racial democracy and was later adopted as the ANC’s manifesto.

## Underground Resistance and Imprisonment

After the Sharpeville massacre in 1960 and the subsequent banning of the ANC, Mandela co-founded Umkhonto we Sizwe (Spear of the Nation) in 1961, the ANC’s armed wing, to carry out sabotage against government installations. In 1962, he was arrested, convicted of incitement and leaving the country illegally, and sentenced to five years’ imprisonment. In 1963 he was later charged in the Rivonia Trial with sabotage and conspiracy to overthrow the government, receiving a life sentence and being sent to Robben Island, where he spent 18 of his 27 years in captivity under harsh conditions.

## International Pressure and Release

During his imprisonment, Mandela became the most widely recognized symbol of resistance to apartheid. Global campaigns—led by the “Free Nelson Mandela” movement—applied diplomatic and economic pressure on South Africa. In the late 1980s, as the apartheid regime faced mounting internal protests and international sanctions, President F. W. de Klerk initiated reforms. Mandela was released on 11 February 1990. He immediately entered negotiations with the government for a peaceful end to apartheid, legalizing the ANC and other political organizations and paving the way for multiracial elections.

## Presidency and Reconciliation

In South Africa’s first fully representative election in April 1994, the ANC won a majority, and Mandela was inaugurated on 10 May 1994. His presidency prioritized national reconciliation through the establishment of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, which granted conditional amnesty for politically motivated crimes in exchange for full disclosure. Mandela also oversaw the drafting of a new constitution guaranteeing equal rights, and he promoted policies aimed at redressing economic inequalities, including affirmative action and broader access to housing and healthcare.

## Post-Presidential Years and Global Statesmanship

Mandela declined a second term and retired from formal politics in 1999, dedicating his remaining years to philanthropy and advocacy. He founded the Nelson Mandela Foundation to combat HIV/AIDS and poverty and to promote education. As a global statesman, he mediated in conflicts such as those in Burundi and the Democratic Republic of Congo, championed human rights, and remained a unifying figure in South Africa’s political life, often speaking out on issues of social justice.

## Legacy and Significance

Mandela’s leadership transformed South Africa’s political landscape, ending centuries of racial oppression and establishing a constitutional democracy. He is celebrated for his capacity for forgiveness and his belief in dialogue, setting a precedent for peaceful transitions in deeply divided societies. Awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1993 alongside F. W. de Klerk, Mandela’s moral authority and personal sacrifice have made him an enduring icon of resistance to injustice worldwide. His birthday, 18 July, is observed globally as Mandela Day, encouraging acts of service and community engagement in his honor.
