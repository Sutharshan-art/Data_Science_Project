**Politics of Bosnia and Herzegovina**

The **politics of Bosnia and Herzegovina** operate under a complex system of power-sharing and ethno-political representation, shaped primarily by the country’s diverse ethnic composition and the legacy of the **Bosnian War** (1992–1995). The country is a **parliamentary representative democratic republic**, but its political structure is highly decentralized and rooted in the terms of the **Dayton Peace Agreement**, which ended the war and established a constitutional framework aimed at maintaining peace between the country’s main ethnic groups: **Bosniaks**, **Croats**, and **Serbs**.

The political landscape of Bosnia and Herzegovina is characterized by overlapping layers of authority at the state, entity, and local levels. Ethnic identity plays a central role in elections and policymaking, often resulting in political gridlock and inefficiency.

---

### Constitutional Framework

The constitution of Bosnia and Herzegovina is contained within **Annex 4 of the Dayton Peace Agreement** (1995), signed in Paris and brokered by the United States. The agreement divided the country into two autonomous entities:

* The **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (FBiH)**, primarily inhabited by Bosniaks and Croats
* The **Republika Srpska (RS)**, primarily inhabited by Serbs

Additionally, the **Brčko District** was created as a self-governing, neutral administrative unit under the sovereignty of the state but formally belonging to neither entity.

The constitution recognizes three constituent peoples—Bosniaks, Croats, and Serbs—along with “Others,” a category encompassing minorities and those not identifying with the main ethnic groups. Political power is divided along ethnic lines, with mechanisms to ensure group representation and prevent dominance by any one community.

---

### Executive Branch

The **Presidency of Bosnia and Herzegovina** is a three-member collective head of state, composed of one Bosniak, one Croat (both elected from the Federation), and one Serb (elected from Republika Srpska). The presidency rotates every eight months among the three members during a four-year term. The presidency conducts foreign policy and represents the state internationally.

The **Council of Ministers**, led by the **Chairperson (Prime Minister)**, functions as the executive government of the state. It includes several deputy ministers and handles domestic and economic policy, though its powers are limited compared to those of the entities.

Each entity also has its own president, prime minister, parliament, and ministries, with significant autonomy in governance, education, policing, and taxation.

---

### Legislative Branch

The **Parliamentary Assembly of Bosnia and Herzegovina** is the country’s bicameral legislature and consists of:

* The **House of Peoples**, composed of 15 delegates (five Bosniaks, five Croats, and five Serbs)
* The **House of Representatives**, with 42 members (elected through proportional representation, two-thirds from the Federation and one-third from Republika Srpska)

The House of Peoples is a vital instrument of ethnic veto and group protection, often used to block legislation perceived as discriminatory against a constituent people. This structure aims to prevent majoritarianism but frequently leads to legislative deadlock.

---

### Judiciary and International Oversight

The judicial system is divided into state and entity levels, with the **Constitutional Court of Bosnia and Herzegovina** serving as the highest arbiter of constitutional issues. It includes nine judges: two Bosniaks, two Croats, two Serbs, and three foreign judges appointed by the **President of the European Court of Human Rights**.

An additional layer of international supervision is provided by the **Office of the High Representative (OHR)**, established to oversee the civilian implementation of the Dayton Agreement. The High Representative has broad powers, including the ability to impose legislation and remove officials. While intended as a temporary measure, the OHR has remained in place due to ongoing political instability.

---

### Political Parties and Ethnic Politics

Bosnia and Herzegovina’s political system is heavily fragmented along ethnic lines. Most political parties represent specific ethnic constituencies, and cross-ethnic parties remain relatively weak. Prominent parties include:

* **Party of Democratic Action (SDA)** – representing Bosniak interests
* **Croatian Democratic Union of Bosnia and Herzegovina (HDZ BiH)** – representing Croats
* **Alliance of Independent Social Democrats (SNSD)** – dominant Serb party in Republika Srpska

This ethnic segmentation fuels political competition over national identity, state symbols, and power-sharing rather than policy debates, hindering reform efforts and EU integration.

---

### Challenges and Reforms

Since the end of the war, Bosnia and Herzegovina has faced persistent challenges in building a cohesive political system. These include:

* **Ethnic division** and institutional paralysis
* **Corruption** and weak rule of law
* **Resistance to constitutional reform**, particularly proposals to reduce ethnic quotas or strengthen state institutions

Despite aspirations for **European Union membership**, progress has been slow due to the country’s internal divisions and failure to meet key political and legal criteria. The **European Court of Human Rights** has ruled that the exclusion of minorities from running for certain offices violates international norms, but reforms have been stalled.

---

### Conclusion

The politics of Bosnia and Herzegovina remain shaped by the post-conflict power-sharing arrangement instituted by the Dayton Agreement. While this system has preserved peace and allowed for basic governance, it has also entrenched ethnic divisions and created a rigid institutional framework resistant to change. Efforts to reform the political system and move toward greater EU integration continue, but progress is often hindered by entrenched ethno-political interests and institutional complexity.
