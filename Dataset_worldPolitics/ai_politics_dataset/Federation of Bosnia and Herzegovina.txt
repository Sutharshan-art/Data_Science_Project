**Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina**

The **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina** (FBiH) is one of the two constitutional and legal entities of Bosnia and Herzegovina, the other being Republika Srpska. Established by the 1994 Washington Agreement and enshrined in the 1995 Dayton Peace Accords, the Federation covers approximately 51 % of the country’s territory and is home to a majority of its Bosniak (Bosnian Muslim) and Croat populations. It is a complex decentralized polity designed to balance ethnic representation and prevent a resurgence of interethnic conflict following the Bosnian War (1992–1995).

### Historical Background

Following Bosnia and Herzegovina’s declaration of independence from the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia in March 1992, violent conflict erupted among its three main ethnic groups: Bosniaks, Croats, and Serbs. By early 1993, Croat forces in central and western Bosnia clashed with the Bosnian government, leading to the Croat–Bosniak War. To halt intra-ethnic fighting and solidify a Bosniak–Croat alliance against Bosnian Serb forces, the U.S.-brokered **Washington Agreement** was signed on 18 March 1994. This accord unified Bosniak- and Croat-controlled territories into the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina, establishing a power-sharing framework that was later incorporated into the broader **Dayton Peace Accords** of December 1995.

### Constitutional and Political Structure

The Federation’s constitution creates a highly decentralized federal system comprising ten cantons, each with its own government, legislature, and judiciary. This arrangement reflects wartime territorial divisions and ensures that both constituent peoples (Bosniaks and Croats) have substantial autonomy at the canton level. The cantons are:

1. Una-Sana
2. Posavina
3. Tuzla
4. Zenica-Doboj
5. Bosnian Podrinje
6. Central Bosnia
7. Herzegovina-Neretva
8. West Herzegovina
9. Sarajevo
10. Canton 10 (Herzeg-Bosnia)

At the entity level, the Federation has its own bicameral parliament—the House of Representatives and the House of Peoples—as well as a President and two Vice-Presidents (one Bosniak and one Croat in rotation). The **House of Representatives** consists of 98 deputies elected by proportional vote, while the **House of Peoples** comprises 58 delegates (17 Bosniaks, 17 Croats, and 7 representatives of “Others,” plus alternates) selected by the cantonal assemblies. The Council of Ministers of the Federation, headed by a Prime Minister, implements policies under executive authority.

### Power-Sharing and Ethnic Balance

The FBiH’s governance model enshrines consociational principles to prevent domination by any one group. Key mechanisms include:

* **Mutual vetoes** in the House of Peoples to block legislation deemed harmful to constituent peoples.
* **Rotational presidency**, ensuring that Bosniak and Croat officeholders alternate roles.
* **Ethnic quotas** in public administration and judiciary, guaranteeing representation for Bosniaks, Croats, and Others.
* **Cantonal autonomy**, which allows local majorities to govern their own affairs, reducing the risk of entity-wide ethnic tensions.

### Demographics and Territory

According to the 2013 census, the Federation’s population was approximately 2.2 million, making it the more populous of Bosnia and Herzegovina’s two entities. Bosniaks constitute roughly 70 % of the entity’s inhabitants, Croats approximately 22 %, and “Others” (including Serbs, Roma, and multiethnic citizens) the remainder. The Federation includes major urban centers such as **Sarajevo** (the country’s capital), **Mostar**, **Tuzla**, and **Zenica**, as well as diverse geographic regions ranging from Dinaric Alps highlands to the Adriatic-influenced south.

### Economic and Administrative Challenges

The Federation faces significant challenges stemming from its complex institutional architecture. Critics argue that overlapping authorities between the entity, cantons, and municipalities lead to bureaucratic inefficiencies, inflated public spending, and slow decision-making. Economic disparities exist both between cantons—particularly between the more industrialized north (Tuzla, Zenica) and the tourism-oriented south (Herzegovina-Neretva)—and within urban–rural divides. Unemployment rates have historically exceeded 20 %, and the public sector remains a major employer.

Reform efforts—often encouraged by the European Union and international financial institutions—aim to streamline government levels, harmonize regulations, and improve the business climate. However, proposals for administrative consolidation have been stalled by fears among Croat leaders of potential marginalization at the entity level.

### Relations with Republika Srpska and National Institutions

Under the Dayton framework, Bosnia and Herzegovina operates as a single sovereign state with shared institutions: a tripartite **Presidency**, a **Council of Ministers**, and a **Parliamentary Assembly**. The Federation and Republika Srpska maintain parallel governmental systems but cooperate on foreign policy, defense, fiscal policy, and immigration at the national level. Tensions occasionally arise over jurisdictional boundaries, fiscal transfers, and political representation, but the entities’ constitutional arrangements have largely prevented a return to war.

### International Support and EU Integration

The international community—through the Office of the High Representative (OHR), the European Union, and various UN agencies—continues to monitor and support political and economic reforms in the Federation. Efforts focus on strengthening the rule of law, combating corruption, and aligning legislation with European Union standards. Bosnia and Herzegovina was granted EU candidate status in December 2022, conditional on further reforms in governance, public administration, and judiciary. Progress in the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina is deemed crucial to the country’s overall EU accession prospects.

### Conclusion

The Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina represents a unique consociational polity born out of wartime necessity and international mediation. Its decentralized, canton-based structure and power-sharing mechanisms seek to balance ethnic interests and maintain peace between Bosniaks and Croats. While the system has successfully averted renewed conflict, it also faces challenges of administrative complexity, economic disparity, and the need for further integration into European structures. The Federation’s evolution continues to shape Bosnia and Herzegovina’s trajectory toward sustainable stability and Euro-Atlantic membership.
