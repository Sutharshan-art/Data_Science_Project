**Dissolution of the Soviet Union**

The **dissolution of the Soviet Union** was the process by which the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) ceased to exist on 26 December 1991, ending a seventy-year experiment in Marxist-Leninist government and fundamentally reshaping the global political order. The disintegration followed a period of political liberalization, economic crisis, and burgeoning national movements, culminating in the recognition of fifteen independent republics and the creation of the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS).

## Background and Political Context

### Perestroika and Glasnost

By the early 1980s, the Soviet economy was stagnating under centralized planning, while the arms race with the United States imposed unsustainable fiscal burdens. In March 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev became General Secretary of the Communist Party and launched **perestroika** (restructuring) to introduce limited market mechanisms and **glasnost** (openness) to encourage public discussion. Though intended to revitalize the USSR, these reforms loosened the Party’s ideological monopoly and exposed systemic weaknesses.

### National Movements and Sovereignty Declarations

Encouraged by glasnost, republics’ **supreme soviets** and emerging civic movements pressed for greater autonomy or full independence. Lithuania led the way by **declaring independence** on 11 March 1990, followed by Estonia on 30 March and Latvia on 4 May. In Russia, Boris Yeltsin’s election as President of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic (RSFSR) in June 1991—shortly after the RSFSR adopted a sovereignty declaration—signaled that Russia, the USSR’s largest republic, would no longer accept communist party supremacy.

## The August 1991 Coup Attempt

On 19 August 1991, hardline members of Gorbachev’s government attempted a **state coup** to roll back reforms and restore central control. Detaining Gorbachev at his Crimean dacha, the **State Committee on the State of Emergency** proclaimed itself in charge. Boris Yeltsin rallied public resistance in Moscow, taking sanctuary atop a tank outside the Russian White House. The coup collapsed after three days, discrediting the Communist Party and accelerating republican secessionist momentum. Gorbachev’s authority was irrevocably weakened.

## Belavezha and Alma-Ata Accords

On 8 December 1991, leaders of three Slavic republics—Russia’s Boris Yeltsin, Ukraine’s Leonid Kravchuk, and Belarus’s Stanislav Shushkevich—met in Belavezha Forest (Belarus) and signed the **Belavezha Accords**, declaring the USSR dissolved and announcing the formation of the **Commonwealth of Independent States**. Gorbachev denounced the accords as illegal but lacked the political support to contest them.

Between 21 and 23 December, more than a dozen non-Baltic republics convened in Alma-Ata (Almaty), Kazakhstan, to refine the CIS charter, formally accepting the Soviet Union’s end and establishing mechanisms for cooperation in defense, economy, and foreign policy. On 25 December, Gorbachev **resigned** as President of the USSR, handing over the nuclear launch codes to Yeltsin.

## Legal and Constitutional Termination

On 26 December 1991, the **Supreme Soviet** of the Soviet Union voted itself and the USSR into extinction, formally acknowledging the independence of the republics and repealing the 1924 Constitution. The Russian Federation, under Yeltsin, inherited the USSR’s United Nations Security Council seat, its nuclear arsenal, and diplomatic treaties, subject to subsequent disarmament and treaty renegotiations.

## International Response and Impact

World leaders rapidly recognized the new republics. The dissolution marked the definitive end of the **Cold War**, as the global bipolar standoff gave way to U.S. unipolarity and the spread of market-oriented democracy. NATO’s mission and European security architecture pivoted to engage former Warsaw Pact states. Former Soviet republics charted diverse paths—Balts joined NATO and the EU, while Central Asian states retained closer ties to Russia and China.

## Economic and Social Consequences

The collapse of centralized planning unleashed severe economic contraction across the former USSR. Hyperinflation, unemployment, and supply shortages characterized the early 1990s, prompting mass emigration and demographic decline. Institutional capacity deficits and corruption hindered privatization and market reforms. Russia’s “wild-market” transition under **shock therapy** contrasted with more gradual approaches in the Baltics and parts of Eastern Europe.

## Legacy

The dissolution of the Soviet Union stands as one of the late 20th century’s most transformative events. It ended the world’s largest land empire, reshaped national borders, and prompted debates over nationalism, sovereignty, and federalism. Its political aftermath persists in regional conflicts—such as those in Transnistria, Abkhazia, and Nagorno-Karabakh—and in Russia’s foreign policy assertiveness toward its “near abroad.” Economic legacies include enduring disparities between Western-oriented and CIS-aligned states. Gorbachev’s reforms, intended to save the USSR, instead precipitated its demise, leaving a complex legacy of both liberation and hardship for its successor nations.
