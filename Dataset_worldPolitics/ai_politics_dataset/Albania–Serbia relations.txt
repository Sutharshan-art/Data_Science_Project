**Albania–Serbia Relations**

**Albania–Serbia relations** refer to the diplomatic, political, and historical interactions between the **Republic of Albania** and the **Republic of Serbia**. These relations have been shaped by complex historical legacies, ethnic and territorial disputes—most notably regarding **Kosovo**—and periods of both hostility and attempted rapprochement. While contemporary efforts have aimed to normalize ties and promote regional cooperation, deep-rooted mistrust and divergent national narratives continue to influence bilateral relations.

---

### Historical Background

The historical foundations of Albania–Serbia relations trace back to the **Balkan Wars** (1912–1913) and the **dissolution of the Ottoman Empire**, when the modern Albanian state was first established. During this period, Serbia sought territorial expansion into areas with mixed populations, including Kosovo and parts of northern Albania. The resulting **First Balkan War** saw Serbia gain control over Kosovo, a region with a significant ethnic Albanian population, laying the groundwork for long-term tension.

In **1913**, the **London Conference of Ambassadors** recognized Albania as an independent state, largely through the diplomatic efforts of Austria-Hungary and Italy. Serbia opposed the full realization of an Albanian state, fearing it would obstruct its access to the Adriatic Sea. Armed conflicts along the **Albanian–Serbian frontier**, including the **Albanian–Yugoslav border war** of 1921, further strained relations in the interwar period.

Under the **Kingdom of Yugoslavia** (1918–1941), ethnic Albanians in Kosovo and other regions were subjected to assimilation policies and demographic engineering, fueling resentment. During **World War II**, parts of Kosovo and western Macedonia were annexed to Italian-occupied Albania under Axis control, further complicating the ethnic and territorial dynamics.

---

### Communist Era (1945–1991)

Following World War II, both Albania and Yugoslavia (which included Serbia as one of its republics) became communist states under the leadership of **Enver Hoxha** in Albania and **Josip Broz Tito** in Yugoslavia. Initially, Albania aligned closely with Yugoslavia, but relations deteriorated sharply in **1948** after the **Tito–Stalin split**. Hoxha sided with the Soviet Union and accused Tito of revisionism and hegemonic ambitions, particularly over Albanian sovereignty.

Throughout the Cold War, Albania pursued an isolationist foreign policy, aligning first with the Soviet Union and later with China. Meanwhile, Yugoslavia maintained its policy of non-alignment. Tensions remained high, especially over the treatment of ethnic Albanians in **Kosovo**, who faced political repression and cultural marginalization under the Yugoslav regime, particularly during the **1980s** under **Slobodan Milošević**.

---

### Post-Cold War Period and Kosovo Conflict

With the **dissolution of Yugoslavia** in the 1990s and the rise of Serbian nationalism under Milošević, Albania–Serbia relations reached a new low. Albania openly supported the **Kosovo Albanian movement** for autonomy and, eventually, independence. The **Kosovo War** (1998–1999), in which the Serbian military conducted a campaign against ethnic Albanians, led to a **NATO intervention** and the de facto separation of Kosovo from Serbia.

Albania provided humanitarian and logistical support to Kosovo Albanians during the war, including shelter for hundreds of thousands of refugees. Serbia accused Albania of supporting the **Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA)**, which it classified as a terrorist organization.

Following the war, **Kosovo was placed under UN administration**, and Albania became a vocal proponent of its formal independence, which was declared in **2008**. Serbia, however, does not recognize Kosovo’s independence, viewing it as a breakaway province—a major ongoing point of contention in Albania–Serbia relations.

---

### Contemporary Relations

In the 21st century, both Albania and Serbia have expressed interest in **European Union membership**, leading to increased diplomatic engagement. Despite lingering tensions, leaders from both countries have participated in **regional forums**, such as the **Berlin Process** and the **Open Balkan initiative**, aimed at fostering economic cooperation and political stability in the Western Balkans.

High-level meetings between Albanian and Serbian leaders—such as those involving **Edi Rama** and **Aleksandar Vučić**—have taken place, often marked by public disagreements over Kosovo but also calls for normalization and regional integration.

However, political relations remain fragile. Incidents related to nationalism, historical grievances, and Kosovo continue to provoke diplomatic spats. In 2014, a **drone incident** during a football match in Belgrade sparked a political controversy, reflecting the underlying sensitivity in national identities and symbols.

---

### Economic and Cultural Relations

Despite political tensions, **economic relations** between Albania and Serbia have slowly improved. Bilateral trade remains limited but has shown gradual growth. Joint infrastructure projects, energy cooperation, and tourism are areas with potential for further development.

Cultural and educational exchanges remain modest, often hindered by nationalist rhetoric and historical mistrust. However, civil society groups and regional organizations have promoted people-to-people dialogue and youth engagement across ethnic lines.

---

### Conclusion

Albania–Serbia relations are defined by a complex interplay of history, nationalism, and regional geopolitics. While diplomatic ties have evolved from open hostility to cautious engagement, the unresolved status of Kosovo continues to be a central obstacle to full normalization. Nevertheless, shared aspirations for European integration and economic cooperation offer pathways for improving relations, provided both nations can manage their historical narratives and commit to constructive dialogue in a volatile region.
