**List of People Pardoned by Bill Clinton**

The list of people pardoned by Bill Clinton encompasses the individuals who received executive clemency—either a full pardon or a commutation of sentence—from the 42nd president of the United States. During his two terms in office from January 20, 1993, to January 20, 2001, Clinton granted 456 pardons and commutations. These acts of clemency were issued under Article II, Section 2, of the U.S. Constitution, which gives the president the authority to grant reprieves and pardons for federal offenses.

Presidential pardons are typically issued to individuals who have served their sentences and demonstrated rehabilitation, but they can also be granted preemptively or while a sentence is still being served. The use of this power varies by administration and often reflects broader political, legal, and humanitarian considerations.

Clinton’s use of the clemency power drew widespread attention, particularly for its timing, volume, and controversial recipients. While many of his pardons were routine, several were the subject of public scrutiny and congressional inquiry, especially those issued in the final hours of his presidency.

### Early Pardons and Commutations

Bill Clinton began issuing pardons early in his presidency, with the first batch announced in 1994. Early recipients included individuals convicted of nonviolent drug offenses, fraud, and military crimes. Many of these pardons aligned with traditional considerations such as good conduct, long-past convictions, and strong recommendations from the Department of Justice’s Office of the Pardon Attorney.

Clinton also issued a number of commutations for inmates serving lengthy sentences for low-level drug offenses. These clemency decisions were seen by some as a recognition of the disproportionate sentencing laws in effect during the 1980s and early 1990s, particularly those related to crack cocaine.

### Puerto Rican Nationalists

One of Clinton’s most controversial clemency actions occurred in August 1999, when he offered conditional commutations to 16 members of the FALN (Fuerzas Armadas de Liberación Nacional), a Puerto Rican nationalist group responsible for over 130 bombings in the United States during the 1970s and 1980s. The prisoners had been convicted of offenses including seditious conspiracy, possession of explosives, and firearms violations, though none were convicted of directly causing deaths or injuries.

The offer was made on humanitarian grounds and was conditional upon renouncing violence. The clemency was supported by figures such as Archbishop Desmond Tutu and former President Jimmy Carter but was sharply criticized by the FBI, U.S. Attorney’s Offices, and Congress. The controversy raised questions about the criteria and motivations behind presidential clemency.

### Pardons Related to the Clinton Administration

Several individuals with personal or political ties to the Clinton administration were granted clemency, including:

* **Henry Cisneros**: Former Secretary of Housing and Urban Development, who pleaded guilty to a misdemeanor charge of making false statements to the FBI. He was pardoned in 2001.

* **Roger Clinton Jr.**: The president’s half-brother, convicted in 1985 for cocaine distribution, received a pardon in January 2001.

Critics argued that these pardons reflected cronyism, while supporters contended that the actions were consistent with prior clemency practices for individuals who had completed their sentences and shown rehabilitation.

### The Marc Rich Pardon

The most controversial and politically explosive pardon of Clinton’s presidency was issued on January 20, 2001, to **Marc Rich**, a billionaire financier indicted in 1983 on charges of tax evasion, fraud, and illegal trading with Iran during the hostage crisis. Rich had fled to Switzerland and remained a fugitive.

Clinton’s decision to pardon Rich—who had not stood trial or served time—sparked widespread criticism and allegations of influence peddling. Rich’s ex-wife, Denise Rich, had made significant donations to the Clinton Presidential Library and Democratic causes, leading to accusations that the pardon had been bought.

The backlash led to congressional hearings and a federal investigation by a special prosecutor, though no charges were filed against Clinton. The incident contributed to a broader debate about the transparency and accountability of presidential pardons.

### Final Batch of Pardons

On his last day in office, January 20, 2001, Clinton issued 140 pardons and 36 commutations. In addition to Marc Rich, this list included:

* **Susan McDougal**, a former associate in the Whitewater investigation, who served prison time for contempt and fraud.

* **Patty Hearst**, who had been convicted in the 1970s for her involvement with the Symbionese Liberation Army.

* **Dan Rostenkowski**, a former Democratic congressman convicted of mail fraud.

These final pardons generated a wave of criticism from political opponents, media commentators, and legal analysts. Critics claimed the process lacked transparency, while defenders pointed out that many recipients had compelling cases for clemency and had served their sentences.

### Legacy and Impact

Bill Clinton’s use of the pardon power remains one of the most scrutinized in modern presidential history. While many of the clemency decisions followed standard legal procedures and humanitarian grounds, others—particularly those involving political allies or controversial figures—raised concerns about the influence of wealth, connections, and timing in the exercise of executive clemency.

The controversy over Clinton’s pardons contributed to calls for reform, including proposals to increase oversight of the process and to limit last-minute clemency decisions. Nevertheless, the constitutional authority to grant pardons remains broad and largely unchecked by other branches of government.
