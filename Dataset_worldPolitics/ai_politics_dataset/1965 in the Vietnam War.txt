**1965 in the Vietnam War**

The year **1965** marked a fundamental turning point in the Vietnam War, as the United States transitioned from an advisory role to direct military intervention with the deployment of ground combat troops. It also witnessed the initiation of sustained bombing campaigns against **North Vietnam**, making the conflict a full-scale war involving two sovereign nations. This escalation profoundly altered the war’s political dynamics, both within Vietnam and globally, and set the stage for a prolonged and increasingly controversial U.S. military commitment.

---

### U.S. Combat Troops and the Shift in Military Strategy

Until early 1965, the United States had limited its role in Vietnam to military advising and support for the South Vietnamese government. However, following a series of Viet Cong attacks in late 1964 and early 1965 — notably the assault on **Camp Holloway** near Pleiku on **February 7** — President **Lyndon B. Johnson** authorized a major escalation.

In March 1965, the first contingent of U.S. combat troops — **3,500 Marines** — landed at **Da Nang**, marking the official beginning of large-scale American ground involvement. By the end of the year, the U.S. troop presence had surged to approximately **184,000**. This decision reflected Johnson’s belief that South Vietnam could not survive without direct American military support and that U.S. credibility in the Cold War context was at stake.

American forces began conducting major ground operations, including **search-and-destroy missions** intended to find and eliminate Viet Cong units and infrastructure in South Vietnam. These operations were aimed at weakening the insurgency and restoring government control in contested regions, although success proved elusive due to guerrilla tactics and the Viet Cong’s knowledge of the terrain.

---

### Operation Rolling Thunder

In response to continued attacks on U.S. forces and facilities, the Johnson administration launched **Operation Rolling Thunder** in March 1965, a sustained aerial bombing campaign against North Vietnam. The operation aimed to punish Hanoi for supporting the insurgency in the South, disrupt logistics and supply routes (including the **Ho Chi Minh Trail**), and pressure the North Vietnamese leadership into abandoning its military campaign.

However, the strategic effectiveness of Rolling Thunder was limited. North Vietnam responded by dispersing its infrastructure, strengthening its air defenses with Soviet and Chinese assistance, and redoubling its support for the Viet Cong. The campaign also failed to break the will of North Vietnam’s leadership, which remained committed to reunification by force.

Rolling Thunder marked the beginning of an open war between the United States and North Vietnam, transforming what had been a civil conflict into a major international war.

---

### Political Developments in South Vietnam

South Vietnam remained politically unstable in 1965. Following the repeated coups of 1963 and 1964, a tenuous military junta led by **General Nguyễn Văn Thiệu** and **Air Marshal Nguyễn Cao Kỳ** assumed control. While the junta received backing from the United States, it lacked widespread legitimacy among the Vietnamese population.

The government struggled to implement reforms, suppress corruption, and maintain public support. U.S. officials viewed the regime as a necessary, if imperfect, ally, and prioritized stability over democratization. Despite continued American aid and advisory support, the **Army of the Republic of Vietnam (ARVN)** was increasingly dependent on U.S. military operations to counter the growing Viet Cong insurgency.

---

### The Viet Cong and North Vietnamese Response

The **National Liberation Front (NLF)**, widely supported by rural populations in the South, expanded its activities in 1965, targeting American bases, government outposts, and transportation networks. Its strategy relied on guerrilla tactics and political mobilization rather than conventional military engagements.

In response to the U.S. escalation, **North Vietnam** increased the deployment of its **People’s Army of Vietnam (PAVN)** forces into the South. Though previously limited to covert support, Hanoi now viewed the war as a two-front conflict requiring a unified military response. The North received substantial support from both the **Soviet Union** and the **People’s Republic of China**, including arms, logistical aid, and military advisors.

The growing scale of the conflict led to increased casualties on all sides and further militarized the region, making a negotiated political settlement more remote.

---

### Domestic and International Reaction

Within the United States, public support for the war remained relatively strong in 1965, driven by anti-communist sentiment and belief in the containment strategy. However, some early signs of dissent began to emerge, particularly among intellectuals, students, and a small but vocal antiwar movement.

Internationally, U.S. allies in the **Southeast Asia Treaty Organization (SEATO)** expressed varying degrees of support. Countries like **Australia**, **South Korea**, and **Thailand** contributed troops or logistical assistance, while others, especially in Western Europe, were more critical of U.S. policy. The war became a point of contention in global Cold War politics, with the Soviet Union and China supporting North Vietnam as part of their ideological rivalry with the United States.

---

### Conclusion

The events of **1965** marked the full-scale internationalization of the Vietnam War. The arrival of U.S. combat troops, the beginning of sustained bombing campaigns, and the intensification of the Viet Cong insurgency reshaped the nature of the conflict. Political instability in South Vietnam, coupled with deepening Cold War rivalries, ensured that the war would not be resolved quickly.

What had begun as a limited engagement to support a fragile ally evolved in 1965 into a direct and protracted U.S. military commitment, with profound implications for Vietnam, the region, and American domestic and foreign policy.
