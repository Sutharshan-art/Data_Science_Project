**Foreign Relations of Samoa**

**Introduction**
The Independent State of Samoa conducts a foreign policy oriented toward regional cooperation in the Pacific, partnership with major powers, and advocacy for small island developing states. Since gaining independence in 1962—becoming the first Pacific island nation to do so—Samoa has navigated Cold War geopolitics, decolonization movements, and climate diplomacy to secure its sovereignty, economic development, and cultural identity on the international stage.

**Historical Background**
Prior to independence, Samoa was administered successively by Germany (1900–1914), New Zealand under League of Nations and then United Nations mandate (1914–1962), and as a UN Trust Territory. During this period, foreign affairs were managed by colonial authorities. As independence approached, Samoan leaders affirmed nonalignment and regional solidarity. The Treaty of Friendship with New Zealand (1962) guaranteed mutual cooperation and New Zealand’s continued economic support, setting the tone for Samoa’s measured engagement with former administrating powers.

**Post-Independence Nonalignment**
Upon independence, Samoa joined the Commonwealth of Nations and pursued a nonaligned foreign policy. During the Cold War, it maintained diplomatic relations with both Western and socialist states, balancing development assistance from Australia, the United States, and New Zealand with solidarity missions to socialist countries in Asia and Eastern Europe. Samoa offered moral support to decolonization struggles in Africa and Asia, voting for UN resolutions condemning racial discrimination and colonial occupation.

**Regional Engagement**
Samoa has been an active participant in Pacific regional institutions. A founding member of the Pacific Islands Forum (formerly the South Pacific Forum) in 1971, it has contributed to collective security, economic integration, and ocean governance initiatives. Samoa also belongs to the Secretariat of the Pacific Community, supporting regional data-sharing, health programs, and educational exchanges. Through the Polynesian Leaders Group (2011), Samoa collaborates on issues of cultural preservation, language revitalization, and climate resilience with fellow Polynesian states.

**Relations with New Zealand and Australia**
New Zealand and Australia remain Samoa’s primary development partners. New Zealand provides significant foreign aid—focusing on education, health, and infrastructure—and facilitates annual high-level consultations under the Pacific Partnership framework. Samoa hosts New Zealand and Australian defense forces for joint exercises and disaster-response training. Both countries offer visa-free or visa-on-arrival entry for Samoan citizens, reflecting deep historical ties and significant Samoan diaspora communities in Auckland and Sydney.

**Engagement with the United States**
Samoa established diplomatic relations with the United States in 1971. Cooperation centers on development aid, maritime security, and regional stability. Under the Pacific Islands Forum and bilateral agreements, the U.S. Coast Guard has aided Samoa in patrolling its exclusive economic zone. Samoa participates in the U.S. Pacific Islands Climate Resilience program and sends observers to the Pacific Islands Conference of Leaders that brings together U.S. Indo-Pacific Command and regional governments.

**Relationships with Asian and European States**
In recent decades, Samoa has diversified partnerships with Asian powers. It recognized the People’s Republic of China in 1975 and has since received assistance in infrastructure projects such as the reconstruction of parliamentary buildings and harbor upgrades. At the same time, Samoa maintains diplomatic relations with Taiwan, underscoring its pursuit of development aid from multiple partners. Japan provides technical assistance in fisheries management and disaster risk reduction, while India has offered scholarships and capacity-building programs. European Union development programs support environmental management and private-sector growth.

**Multilateral Diplomacy and United Nations**
Samoa joined the United Nations in 1976 and has since been an active voice for small island developing states (SIDS). It was instrumental in securing the 1994 Barbados Programme of Action and the 2005 Mauritius Strategy, which address sustainable development and vulnerability of SIDS. As chair of the Alliance of Small Island States (AOSIS) at various times, Samoa has championed global climate-change negotiations, calling for ambitious emissions targets and financing for adaptation. Samoa served on the UN Security Council (2015–2016), focusing on peacekeeping support, climate security, and the implementation of the Sustainable Development Goals.

**Defense and Security Cooperation**
Samoa lacks a standing military, relying on its police force and regional partners for external defense. It hosts joint maritime exercises under the Pacific Islands Forum with Australia, New Zealand, and the United States to strengthen maritime domain awareness. Samoa also participates in regional disaster‐response frameworks, hosting workshops on tsunami early warning systems and coordinating relief efforts through the Pacific Humanitarian Pathway on COVID-19.

**Economic Diplomacy**
Recognizing remittances and seasonal worker schemes as economic lifelines, Samoa has negotiated bilateral labor-mobility agreements with Australia and New Zealand. It joined the Pacific Agreement on Closer Economic Relations (PACER) Plus in 2020 to boost trade, investment, and labor mobility among member states. Samoa advocates in regional forums for fairer access to fisheries resources and continued tariff preferences under the European Union’s Everything But Arms initiative.

**Challenges and Future Directions**
Samoa’s foreign policy faces the twin challenges of climate change and global strategic competition. As rising sea levels threaten low-lying communities, Samoa presses for global climate justice and enhanced adaptation funding. Concurrently, the country navigates intensifying U.S.–China rivalry in the Pacific, seeking to maintain balanced relations without compromising sovereignty. Looking ahead, Samoa aims to strengthen South–South cooperation, deepen engagement with Pacific neighbors, and harness diaspora networks to support national development goals.
