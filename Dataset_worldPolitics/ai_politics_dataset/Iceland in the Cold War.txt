**Iceland in the Cold War**

Iceland, a North Atlantic island republic, occupied a strategic position during the Cold War, balancing its newly established sovereignty with the geopolitical imperatives of superpower rivalry. Lacking its own armed forces, Iceland relied on alliances and diplomatic significance to safeguard its security. From 1949, when it joined the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO), until the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, Iceland’s political landscape and foreign policy were shaped by its role as a NATO outpost, domestic debates over neutrality, and its economic reliance on fisheries.

### Historical Background

Having gained full independence from Denmark in 1944, Iceland emerged from World War II economically strained and militarily undefended. The departure of British then American occupation forces in 1946 left a security vacuum. Rising tensions between the United States and the Soviet Union soon prompted Icelandic political leaders to reconsider long-standing traditions of neutrality. Iceland’s strategic location—midway between North America and Europe—made it a key asset for monitoring North Atlantic sea lanes and early warning against potential Soviet naval or submarine incursions.

### NATO Membership and Defense Agreement

In March 1949, after a heated parliamentary and public debate, Iceland became a founding member of NATO. The decision sparked protests in Reykjavík and rural areas, where many feared loss of neutrality and U.S. military presence. Ultimately, the Althing (parliament) approved the measure by a narrow margin. In 1951, Iceland and the United States signed the Defence Agreement, permitting a permanent U.S. armed forces presence at Keflavík Airport and naval patrols in Icelandic waters. Although no Icelandic combat troops were ever stationed abroad, the U.S. base provided the island with defense guarantees in exchange for Iceland’s commitment to host NATO facilities.

### Domestic Political Dynamics

Throughout the Cold War, Iceland’s politics were dominated by three parties: the conservative Independence Party, the centrist Progressive Party, and the left-wing coalition of the Social Democrats and People’s Alliance. Debates over the U.S. base and NATO membership often split along party lines, but also cut across urban–rural divides. Independence Party leaders generally supported close ties with the West, while leftist parties argued for greater neutrality and eventual withdrawal of foreign forces. Coalition governments grappled with reconciling security needs against popular strains for non-alignment, especially during periods of heightened superpower tensions such as the Korean and Vietnam Wars.

### The Cod Wars and Cold War Context

Between 1958 and 1976, Iceland engaged in a series of fisheries disputes—known as the Cod Wars—with the United Kingdom over control of fishing grounds. While primarily economic and environmental in nature, these conflicts took place under the shadow of Cold War alliances. The presence of U.S. and NATO naval units in the region helped deter escalation into full-scale naval warfare. Iceland’s successful extension of its exclusive economic zone to 200 nautical miles underscored its capacity to assert sovereignty and resource management, reinforcing its bargaining position within NATO and on the world stage.

### Reykjavík Summit of 1986

Iceland’s international prominence peaked with the Reykjavík Summit in October 1986, when U.S. President Ronald Reagan and Soviet General Secretary Mikhail Gorbachev met in the presidential residence at Þingvellir. Although the summit ended without a binding agreement, it brought the superpowers to the brink of landmark arms-control treaties and symbolized Iceland’s role as neutral ground for high-level diplomacy. The event enhanced Reykjavík’s reputation as a venue for détente and underscored Iceland’s capacity to facilitate dialogue despite its small size and lack of military power.

### Base Withdrawal Debates and Transition

By the late 1970s and 1980s, growing public unease over foreign troops prompted successive governments to seek adjustments to the Defence Agreement. In 1968, American fighter detachments were withdrawn, leaving only a small support contingent. In 1985, the U.S. Air Force officially ended its air patrols, and in 1991, following the end of the Cold War, the United States closed the Keflavík base and withdrew all forces. Iceland responded by strengthening its own coast guard and enhancing cooperation with NATO allies through periodic joint exercises rather than permanent deployments.

### Legacy and Post–Cold War Role

The Cold War era solidified Iceland’s status as a reliable NATO partner, even as it navigated tensions between alignment and neutrality. The Defence Agreement shaped Icelandic defense policy for decades, fostering infrastructure and economic benefits, but also sparking enduring debate over sovereignty and foreign presence. In the post–Cold War period, Iceland maintained close ties with NATO, participated in partnership programs, and contributed to peacekeeping efforts, all while capitalizing on its Cold War legacy as a location for international diplomacy. The Keflavík site was later redeveloped for civilian use, and Reykjavík’s role as a diplomatic meeting place endured, exemplified by high-level talks such as the 2008 financial crisis negotiations.

Over the course of four decades, Iceland’s strategic choices during the Cold War reflected a pragmatic blend of security cooperation, economic necessity, and national identity. Its evolution from a newly independent state to a key yet non-combatant NATO member illustrates how small states can exert influence through geography, diplomacy, and principled engagement amid superpower rivalry.
