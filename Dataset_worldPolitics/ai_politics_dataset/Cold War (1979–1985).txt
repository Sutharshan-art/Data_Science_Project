**Cold War (1979–1985)**

The period from **1979 to 1985** marked one of the most confrontational phases of the **Cold War**, characterized by the breakdown of détente, the escalation of the nuclear arms race, and renewed ideological conflict between the **United States** and the **Soviet Union**. This era, often referred to as the **Second Cold War**, was driven by events such as the **Soviet invasion of Afghanistan**, the rise of assertive foreign policy under **U.S. President Ronald Reagan**, and increasing tensions in Europe and the developing world.

Despite the heightened antagonism, this period also witnessed the early stages of transformation in the Soviet Union with the rise of **Mikhail Gorbachev** in 1985, which would later lead to significant shifts in East–West relations.

---

### Soviet Invasion of Afghanistan (1979)

The immediate trigger for the renewed Cold War tensions was the **Soviet invasion of Afghanistan** in **December 1979**. Soviet troops entered the country to support the communist **People’s Democratic Party of Afghanistan (PDPA)**, which was facing internal rebellion and political instability. The invasion was widely perceived in the West as an aggressive move by the USSR to expand its influence in South Asia and potentially gain access to the Persian Gulf.

The U.S., under **President Jimmy Carter**, condemned the invasion and responded with several punitive measures: withdrawing from the **SALT II arms agreement**, imposing economic sanctions, initiating the **Carter Doctrine** (which declared the Persian Gulf a vital interest of the United States), and leading a **boycott of the 1980 Summer Olympics** in Moscow. The U.S. and its allies also began covertly supplying arms and support to the **Afghan mujahideen**, who resisted Soviet occupation, setting the stage for a decade-long conflict.

---

### Decline of Détente

The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan effectively ended the era of **détente**, which had characterized U.S.–Soviet relations in the 1970s. The trust built through arms control agreements and diplomatic engagements eroded rapidly. The **SALT II treaty**, though signed in 1979, was never ratified by the U.S. Senate due to the changing political climate.

The Western perception of Soviet expansionism extended beyond Afghanistan to Soviet and Cuban support for leftist regimes and insurgencies in **Nicaragua**, **Ethiopia**, **Angola**, and **El Salvador**. These conflicts contributed to a growing sense among Western policymakers that the Soviet Union was aggressively seeking to tilt the global balance of power in its favor.

---

### Reagan Administration and U.S. Resurgence

When **Ronald Reagan** took office as U.S. president in **January 1981**, he brought with him a confrontational stance toward the Soviet Union. Viewing the USSR as a global threat and an "evil empire," Reagan reversed many aspects of détente and significantly expanded U.S. military capabilities.

Key policies and developments during Reagan’s first term included:

* **Massive defense spending**: The U.S. launched a major military buildup, including the deployment of new intermediate-range nuclear missiles in Western Europe.
* **Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI)**: Announced in 1983, this proposed missile defense system aimed to protect the U.S. from Soviet nuclear attack, but was criticized by Moscow as a threat to the balance of deterrence.
* **Reagan Doctrine**: A strategic policy of supporting anti-communist insurgencies and governments around the world, including the **Contras** in Nicaragua, **UNITA** in Angola, and **mujahideen** in Afghanistan.

These measures were intended to exert economic and military pressure on the Soviet Union, which was already straining under the weight of military commitments and economic inefficiencies.

---

### NATO and the Euromissile Crisis

One of the most contentious episodes of this period was the **Euromissile crisis**. In response to Soviet deployment of **SS-20 intermediate-range ballistic missiles**, NATO approved the **dual-track decision** in 1979: to pursue arms control negotiations while preparing to deploy U.S. **Pershing II** and **cruise missiles** in Europe.

Large-scale anti-nuclear protests erupted across Western Europe in the early 1980s, reflecting widespread public anxiety about the possibility of nuclear war. Despite this, NATO proceeded with deployments in 1983, prompting the Soviet Union to walk out of arms control talks.

---

### Eastern Bloc and Economic Stagnation

Throughout the early 1980s, the **Eastern Bloc** faced growing economic stagnation and political discontent. The **Polish Solidarity movement**, led by **Lech Wałęsa**, emerged as the most prominent challenge to Soviet-aligned communist regimes. Though the Polish government imposed martial law in 1981 to suppress the movement, it signaled deeper structural weaknesses within the Warsaw Pact.

Meanwhile, the **Soviet economy** was burdened by inefficiencies, the costs of the Afghan war, and the demands of competing with U.S. military advances. Leadership transitions following the deaths of **Leonid Brezhnev** (1982), **Yuri Andropov** (1984), and **Konstantin Chernenko** (1985) further contributed to political stagnation in Moscow.

---

### Rise of Mikhail Gorbachev

In **March 1985**, **Mikhail Gorbachev** became General Secretary of the **Communist Party of the Soviet Union**. His youth and reformist agenda would soon bring about a shift in Soviet foreign and domestic policy. Although the full impact of **glasnost (openness)** and **perestroika (restructuring)** would not be felt until later in the decade, Gorbachev’s rise marked the beginning of a new phase in the Cold War.

---

### Conclusion

The **1979–1985** period of the Cold War was marked by severe tensions, militarization, and ideological confrontation. The end of détente, fueled by the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan and assertive U.S. foreign policy under Ronald Reagan, led to a deep freeze in superpower relations. However, the emergence of Gorbachev in 1985 opened the door to renewed dialogue and eventual transformation, setting the stage for the final phase of the Cold War in the late 1980s.
