**General Secretaryship of Mikhail Gorbachev** refers to the period from March 1985 to August 1991 during which Mikhail Sergeyevich Gorbachev served as the leader of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU). His tenure marked the last phase of Soviet one-party rule and initiated sweeping political and economic reforms that transformed both the USSR and the wider international order.

**Accession and Political Context**
When Konstantin Chernenko died in February 1985, the eleven-member Politburo selected 54-year-old Mikhail Gorbachev—then a relatively young and reform-minded apparatchik—as General Secretary. Gorbachev’s rise reflected a desire among senior officials to inject new energy into an aging leadership, confront stagnation, and address the mounting crises of economic slowdown, technological backwardness, and international confrontation with the West. He inherited a Soviet Union burdened by massive military spending, centralized planning inefficiencies, and widespread public cynicism toward the CPSU.

**Perestroika: Economic Restructuring**
Soon after taking office, Gorbachev introduced **perestroika** (“restructuring”), a program aimed at decentralizing economic management, introducing market-style incentives, and boosting productivity. Key measures included:

* **Law on State Enterprise (1987)**: Granted factories and enterprises greater autonomy over production targets, pricing, and profits.
* **Cooperatives (1988)**: Legalization of small privately owned businesses in services, manufacturing, and agriculture.
* **Joint Ventures**: Encouraged foreign investment through partnerships between Soviet state enterprises and Western firms.

While intended to rejuvenate the command economy, perestroika’s gradual rollout and resistance from entrenched bureaucrats produced uneven results. Shortages persisted, inflation began to rise, and public expectations for rapid improvement went largely unmet.

**Glasnost and Democratization**
Complementing economic reforms, **glasnost** (“openness”) liberalized public discourse and reduced censorship. Media outlets gained the freedom to investigate official corruption, publish previously banned literature, and critique government policies. This newfound openness extended to:

* **Historical Reassessment**: Official acknowledgment of past abuses, including the Stalinist purges and the 1986 Chernobyl disaster cover-up.
* **Political Pluralism**: Introduction of contested elections within the CPSU and for the newly established Congress of People’s Deputies (1989), allowing independent candidates and non-party groups to participate.

Glasnost unleashed pent-up demands for political participation and national self-determination among the USSR’s diverse republics, setting in motion centrifugal forces that would later challenge the integrity of the Soviet state.

**Foreign Policy and the End of the Cold War**
Under Gorbachev, Soviet foreign policy underwent a dramatic shift from confrontation to engagement:

* **“New Thinking”**: Prioritized mutual security over ideological struggle, leading to arms-control breakthroughs such as the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty (1987).
* **Withdrawal from Afghanistan**: Completed the Soviet military exit by February 1989, reducing a costly and unpopular engagement.
* **Non-intervention in Eastern Europe**: Gorbachev signaled that the Brezhnev Doctrine was obsolete, refraining from using Soviet troops to prop up communist regimes in Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, and elsewhere.

These positions facilitated the peaceful collapse of Soviet-backed governments across the Eastern Bloc in 1989, culminating in the fall of the Berlin Wall and German reunification.

**Internal Challenges and Decline**
Despite international successes, Gorbachev faced mounting domestic difficulties. The twin pressures of economic malaise and rising nationalist movements in the Baltic republics and the Caucasus tested central authority. In response, he experimented with a “Union Treaty” to redefine relations between the USSR and its republics, granting greater autonomy while preserving a federative structure. However, the treaty faltered as republic leaders demanded full sovereignty.

**August 1991 Coup Attempt and Resignation**
Conservative party and security-service elements opposed to rapid reform staged a coup from 19 to 21 August 1991, detaining Gorbachev at his Crimean dacha and declaring a state of emergency. The coup collapsed after mass protests in Moscow and resistance led by Boris Yeltsin, then President of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic. Although Gorbachev was restored to office, his authority was irreparably weakened. On 24 August 1991, he resigned as General Secretary, and the CPSU suspended its activities pending reorganization.

**Legacy of the General Secretaryship**
Gorbachev’s period as General Secretary reshaped both the Soviet Union and global politics. His reforms dismantled the authoritarian structures of the CPSU and introduced unprecedented political freedoms, but also unleashed centrifugal forces that contributed to the USSR’s dissolution in December 1991. Internationally, his “new thinking” facilitated the end of the Cold War and enabled new frameworks for cooperation between East and West. Domestically, the legacies of glasnost and perestroika endure in the form of greater media freedom, multiparty politics in the post-Soviet states, and ongoing debates over the balance between state control and market forces.

Viewed through contrasting lenses, Gorbachev’s general secretaryship is praised for its courageous break with rigid orthodoxy and faulted for the economic dislocation that accompanied rapid change. Nonetheless, his tenure remains a defining chapter in 20th-century history, exemplifying the challenges of reforming entrenched political and economic systems under the pressures of popular demand and geopolitical realignment.
