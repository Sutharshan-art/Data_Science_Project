**Term Limits in Russia**

**Introduction**
Term limits in the Russian Federation define the maximum number of consecutive terms that certain elected officials—most notably the President—may serve. Established in the 1993 Constitution, and subsequently amended, these provisions have played a central role in Russia’s post-Soviet political evolution, shaping power transitions and contributing to debates about democratic governance and authoritarian consolidation.

**Constitutional Foundations (1993)**
Following the dissolution of the Soviet Union, Russia adopted a new Constitution by national referendum on 12 December 1993. Article 81(3) of the 1993 Constitution stipulates that a President “may not hold the presidency for more than two consecutive terms.” Each presidential term was set at four years (extended to six years in 2008), thereby limiting incumbents to a maximum of eight years in office before stepping aside for at least one term.

**Early Practice: Yeltsin and the First Term-Limit Test**
Boris Yeltsin, first elected President of the Russian Federation in 1991 and re-elected in 1996 under the 1993 Constitution, was the first to confront the new term-limit regime. In August 1999, citing health reasons, Yeltsin resigned before the end of his second term, allowing Prime Minister Vladimir Putin to assume the presidency in an acting capacity. Putin subsequently won the 2000 election, beginning Russia’s post-Yeltsin leadership era.

**Putin–Medvedev Tandem (2000–2012)**
Vladimir Putin served two consecutive four-year terms (2000–2008), in compliance with the term-limit provision. In 2008, unable to seek a third consecutive term, Putin endorsed Dmitry Medvedev as his successor. Medvedev’s election preserved the “tandem” arrangement: Putin became Prime Minister (2008–2012) and wielded significant influence behind the scenes. In 2008, the Duma and Federation Council approved a constitutional amendment extending the presidential term from four to six years, effective for future officeholders, thereby allowing Medvedev to serve a six-year term (2008–2012).

**Resetting Terms: 2020 Constitutional Amendments**
In January 2020, President Putin proposed—and Parliament rapidly approved—a package of constitutional amendments, later ratified by a nationwide referendum in July 2020. Among these changes was a “zero-reset” clause (Article 81.3), which effectively annulled previous presidential terms for Putin personally, permitting him to run again in 2024 and 2030. The amendment preserved the two-consecutive-term limit but counted terms served prior to the vote as never having occurred, enabling a potential cumulative tenure extending through 2036.

**Political Context and Debate**
Proponents of the term-limit reset argued that continuity of leadership would ensure policy stability amid global challenges, economic modernization efforts, and the aftermath of geopolitical crises. Critics contended that the zero-reset undermined the spirit of constitutional checks and balances, consolidating power around a single leader and eroding democratic norms. International observers and domestic opposition groups described the 2020 amendments as part of a broader authoritarian shift, noting that restrictive measures on political competition and media freedom accompanied the constitutional changes.

**Comparative and Historical Perspectives**
Russia’s experience with term limits contrasts with other post-communist states in Eastern Europe, many of which also incorporated presidential term limits into their democracies but have varied in enforcement. During the Soviet era, no formal limits existed for the General Secretary of the Communist Party, leading to lifelong tenures. The introduction—and subsequent circumvention—of limits in the Russian Federation reflects an ongoing tension between institutional rule of law and personalized leadership.

**Other Offices and Sub‐Federal Term Limits**
While the presidential term limit has attracted the most attention, Russia has also regulated terms for other elected posts. The 1993 Constitution capped service for Federation Council members indirectly, but later reforms abolished direct gubernatorial elections (2004–2012) and introduced their own term constraints. Since 2012, most governors are again directly elected with two-consecutive-term limits, although federal authorities retain appointment powers for acting governors, creating flexibility in enforcing these limits.

**Impact on Political Succession**
Term-limit rules have shaped intra-elite bargaining and jockeying for succession. The 2008 Putin–Medvedev arrangement demonstrated an institutional workaround that maintained power continuity without overt constitutional violation. The 2020 reset further institutionalized a model in which apparent constitutional compliance masked practical extension of incumbency, affecting the dynamics of political renewal and opposition viability.

**Conclusion**
Term limits in Russia, originally adopted to prevent indefinite incumbencies and promote leadership turnover, have been subject to significant reinterpretation and amendment. From Yeltsin’s adherence to the 1993 Constitution through the Putin–Medvedev tandem and the 2020 zero-reset, Russia’s term-limit history illustrates the interplay between constitutional design, political strategy, and the evolution of power in a semi-authoritarian system. As of 2025, with the reset in place, the Russian presidency remains structured to allow extended tenures under the guise of constitutional legitimacy, continuing to provoke debate over democratic governance and institutional integrity.
