**Ronald Reagan**

**Early Life and Career**
Ronald Wilson Reagan was born on February 6, 1911, in Tampico, Illinois. He graduated from Eureka College in 1932 with a degree in economics and sociology and began a career in radio broadcasting. In 1937, he moved to Hollywood, signing a contract with Warner Bros. and appearing in over 50 films, including *Knute Rockne, All American* (1940) and *Kings Row* (1942). During World War II, Reagan served in the Army Reserve’s film unit. After the war, he became president of the Screen Actors Guild (SAG), where he developed a reputation for anti‐communism and labor negotiation.

**Entry into Politics**
Having shifted from liberal to conservative viewpoints in the late 1950s, Reagan entered politics as a Republican. He delivered a nationally broadcast address, “A Time for Choosing,” in support of Barry Goldwater’s 1964 presidential campaign, establishing himself as a leading voice of the conservative movement. In 1966, Reagan was elected Governor of California, defeating Democratic incumbent Pat Brown. He was reelected in 1970 and served two terms (1967–1975), focusing on welfare reform, tax cuts, and law-and-order policies, while navigating student protests and budgetary challenges.

**Presidential Campaigns and Election**
Reagan sought the Republican presidential nomination in 1976 but narrowly lost to incumbent President Gerald Ford. Undeterred, he launched a second bid in 1980, advocating supply-side economics—later dubbed “Reaganomics”—a strong anti-communist foreign policy, and a restoration of national confidence. He secured the nomination and defeated Democratic President Jimmy Carter in a landslide, winning 489 electoral votes to Carter’s 49 and carrying 44 states with 50.7% of the popular vote.

**First Term (1981–1985)**
Reagan was inaugurated on January 20, 1981. Thirteen days later, he survived an assassination attempt by John Hinckley Jr. His administration pursued major tax cuts under the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, which reduced marginal rates across income brackets, and the Tax Reform Act of 1986, which simplified the tax code. He sought to curb inflation and spur growth through deregulation, reductions in nondefense spending, and tight monetary policy guided by Federal Reserve Chairman Paul Volcker.

On foreign policy, Reagan escalated defense spending, expanded the U.S. nuclear arsenal, and launched the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI), a research program for missile defense. He strengthened alliances through NATO, condemned the Soviet Union as an “evil empire,” and authorized covert support for anti-communist insurgencies under the Reagan Doctrine. In 1983, he deployed U.S. Marines to Lebanon (withdrew after the Beirut barracks bombing) and invaded Grenada in Operation Urgent Fury.

**Second Term (1985–1989)**
Reagan won reelection in 1984, defeating former Vice President Walter Mondale in a historic landslide—49 states, 525 electoral votes, and 58.8% of the popular vote. His second term was marked by a thaw in Cold War tensions following Mikhail Gorbachev’s rise as Soviet General Secretary. The two leaders held summits in Geneva (1985), Reykjavik (1986), and Washington (1987), culminating in the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty, the first agreement to eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons.

Domestically, the administration grappled with growing budget deficits, which reached nearly 6% of GDP by 1986, driven by tax cuts and defense spending. The 1986 Tax Reform Act lowered the top individual rate to 28% and closed loopholes. The decade’s economic expansion averaged over 4% annual GDP growth, while inflation fell from double digits to around 4% by 1988. However, income inequality widened, and savings-and-loan failures prompted calls for financial-sector reform.

**Iran–Contra Affair**
In late 1986, disclosures emerged that senior administration officials had secretly facilitated arms sales to Iran—then under an arms embargo—and diverted proceeds to fund Contra rebels in Nicaragua, in violation of the Boland Amendment. The scandal led to congressional investigations and independent counsel indictments of key figures, though Reagan maintained he was unaware of the full operation. The episode tarnished the administration’s reputation for strict adherence to the rule of law.

**Retirement and Legacy**
Reagan left office on January 20, 1989, handing the presidency to Vice President George H. W. Bush. His post‐presidential years were spent at his ranch in California and at the Reagan Library in Simi Valley. In 1994, he disclosed a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease and withdrew from public life. Ronald Reagan died on June 5, 2004, at the age of 93 and was interred at the Reagan Library.

Reagan’s legacy is the subject of vigorous debate. Supporters credit him with revitalizing the U.S. economy, restoring national morale, and contributing decisively to the end of the Cold War. Critics argue that his policies increased deficits, deregulated industries excessively, and exacerbated social inequalities. Nevertheless, decades after his presidency, he remains an iconic figure in American conservative thought and 20th-century political history.
