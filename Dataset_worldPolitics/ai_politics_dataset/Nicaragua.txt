**Nicaragua**, officially the **Republic of Nicaragua**, is the largest country in Central America by area and the second most populous, with a diverse history marked by indigenous civilizations, Spanish colonization, periods of dictatorship, revolutionary upheaval, and contemporary democratic developments. Its political trajectory has been shaped by internal struggles between conservative and liberal factions, foreign intervention, and the enduring influence of the Sandinista movement.

## Pre‐Colonial and Colonial Period

Prior to Spanish contact, the territory of modern Nicaragua was inhabited by diverse indigenous groups, including the Nicarao in the west and the Miskito and other Mesoamerican and Caribbean‐affiliated peoples in the east. In 1524, Spanish conquistador Francisco Hernández de Córdoba founded the Pacific‐coast cities of Granada and León, which became centers of colonial administration. The colony formed part of the Captaincy General of Guatemala within the Viceroyalty of New Spain. Throughout the 16th–18th centuries, Spanish authorities alternated between indirect rule over indigenous communities and importation of African slaves to work in coastal plantations.

## Independence and Early Republic (1821–1909)

Following the collapse of Spanish rule in 1821, Nicaragua joined the short‐lived Mexican Empire under Agustín de Iturbide and then the Federal Republic of Central America (1823–1838). After the federation’s dissolution, Nicaragua became fully independent in 1838. Its early republican period was defined by bitter rivalry between the Conservative Party, based in Granada, and the Liberal Party, centered in León. Frequent civil wars and power struggles hindered nation‐building. In 1855, American filibuster William Walker briefly seized power, reinstating slavery before being expelled by a coalition of Central American armies.

## The Somoza Dynasty (1936–1979)

In 1936, General Anastasio Somoza García, head of the U.S.–trained National Guard, orchestrated a coup and was elected president, inaugurating the Somoza family’s autocratic rule. Over the next four decades, Somoza and his sons—Luis Somoza Debayle (1956–1963) and Anastasio Somoza Debayle (1967–1972, 1974–1979)—controlled Nicaragua through a patronage network, repression of dissent, and close ties to U.S. economic and security interests. Although the regime spurred some economic growth, it was marked by widespread corruption, human‐rights abuses, and deep social inequality.

## Sandinista Revolution and Civil War (1979–1990)

Opposition coalesced around the Sandinista National Liberation Front (Frente Sandinista de Liberación Nacional, FSLN), a socialist guerrilla movement named after Augusto César Sandino, who had led resistance to U.S. occupation in the 1920s–30s. In July 1979, the FSLN overthrew Somoza Debayle, establishing a revolutionary government under Daniel Ortega. The Sandinistas implemented land reform, literacy campaigns, and health programs, aligning with leftist states and soliciting aid from Cuba and the Soviet bloc.

Meanwhile, the U.S. government—under Presidents Reagan and Bush—viewed the Sandinistas as Soviet proxies and funded the Contra insurgency, a loose coalition of former National Guard members and rural anti‐Sandinista groups. The ensuing civil war (c. 1981–1990) destabilized the country, causing human‐rights violations on both sides and economic hardship exacerbated by U.S. economic sanctions. Under international pressure and a UN‐brokered ceasefire, free elections were held in February 1990.

## Transition to Electoral Democracy (1990–2006)

In 1990, Violeta Chamorro of the center‐right National Opposition Union (UNO) defeated Ortega, becoming the first female president in the Americas. Her administration (1990–1997) prioritized demobilization of combatants, structural adjustment policies advocated by the International Monetary Fund, and reconciliation efforts. Arnoldo Alemán (1997–2002) of the Liberal Alliance focused on economic liberalization and infrastructure but was later convicted of corruption. Enrique Bolaños (2002–2007) continued anti‐corruption campaigns and negotiated debt relief under the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries initiative, while FSLN reorganized in opposition under Ortega’s leadership.

## Daniel Ortega’s Return and the FSLN Era (2007–Present)

In November 2006, Daniel Ortega was re‐elected president, returning the Sandinistas to power. His subsequent terms have seen constitutional reforms eliminating presidential term limits, consolidation of executive authority, and increasing control over the judiciary, electoral council, and media. Critics accuse Ortega of eroding democratic institutions, repressing political dissent—including the violent crackdown on anti‐government protests in 2018—and marginalizing opposition parties. Supporters highlight social programs aimed at poverty reduction, rural electrification, and literacy.

## Political System and Parties

Nicaragua is formally a presidential republic, with executive power vested in the president, who serves five‐year terms (renewable). Legislative authority resides in the unicameral National Assembly (Asamblea Nacional) of 92 seats, elected by proportional representation. Major parties include the ruling FSLN, the Constitutionalist Liberal Party (PLC), the independent Citizen’s Alliance (AC), and smaller leftist and centrist groups. Nicaragua is also divided into 15 departments and two autonomous regions on the Caribbean coast, each with its own regional councils.

## Foreign Relations and Regional Role

Nicaragua’s foreign policy has oscillated between alignment with the United States—especially during the Somoza era and transitional governments—and turns toward leftist governments in Latin America during Sandinista rule. Ortega’s government has strengthened ties with Venezuela, Russia, and China, receiving substantial development assistance. Nicaragua remains a member of the Central American Integration System (SICA), the Organization of American States (OAS), and the Community of Latin American and Caribbean States (CELAC).

## Socio‐Economic Challenges

Despite periods of growth driven by agriculture, remittances, and tourism, Nicaragua remains one of the region’s poorest countries. Key challenges include land tenure conflicts, limited industrial diversification, vulnerability to natural disasters, and improving indicators in education, healthcare, and infrastructure. The Ortega government’s social policies have produced gains in literacy and maternal health but are scrutinized for targeting political loyalty.

## Legacy and Contemporary Significance

Nicaragua’s political history reflects the interplay of domestic ideologies—liberalism, conservatism, socialism—and external influences, notably U.S. interventionism and Cold War geopolitics. The Sandinista movement remains central to national identity, representing both revolutionary aspirations and contested governance practices. Nicaragua’s future trajectory hinges on the balance between democratic renewal, respect for human rights, and strategies for inclusive economic development.
