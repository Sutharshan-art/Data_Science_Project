\==Introduction==
The Supreme Soviet of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) was the highest legislative body of the Soviet state from its establishment in 1938 until its dissolution in December 1991. Constitutionally vested with supreme authority, it was empowered to enact laws, approve state plans and budgets, amend the constitution, and ratify international treaties. In practice, however, real policymaking power resided with the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU) and its Politburo, rendering the Supreme Soviet largely a ritual forum for rubber-stamping decisions already made by party leaders.

\==Establishment and Legal Basis==
The Supreme Soviet was created by the 1936 Soviet Constitution to replace the Congress of Soviets as the nominal legislature of the USSR. It first convened in 1938, meeting biennially until 1977 and thereafter every five years under the 1977 Constitution. The body was vested with plenipotentiary legislative authority: it held the exclusive right to amend the constitution, approve national economic plans (called “Five-Year Plans”), and confirm appointments to top state posts, including the Chairman of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet (the de jure head of state).

\==Structure and Composition==
The Supreme Soviet was a bicameral body, comprising two equal chambers:

* **Soviet of the Union**, representing citizens based on population, with one deputy per 300,000 inhabitants.
* **Soviet of Nationalities**, representing the USSR’s federal structure, with deputies allocated to union republics, autonomous republics, autonomous regions (okrugs), and national districts, irrespective of population size.
  Each chamber elected the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet—a permanent body acting on behalf of the legislature between sessions. The Presidium wielded significant authority, issuing decrees, interpreting laws, and calling sessions.

\==Powers and Functions==
Formally, the Supreme Soviet exercised comprehensive state powers:

* **Legislation**: Enacting laws proposed by the Presidium, Council of Ministers, or union republic legislatures.
* **Constitutional Amendments**: Revising the constitution required approval by two successive convocations.
* **Economic Planning**: Ratifying and monitoring implementation of central economic plans and state budgets.
* **Foreign Policy**: Ratifying treaties and declaring states of war or peace.
* **Appointments**: Confirming the Council of Ministers (the government), Supreme Court justices, and Procurator General.

In reality, these actions followed directives set by the CPSU Central Committee, leaving the Supreme Soviet a formal endorsement mechanism rather than an autonomous policymaking institution.

\==Electoral System and Sessions==
Deputies were elected from single-member constituencies through indirect nominations tightly controlled by the CPSU. Until 1989, elections featured only one official candidate per seat, ensuring Communist dominance. Sessions of the full Supreme Soviet, usually lasting only a few weeks, approved legislation prepared in advance by party organs. Between convocations, the Presidium acted as the legislature, issuing decrees that had the force of law until confirmed by the next session.

\==Political Role and Party Relationship==
The Supreme Soviet functioned within the Soviet system of “democratic centralism,” where policymaking was centralized in the CPSU. Party congresses and the Central Committee defined major policies; the Supreme Soviet enacted them into law. The dual role of top officials—many serving simultaneously in party and state posts—ensured the legislature’s subordination. The Chairman of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet, though nominal head of state, held limited independent authority and was typically a figurehead rather than a policymaker.

\==Key Legislative Actions==
Throughout its history, the Supreme Soviet enacted landmark legislation:

* **Stalin Constitution (1936)**: Established the Supreme Soviet itself and formalized citizens’ rights and duties.
* **Post-Stalin Reforms**: Amendments under Nikita Khrushchev facilitated de-Stalinization and introduced limited decentralization.
* **1977 Constitution**: Reaffirmed the leading role of the CPSU and codified citizens’ economic, social, and political rights while maintaining party supremacy.
* **Gorbachev Era Reforms (1988–1991)**: Under perestroika and glasnost, the Supreme Soviet’s 1988 elections allowed multiple candidates per seat and, for the first time, non-party deputies. The 1990 session enacted laws permitting republic sovereignty, legalizing other parties, and reducing the party’s monopoly on power.

\==Decline and Dissolution==
The Supreme Soviet’s decline accelerated during the late 1980s as Mikhail Gorbachev’s reforms weakened the CPSU’s control and empowered republic-level legislatures and popular deputies. The 1991 attempted coup by hardline party members further discredited the CPSU and accelerated the USSR’s fragmentation. On 26 December 1991, the Supreme Soviet of the USSR formally voted to dissolve the Soviet Union and transfer its powers to the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS), bringing an end to its jurisdiction and marking the conclusion of the Soviet state.

\==Legacy==
Though often criticized as a rubber-stamp body, the Supreme Soviet’s transformation during perestroika demonstrated its latent potential to act as an arena for genuine political contestation. Its late-1980s sessions introduced multiparty elements, legal pluralism, and asserted republican sovereignty—developments that contributed directly to the USSR’s dissolution. The Supreme Soviet’s history thus reflects both the rigid centralism of Soviet government and the unexpected currents of reform that reshaped the political landscape of Eastern Europe and Central Asia at the end of the 20th century.
