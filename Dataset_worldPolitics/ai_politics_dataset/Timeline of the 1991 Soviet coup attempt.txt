**Timeline of the 1991 Soviet Coup Attempt**

The 1991 Soviet coup attempt—also known as the August Coup—took place from 19 to 21 August 1991 and represented the last significant challenge to Mikhail Gorbachev’s reform agenda and the Soviet Union’s existing structure. Orchestrated by hard‐line members of the Communist Party, the coup aimed to reverse Gorbachev’s policies of **perestroika** (restructuring) and **glasnost** (openness) and to forestall the impending union treaty that would decentralize power to the republics. Although the plot collapsed within three days, it accelerated the Soviet collapse and boosted the authority of reformers such as Russian President Boris Yeltsin.

**Background**

* **March 1990–July 1991:** Gorbachev’s reforms—including the December 1990 announcement to relinquish the Communist Party’s constitutional monopoly—triggered resistance from conservatives within the **State Committee on the State of Emergency** (GKChP), a group of eight senior officials.
* **July 1991:** Gorbachev and leaders of nine Soviet republics signed the New Union Treaty in Alma-Ata, paving the way for a looser federation. Hard‐liners feared the treaty’s ratification (due 20 August) would irrevocably weaken central authority.

**18 August 1991**

* **Evening:** While Gorbachev was on vacation at his dacha in Foros, Crimea, the GKChP convened secretly in Moscow. Key members included Vice President Gennady Yanayev, Prime Minister Valentin Pavlov, KGB Chairman Vladimir Kryuchkov, Defense Minister Dmitry Yazov, and Interior Minister Boris Pugo.
* **Late Night:** The committee drafted an emergency decree alleging Gorbachev’s inability to govern due to health issues and declared a state of emergency nationwide, deploying troops to Moscow and key cities.

**19 August 1991**

* **Early Hours:** Military units—most notably the **Taman** and **Tamanskaya Motor Rifle Divisions**—rolled into Moscow without insignia, occupying strategic locations around the Kremlin, White House (Russian parliament), and television studios.
* **08:00:** State television interrupted programming to announce that Gorbachev was under house arrest in Foros; Yanayev addressed the nation as “acting president.”
* **10:00:** Boris Yeltsin, President of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic (RSFSR), rallied supporters atop a tank outside the White House, condemning the coup as unconstitutional.
* **Throughout the Day:** Mass demonstrations erupted in Moscow and Leningrad, with citizens erecting barricades and gathering at Russian and Ukrainian parliament buildings. Information blackouts and press censorship sparked popular unrest rather than compliance.

**20 August 1991**

* **Morning:** Conflicting orders paralyzed the chain of command. Defense Minister Yazov assured Yeltsin that the military would remain neutral. KGB head Kryuchkov withdrew support for the coup, fearing reprisals if it failed.
* **Afternoon:** Gorbachev, still in Foros, released a videotaped statement rejecting the committee’s actions and refusing to resign. GKChP members—surrounded by family at their dachas—began to waver.
* **Evening:** Military units withdrew from key positions; the White House defenders maintained control. Local Soviet republics, led by Ukraine and the Baltic states, denounced the coup and accelerated declarations of independence.

**21 August 1991**

* **Early Morning:** Key coup leaders surrendered to authorities at the Moscow military district headquarters. Yanayev and Pavlov were arrested and flown to Moscow’s Lefortovo Prison.
* **09:00:** Gorbachev returned to Moscow under heavy security and resumed his duties as president. He denounced the coup as a “criminal gang” action.
* **Later that Day:** Yeltsin and Gorbachev jointly appeared at the Kremlin, signaling a fragile unity. Yeltsin’s RSFSR government assumed control over the Communist Party’s property and communications, initiating the party’s rapid loss of power.

**Aftermath and Political Consequences**

* **Late August–September 1991:** The RSFSR declared the Communist Party illegal on its territory; similar bans followed in Ukraine and the Baltic states.
* **December 1991:** Amid rising secessionist momentum, leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus signed the Belavezha Accords, formally dissolving the Soviet Union and establishing the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS). Gorbachev resigned on 25 December 1991.
* **Legal Proceedings:** Coup leaders were tried in the Moscow Military District court. Yanayev, Pavlov, Kryuchkov, and Yazov received suspended sentences in 1994 under an amnesty law; several others committed suicide during or after the coup’s collapse.

**Historical Significance**
The August Coup crystallized the Soviet Union’s internal contradictions, demonstrating that neither military force nor bureaucratic decree could suppress popular demand for reform and national self‐determination. Yeltsin’s defiance elevated him to a preeminent political role, while Gorbachev’s weakened presidency presaged the Soviet system’s end. The coup’s failure emboldened republics—from Lithuania to Azerbaijan—to pursue independence, making it a pivotal moment in the 20th century’s geopolitical landscape.
