**Chetniks**

The **Chetniks** were a Yugoslav royalist and Serbian nationalist movement that emerged during the early 20th century and played a prominent role during World War II. The term broadly refers to various paramilitary formations, but it is most commonly associated with the **Yugoslav Army in the Homeland** (*Jugoslovenska vojska u otadžbini*, or JVUO), a royalist resistance movement led by **General Draža Mihailović**. Active primarily in Serbia, Montenegro, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and parts of Croatia, the Chetniks initially resisted Axis occupation but later became involved in complex alliances and conflicts, including collaboration with Axis powers and violent rivalries with communist partisans.

The Chetnik movement remains a deeply controversial subject in the historiography and politics of the former Yugoslavia, viewed alternately as a legitimate royalist resistance or as a reactionary, collaborationist force implicated in ethnic violence and war crimes.

---

### Origins and Early History

The term “Chetnik” originally referred to Serbian guerrilla fighters engaged in insurgency against the Ottoman Empire in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, particularly during the **Macedonian Struggle** (1903–1908) and the **Balkan Wars** (1912–1913). These irregular bands, often operating with the support of the Kingdom of Serbia, aimed to protect Serbian populations and assert control over contested territories in the Ottoman Balkans.

During World War I, Chetnik units were loosely affiliated with the Serbian Army and conducted guerrilla operations behind enemy lines. Their nationalist ethos, irregular warfare tactics, and allegiance to the Serbian monarchy provided a foundational identity for future incarnations of the movement.

---

### World War II and the Yugoslav Army in the Homeland

The most well-known and politically significant phase of Chetnik activity occurred during **World War II**, following the Axis invasion and occupation of the Kingdom of Yugoslavia in April 1941. In response to the occupation and the collapse of the royal Yugoslav Army, **Colonel Draža Mihailović**, a monarchist officer, formed the Chetnik movement under the nominal command of the Yugoslav government-in-exile.

The Chetniks initially positioned themselves as a resistance force against Axis occupation and a loyalist army seeking the restoration of the Yugoslav monarchy. Mihailović’s strategy emphasized cautious engagement with Axis forces, prioritizing the preservation of Serbian lives and waiting for an Allied landing in the Balkans.

---

### Rivalry with the Partisans

The Chetniks' main rival was the **Yugoslav Partisans**, a communist-led resistance movement organized by **Josip Broz Tito**. While both groups began as anti-Axis resistance forces, their ideological, strategic, and national goals diverged significantly. The Partisans advocated for a multi-ethnic socialist federation and pursued a more aggressive policy of armed resistance.

By late 1941, tensions between the Chetniks and Partisans escalated into open conflict. The Chetniks increasingly viewed the Partisans as a greater threat than the occupying forces, due to their revolutionary agenda and potential to destabilize Serbian society. This rivalry would dominate the internal dynamics of the Yugoslav resistance, often overshadowing the broader struggle against the Axis.

---

### Collaboration and Controversy

As the war progressed, elements of the Chetnik movement entered into tactical collaborations with Axis forces, including the **Italians** and later the **Germans**, particularly in anti-Partisan operations. These collaborations were justified by Mihailović and others as necessary for survival and the eventual defeat of communism.

However, such actions undermined Allied support, particularly from Britain and the United States. Reports of Chetnik collaboration, inaction against Axis forces, and involvement in atrocities against non-Serb civilians—especially Bosniaks, Croats, and Muslims—led the Allies to shift their support to Tito’s Partisans in 1943.

Chetnik units were implicated in **ethnically motivated violence**, including massacres, forced expulsions, and the destruction of villages. While the leadership claimed to represent a pan-Yugoslav monarchy, the movement increasingly adopted **Serbian nationalist** goals, including the vision of an ethnically homogeneous Greater Serbia.

---

### Defeat and Post-War Repression

By the end of World War II, the Chetniks had lost significant ground to the Partisans, who received growing Allied support and emerged as the dominant resistance force. Tito's forces took control of Yugoslavia in 1945, establishing a communist government and abolishing the monarchy.

Mihailović was captured in 1946, tried by the new regime for war crimes and collaboration, and executed. Many Chetnik fighters were killed in the immediate post-war purges or fled the country. The movement was banned under Yugoslavia's socialist government and officially labeled as traitorous.

---

### Legacy and Historical Debate

The legacy of the Chetniks remains a subject of **intense historical and political controversy** in the former Yugoslavia. In **Serbia**, some nationalist and monarchist groups have sought to rehabilitate the Chetnik movement, portraying Mihailović as a patriotic anti-fascist hero. In contrast, many historians and communities in Bosnia, Croatia, and Montenegro regard the Chetniks as perpetrators of war crimes and ethnic violence.

In 2015, Serbia’s government annulled Mihailović’s wartime conviction, a move condemned by critics as historical revisionism. International historians continue to debate the extent and nature of Chetnik collaboration and atrocities, with much depending on interpretations of wartime sources and political context.

---

### Conclusion

The Chetnik movement occupies a complex and contested place in 20th-century Balkan history. While initially conceived as a royalist resistance force, its wartime actions, ideological shifts, and tactical alliances significantly shaped its historical reputation. The Chetniks’ rivalry with the Partisans and their role in the ethnopolitical landscape of World War II Yugoslavia continue to provoke debate and influence political discourse in the region.
