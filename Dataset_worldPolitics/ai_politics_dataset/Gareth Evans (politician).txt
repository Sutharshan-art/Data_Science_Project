Gareth John Evans AC QC (born 26 May 1944) is an Australian statesman, academic and international policy expert whose career has spanned national politics, diplomacy and global governance. He served as a Senator for Victoria from 1978 to 1996 and held senior ministerial posts in the Hawke and Keating Labor governments, most notably as Attorney-General (1983–1984), Minister for Resources and Energy (1984–1987), Minister for Trade (1988–1990), Minister for Foreign Affairs (1988–1996) and leader of the Government in the Senate (1993–1996). After leaving politics, Evans became a prominent voice in international relations, serving as President of the International Crisis Group (2000–2009), Chancellor of the Australian National University (2010–2016) and Co-Convener of the International Commission on Nuclear Non-Proliferation and Disarmament (2008–2009).

**Early life and legal career**
Born in Heidelberg, Victoria, Evans was educated at Wesley College, Melbourne, and the University of Melbourne, where he earned an LLB with honours. He was admitted to the Victorian Bar in 1968 and developed a reputation in constitutional and administrative law. Active in the Australian Labor Party from his student days, he served as president of the Melbourne University ALP Club before entering federal politics.

**Entry into federal politics**
In 1978, Evans won preselection for the Senate vacancy caused by Lionel Murphy’s resignation. He was appointed to fill the vacancy in August and re-elected in 1980 and 1987. As a backbencher, he built expertise in legal and constitutional affairs, and his analytical style and consensus-building skills caught the attention of party leaders.

**Ministerial career under Hawke and Keating**
Shortly after Bob Hawke became Prime Minister in March 1983, Evans was appointed Attorney-General and Minister for Resources and Energy. He oversaw major reforms in energy policy, including the restructuring of state electricity commissions and the introduction of market-based pricing. In 1988—under a second Hawke reshuffle merged with Prime Minister Paul Keating’s ascendancy—Evans took on the foreign affairs portfolio, a position he retained through the entire Keating government until March 1996.

As Foreign Minister, Evans championed Australia’s active engagement in regional and global diplomacy. He played a pivotal role in:

* **East Timor**: Following the UN-sponsored referendum in 1999, Evans’ groundwork in Canberra helped shape the international community’s response to Indonesia’s withdrawal and the establishment of the United Nations Transitional Administration in East Timor.
* **Nuclear non-proliferation**: He co-authored the Canberra Commission Report in 1996 and later co-convened the International Commission on Nuclear Non-Proliferation and Disarmament, arguing for practical pathways toward global nuclear disarmament.
* **Asia-Pacific forums**: Under his stewardship, Australia secured its place as a leading middle power, strengthening ties through APEC, ASEAN Regional Forum, and bilateral partnerships across Southeast Asia.
* **Global human rights**: Evans was a strong advocate for linking human rights considerations to trade and aid policies, pushing for the integration of democratic governance standards in Australia’s international engagements.

He also served as Leader of the Government in the Senate from 1993, skillfully managing complex legislation through the upper house during a period of often fragile parliamentary arithmetic.

**Post-parliamentary roles and academic career**
After electoral defeat in 1996, Evans transitioned to international policy work. In 2000 he became President of the International Crisis Group, an independent organisation dedicated to preventing and resolving deadly conflict. During his nine-year tenure, he oversaw expansion of field operations in Africa, Asia, and Eastern Europe, championing early warning systems and diplomatic interventions.

Returning to Australia in 2009, Evans took up academic appointments at the University of Melbourne, including Director of the Centre for the Study of Armed Conflict and Society. In 2010 he was elected Chancellor of the Australian National University, a role in which he advocated for strengthening ties between academia, government and civil society.

Evans has authored influential books and numerous articles on foreign policy, international law and governance. He is a frequent commentator on Asia-Pacific security issues, nuclear disarmament and climate diplomacy. In recognition of his service to Australia and to international affairs, he was appointed a Companion of the Order of Australia in 1999.

**Political philosophy and legacy**
Evans is widely regarded as a leading exemplar of “constructive middle-power diplomacy.” He emphasised multilateralism, preventive diplomacy, and the integration of human rights with strategic interests. His tenure helped reposition Australia as a broker in regional disputes and as a proactive advocate for global norms on non-proliferation and human security. His influence extended beyond government through his scholarly work and leadership of global policy institutions, shaping the way states, NGOs and international bodies approach conflict resolution.

**Personal life**
Evans married Margaret Crawford in 1968; they have three children. An avid reader and occasional rugby union referee, he remains active in public debates on foreign policy and national security. As of 2025, he chairs several advisory boards and continues to lecture internationally on diplomacy and international law.
