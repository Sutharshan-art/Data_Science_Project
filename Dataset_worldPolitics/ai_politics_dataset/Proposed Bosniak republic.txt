**Proposed Bosniak Republic**

The **Proposed Bosniak Republic** refers to various historical and political initiatives, primarily during and following the Bosnian War (1992–1995), that sought to establish a distinct political entity or autonomous state for Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims) within or outside the boundaries of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Although such a republic never materialized as a sovereign state, the concept emerged in reaction to the ethnic partitioning of Bosnia and Herzegovina during the conflict and the competing national aspirations of Serbs and Croats in the region.

The idea of a Bosniak republic was largely a response to the creation of Serb and Croat political and territorial units—namely Republika Srpska and the Croatian Republic of Herzeg-Bosnia—during the war, and to international peace plans that proposed the ethnic federalization or division of Bosnia and Herzegovina. While Bosniak leadership generally favored a unified, multiethnic Bosnian state, the possibility of a Bosniak-majority republic gained temporary traction during peace negotiations and in post-war discourse.

---

### Background

Bosnia and Herzegovina, a former republic of socialist Yugoslavia, declared independence in 1992 following a referendum boycotted by the Serb population. The declaration led to a brutal conflict involving Bosniak, Croat, and Serb factions, each with different goals: Serbs aimed to remain within a Yugoslav framework or unite with Serbia; Croats supported autonomy or unification with Croatia; and Bosniaks, who constituted the largest single ethnic group, generally advocated for a unified, independent state.

As the war escalated, the de facto territorial fragmentation of Bosnia and Herzegovina occurred along ethnic lines. Serb forces declared the establishment of the **Republika Srpska**, while Croats formed the **Croatian Republic of Herzeg-Bosnia**. In contrast, Bosniak-controlled areas were not formally organized into a separate republic, although they were represented by the internationally recognized **Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina**, with its capital in Sarajevo.

---

### Emergence of the Bosniak Republic Concept

The idea of a Bosniak republic emerged primarily as a reaction to peace proposals that envisioned the division of Bosnia and Herzegovina into ethnic federal units or even full territorial partition. During the **Owen-Stoltenberg Plan** (1993), which proposed a decentralized Bosnia composed of three ethnic republics (Serb, Croat, and Bosniak), Bosniak leaders reluctantly entertained the possibility of a Bosniak-majority entity, though with strong reservations.

The proposed Bosniak republic would have included central and northwestern Bosnia, encompassing key cities such as Sarajevo, Zenica, Tuzla, and Bihać. However, these areas were non-contiguous and vulnerable to military pressure, making the viability of such a state highly uncertain. The concept was further weakened by the Bosniak leadership’s desire to maintain Bosnia and Herzegovina as a unified, multiethnic state.

The proposal gained some traction among Bosniak intellectuals and nationalists who believed that an exclusively Bosniak entity might be necessary to preserve the community’s security and cultural identity. However, the notion was never officially endorsed as a long-term objective by the government of President **Alija Izetbegović**, who prioritized international recognition of Bosnia and Herzegovina’s territorial integrity.

---

### Dayton Accords and Aftermath

The **Dayton Peace Agreement** of 1995, which ended the Bosnian War, rejected the partition of Bosnia and Herzegovina into independent ethnic states. Instead, it created a complex political structure composed of two main entities: the **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina** (jointly administered by Bosniaks and Croats) and the **Republika Srpska** (with a Serb majority). Although the Federation includes a majority of Bosniak citizens and is often informally referred to as the Bosniak-Croat entity, it is not exclusively Bosniak in its legal or institutional structure.

The Dayton framework preserved the sovereignty of Bosnia and Herzegovina but institutionalized ethnic power-sharing and decentralized governance. This compromise ended immediate hostilities but left unresolved tensions regarding identity, representation, and the long-term viability of a unified state.

---

### Contemporary Discourse

While the idea of a Bosniak republic has largely faded from mainstream political discourse, it occasionally resurfaces in response to nationalist rhetoric from Serb or Croat political leaders who advocate for greater autonomy or secession. Some Bosniak politicians and commentators warn that continued threats to Bosnia’s territorial integrity could lead to the reactivation of proposals for a Bosniak-majority republic as a defensive measure.

Despite these occasional assertions, the prevailing stance among major Bosniak political parties remains in support of a unified Bosnia and Herzegovina, albeit with reforms aimed at strengthening state institutions and ensuring equal representation for all citizens, regardless of ethnicity.

---

### Conclusion

The Proposed Bosniak Republic was a political concept that emerged amid the fragmentation and ethnic violence of the Bosnian War. It reflected both strategic considerations during peace negotiations and broader concerns about ethnic survival and autonomy. While it never materialized as a concrete entity, the idea underscores the complexities of post-Yugoslav state-building and the enduring challenges of ethnic coexistence in Bosnia and Herzegovina’s fragile political landscape.
