**Death and State Funeral of Ronald Reagan**

Ronald Wilson Reagan, the 40th President of the United States (1981–1989), died on June 5, 2004, at the age of 93. His passing and the subsequent state funeral reflected both the protocols for honoring former U.S. heads of state and the enduring impact of his presidency on American and international politics.

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

Reagan died at his Bel Air residence in Los Angeles at 3:30 p.m. Pacific Daylight Time on June 5, 2004, following complications from pneumonia and Alzheimer’s disease, which he had publicly acknowledged in 1994. His wife, Nancy Reagan, and members of his family were at his side. Upon confirmation of his death, President George W. Bush ordered flags at all federal buildings flown at half-staff until the day after the interment. The Department of Homeland Security issued a national order of mourning, and the White House issued a statement honoring Reagan’s leadership during the late Cold War.

## National and International Reactions

News of Reagan’s death prompted tributes from political leaders worldwide. President Bush lauded Reagan as a “bold leader” whose policies contributed to “victory in the Cold War.” Former presidents George H. W. Bush, Bill Clinton, and Jimmy Carter issued formal remembrances, highlighting Reagan’s optimism, communication skills, and economic reforms. International leaders—including British Prime Minister Tony Blair, German Chancellor Gerhard Schröder, and Russian President Vladimir Putin—paid tribute to Reagan’s role in ending the Soviet–American standoff, citing his advocacy for freedom in Eastern Europe and his symbolic call to “tear down this wall.” Public memorials and vigils were held at U.S. embassies and consulates in dozens of countries.

## Lying in State in the U.S. Capitol

Reagan’s body was transported by Air Force One to Andrews Air Force Base and then by motorcade to the Capitol on June 9, 2004. For two days, his flag-draped casket lay in state in the Capitol Rotunda, allowing public and official delegations to pay respects. A joint service honor guard of the U.S. Army’s 3rd Infantry Regiment (The Old Guard) and sentries from all five military branches stood watch. Congressional leaders, Supreme Court justices, and members of Reagan’s administration filed past in a formal procession. Thousands of citizens queued outside, reflecting the broad public interest in honoring Reagan’s legacy.

## State Funeral Service at Washington National Cathedral

On the morning of June 11, 2004, a state funeral service was held at Washington National Cathedral. The ceremony combined religious liturgy with military and civic honors:

* **Procession and Arrival**: Reagan’s casket was borne on a caisson drawn by six black-and-white horses of the Caisson Platoon, followed by a joint service honor guard and a military band performing a funeral march.
* **Officiants and Music**: The Episcopal Bishop of Washington and the cathedral’s dean presided over the service. Musical selections included Handel’s *Largo* and hymn arrangements performed by the cathedral choir and a military brass ensemble.
* **Eulogies**: Eulogies were delivered by President George W. Bush, former British Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher (via video), and Reagan’s longtime friend and advisor, former Secretary of State George P. Shultz. Their remarks emphasized Reagan’s role in revitalizing American optimism, securing peace through strength, and advancing democratic values worldwide.

Dignitaries in attendance included all living former presidents, members of Congress, the Supreme Court, Cabinet officials, foreign heads of state, and representatives of the 50 U.S. governors.

## Final Committal at the Reagan Presidential Library

Following the cathedral service, Reagan’s remains were flown to Burbank, California, and then transported by motorcade to the Ronald Reagan Presidential Library in Simi Valley. A private interment service took place on a terrace overlooking the Santa Susana Mountains, where Reagan and his wife were laid to rest side by side. Family members and close friends participated in a brief committal ceremony that included the playing of “Taps,” a 21-gun salute, and the folding of the U.S. flag, which was presented to First Lady Nancy Reagan.

## Historical and Political Context

Reagan’s death and state funeral were conducted under “Operation Serenade,” the presidential funeral protocol established by the military to coordinate national honors. As the first former president to lie in state in the Capitol Rotunda since Lyndon B. Johnson in 1973, Reagan’s funeral underscored the enduring national reverence for the office of the presidency and the importance of ceremonial tradition. Moreover, the broad international participation and public engagement highlighted Reagan’s global stature as a pivotal figure in the final decades of the Cold War.

Reagan’s presidency had been characterized by a shift toward conservative economic policies, a robust anti-communist foreign posture, and a communication style that earned him the moniker “Great Communicator.” His funeral ceremonies revisited these themes, framing his leadership as instrumental in restoring American confidence and shaping the post–Cold War world order.

## Legacy of the Funeral

The death and state funeral of Ronald Reagan reaffirmed the United States’ capacity to unite in commemoration across partisan lines. While debates over Reagan’s domestic policies and their long-term effects persisted, the state funeral reflected a moment of national solidarity. The ceremonies—combining military precision, religious solemnity, and public participation—served both to honor Reagan’s individual achievements and to underscore the symbolic continuity of American democratic institutions. In subsequent years, the images and recordings of the funeral became reference points for discussions of presidential legacies and the rituals through which the nation honors its leaders.
