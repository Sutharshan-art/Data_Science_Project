**Iraq War**

The Iraq War was a protracted armed conflict that began with the 2003 invasion of Iraq by a United States–led coalition and continued through 2011, transforming into an insurgency and sectarian civil conflict before major combat operations formally concluded. Rooted in debates over weapons of mass destruction (WMD), regime change, and regional security, the war reshaped Iraq’s political order and had lasting implications for Middle East stability and international intervention doctrine.

### Background and Causes

In the aftermath of the 1991 Gulf War, United Nations resolutions imposed weapons inspections and no-fly zones on Iraq to prevent the resurgence of Saddam Hussein’s regime and its suspected WMD programs. Throughout the 1990s, Iraq remained subject to stringent sanctions, and periodic confrontations occurred over compliance with UN inspectors. In U.S. political discourse, the terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, intensified focus on preventing future threats. The Bush administration argued that Iraq’s alleged possession of chemical, biological, and potentially nuclear weapons—and its purported links to terrorist organizations—posed an imminent danger. Although subsequent intelligence judgments proved flawed, these claims formed the core casus belli for invasion.

### Invasion and Rapid Regime Collapse (March–May 2003)

On 20 March 2003, U.S., British, Australian, and Polish forces launched “Operation Iraqi Freedom,” employing a “shock and awe” aerial bombardment of Baghdad followed by ground offensives. Armored and mechanized units advanced along multiple axes: the main thrust from Kuwait into southern Iraq, secondary columns from Turkey into the north, and amphibious landings along the Persian Gulf coast. Within weeks, coalition forces overwhelmed Iraqi Republican Guard divisions; Baghdad fell on 9 April, and President Saddam Hussein’s regime collapsed. Hussein himself went into hiding, eventually captured in December 2003.

### Occupation and Rise of Insurgency (2003–2006)

The Coalition Provisional Authority (CPA), under U.S. administrator Paul Bremer, undertook rapid political and economic reforms: disbanding the Iraqi army, de-Ba‘thification of state institutions, and the introduction of a transitional governing council. These decisions, intended to dismantle authoritarian structures, inadvertently generated widespread unemployment among former soldiers and bureaucrats, fueling hostility. By mid-2003, armed resistance began coalescing into a Sunni-dominated insurgency, targeting coalition troops and Shia communities. Foreign jihadists also joined, further escalating violence. Attacks on civilian markets, mosques, and infrastructure deepened insecurity and sectarian mistrust.

### Political Transition and Sectarian Conflict (2005–2007)

Iraq held its first multi-party elections in January 2005, leading to a Shia-Kurdish-dominated government and adoption of a new constitution in October. While formal sovereignty transferred to Iraqi authorities in June 2004, real power remained contested. Shia militias, some aligned with political parties, vied for influence against Sunni insurgent groups and Kurdish regional forces. The bombing of the Golden Mosque in Samarra in February 2006 triggered a surge of retaliatory killings, plunging Iraq into near-civil war. Coalition security operations expanded, but the lack of cohesive national institutions hampered stabilization.

### The “Surge” and Counterinsurgency (2007–2008)

In January 2007, the U.S. deployed an additional 20,000 troops under a new counterinsurgency strategy emphasizing population protection, local engagement, and support for fledgling Iraqi security forces. Known as the “surge,” this approach paired kinetic operations against insurgent strongholds with efforts to empower local Sunni tribal militias—most notably the Anbar Awakening—to combat al-Qaeda in Iraq. By late 2008, violence had declined significantly: roadside bombings, civilian deaths, and coalition casualties reached multi-year lows. Iraqi forces began assuming primary security responsibilities in major cities.

### Withdrawal of U.S. Forces and Aftermath (2009–2011)

Under a 2008 U.S.–Iraq Status of Forces Agreement, American combat troops withdrew from Iraqi cities by mid-2009 and completed their departure on 18 December 2011. Prime Minister Nouri al-Maliki’s government, led by the Islamic Dawa Party, faced the task of national reconciliation amid continuing sectarian tension, corruption, and the challenge of reintegrating former insurgents. Though large-scale combat had ended, isolated attacks persisted. The war’s human toll included an estimated 200,000 to 250,000 Iraqi civilian deaths, thousands of coalition military casualties, and millions displaced internally and as refugees.

### Political and Regional Impact

The Iraq War’s legacy is multifaceted. Politically, it dismantled one-man Ba‘thist rule and introduced competitive elections, though Iraq’s democracy has remained fragile, shadowed by sectarian patronage networks. The conflict altered regional power balances: Iran emerged with greater influence over Shia militias and Iraqi politics, while neighboring states like Turkey and Saudi Arabia navigated complex security and refugee dynamics. Internationally, the war prompted intense debate over preemptive intervention, post-war planning, and the reliability of intelligence assessments.

### Conclusion

The Iraq War was a seminal event of the early 21st century, illustrating both the potential and pitfalls of externally orchestrated regime change. Its years of combat, occupation, and political transformation left enduring marks on Iraqi society, U.S. foreign policy, and the broader Middle East. Ongoing efforts to address unresolved grievances, strengthen institutions, and manage regional rivalries continue to shape Iraq’s post-war trajectory.
