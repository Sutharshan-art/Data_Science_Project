**Peace Plans Proposed Before and During the Bosnian War**

A series of **peace plans were proposed before and during the Bosnian War (1992–1995)** in an effort to prevent or resolve the conflict in the multiethnic republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina. These plans were initiated by international actors and mediators, and they sought to address the complex political, territorial, and ethnic disputes among Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims), Croats, and Serbs following the breakup of Yugoslavia. Although several agreements were drafted and negotiated, most were ultimately rejected or failed to halt the violence, with only the **Dayton Accords** in 1995 achieving a lasting ceasefire and political settlement.

---

### Background

The disintegration of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia in the early 1990s led to rising nationalist tensions among its constituent republics. Bosnia and Herzegovina, with a mixed population of Bosniaks, Croats, and Serbs, declared independence in March 1992 following a referendum. The move was rejected by the Bosnian Serb leadership, which, backed by the government of Serbia, sought to establish Serbian control over parts of Bosnia. Soon after, armed conflict broke out, marked by ethnic cleansing, sieges, and atrocities.

In response to the escalating crisis, various international actors, including the European Community (later European Union), the United Nations, and the United States, facilitated peace initiatives to end the war and prevent further regional instability.

---

### The Carrington–Cutileiro Plan (1992)

One of the earliest attempts to prevent conflict in Bosnia and Herzegovina was the **Carrington–Cutileiro Plan**, also known as the **Lisbon Agreement**. Proposed in February–March 1992 by EC negotiators **Lord Peter Carrington** and **José Cutileiro**, the plan envisioned Bosnia as a decentralized state divided into three ethnic-based cantons, granting autonomy to Bosniaks, Croats, and Serbs.

Although the plan was initially accepted in principle by all three ethnic leaders—**Alija Izetbegović** (Bosniaks), **Radovan Karadžić** (Serbs), and **Mate Boban** (Croats)—Izetbegović later withdrew his agreement under international pressure and with hopes of international recognition of a unitary state. The failure of the plan coincided with the onset of hostilities in April 1992.

---

### Vance–Owen Peace Plan (1993)

The **Vance–Owen Peace Plan (VOPP)** was introduced in early 1993 by UN special envoy **Cyrus Vance** and EC representative **Lord David Owen**. The plan aimed to divide Bosnia into ten ethnically defined provinces, each with significant autonomy under a weak central government. The provinces were designed to reflect the ethnic composition of the population while avoiding large-scale displacements.

Although the Bosniak and Croat leaderships showed conditional support for the plan, Bosnian Serb leaders rejected it. A key point of contention was that the VOPP required Serb forces to surrender large areas captured during the war, which they were unwilling to do. Despite brief international support, including from U.S. and European diplomats, the plan collapsed in May 1993 after it failed to be ratified by the Bosnian Serb assembly.

---

### Owen–Stoltenberg Plan (1993)

Following the failure of the VOPP, the **Owen–Stoltenberg Plan**, also known as the **Peace Plan for Bosnia and Herzegovina**, was proposed later in 1993. It was introduced by David Owen and Thorvald Stoltenberg, the new UN mediator, and proposed the division of Bosnia into three ethnic republics—Serb, Croat, and Bosniak—under a loose confederation.

The plan effectively accepted the principle of partition, which was strongly opposed by the Bosniak leadership. Although it was endorsed by Croat and Serb negotiators, the plan failed due to Bosniak resistance, particularly because it would legitimize gains made through ethnic cleansing and weaken central authority.

---

### Washington Agreement (1994)

The **Washington Agreement** was signed in March 1994 between Bosniak and Bosnian Croat leaders under the mediation of the United States. It ended the Croat–Bosniak conflict within the larger war and established the **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina**, a joint entity composed of Bosniak- and Croat-majority areas.

Although the agreement did not end the war with the Bosnian Serbs, it was a key diplomatic success that narrowed the conflict and aligned Bosniak and Croat forces under a unified command, paving the way for future peace efforts.

---

### Dayton Agreement (1995)

The culmination of international peace efforts came with the **Dayton Peace Accords**, negotiated in November 1995 at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base near Dayton, Ohio, under U.S. mediation led by **Richard Holbrooke**. The agreement formally ended the war and established Bosnia and Herzegovina as a single sovereign state composed of two entities: the **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina** and the **Republika Srpska**.

The Dayton Accords recognized existing military and territorial realities and instituted a power-sharing political system based on ethnic representation. While it brought peace, the agreement entrenched ethnic divisions in the political structure, leading to ongoing governance challenges.

---

### Conclusion

The peace plans proposed before and during the Bosnian War reflected the international community’s efforts to balance competing nationalist ambitions with the goal of preserving a multiethnic state. While early plans failed due to political resistance and battlefield dynamics, they laid the groundwork for eventual compromise. The Dayton Agreement, though imperfect, marked the end of the conflict and established the framework for post-war governance, but the legacy of these peace plans continues to shape Bosnia and Herzegovina's complex political landscape.
