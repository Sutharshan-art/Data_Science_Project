**1936 United States Presidential Election in Connecticut**

The **1936 United States presidential election in Connecticut** was held on **November 3, 1936**, as part of the national election to select the president and vice president of the United States. Voters in Connecticut chose **eight electors** to represent them in the **Electoral College**, which officially selected the president and vice president.

The election in Connecticut was a key component of President **Franklin D. Roosevelt’s** nationwide landslide victory. Running for re-election as the Democratic candidate, Roosevelt defeated Republican nominee **Alf Landon**, the Governor of Kansas. The results in Connecticut reflected the broader national political realignment of the 1930s, as the Democratic Party, traditionally weak in New England, made significant inroads due to Roosevelt’s New Deal policies amid the Great Depression.

---

### Historical and Political Background

Connecticut, historically a Republican stronghold, had favored the GOP in nearly every presidential election since the Civil War, with few exceptions. The state was emblematic of New England’s generally conservative, business-oriented electorate. However, the political landscape began to shift during the 1930s in response to the **economic devastation of the Great Depression**, which eroded public confidence in Republican leadership.

Franklin D. Roosevelt’s **New Deal coalition** brought together an unprecedented alliance of urban workers, farmers, ethnic minorities, and the poor—groups that had previously been marginal to national Democratic politics. In industrial states like Connecticut, the economic hardships of the early 1930s and Roosevelt’s activist government approach resonated strongly with working-class voters, especially in urban centers such as Hartford, Bridgeport, and New Haven.

The 1936 election thus represented a critical test of whether Roosevelt's popularity could further break through long-standing Republican dominance in traditionally conservative regions like Connecticut.

---

### Candidates

* **Franklin D. Roosevelt** (Democratic Party), President of the United States, from New York

  * Running mate: **John Nance Garner**, Vice President of the United States, from Texas

* **Alf Landon** (Republican Party), Governor of Kansas

  * Running mate: **Frank Knox**, newspaper publisher and former U.S. Army officer, from Illinois

Roosevelt campaigned on the success of the New Deal and the promise of continued government intervention to combat unemployment, poverty, and economic inequality. Landon, though personally moderate, represented a Republican Party that criticized New Deal programs as unconstitutional, fiscally irresponsible, and overly bureaucratic.

---

### Election Results

| Candidate             | Party      | Popular Vote | Percentage | Electoral Votes |
| --------------------- | ---------- | ------------ | ---------- | --------------- |
| Franklin D. Roosevelt | Democratic | 408,611      | 55.32%     | 8               |
| Alf Landon            | Republican | 320,518      | 43.37%     | 0               |

Franklin Roosevelt carried **Connecticut by a margin of approximately 12 percentage points**, receiving all eight of the state’s electoral votes. This marked the **first time since 1912** that a Democratic presidential candidate had won the state, and only the second time since the Civil War.

The victory was particularly notable given Connecticut’s prior strong support for Republican candidates such as Calvin Coolidge and Herbert Hoover. Roosevelt’s success signaled the **waning of Republican hegemony in the Northeast**, at least temporarily, and illustrated the appeal of New Deal policies to urban and industrial constituencies.

---

### County and Demographic Trends

Roosevelt carried a majority of counties in Connecticut, particularly those with large urban populations and high concentrations of industrial workers and immigrants. His strongest support came from:

* **New Haven County**, home to factories and a significant immigrant working class
* **Hartford County**, a center of manufacturing and insurance
* **Fairfield County**, particularly in urban centers like Bridgeport and Stamford

Conversely, Landon performed better in **rural and suburban areas**, especially in the northern and eastern parts of the state, though he was unable to overcome Roosevelt's urban margins.

The 1936 election also marked the beginning of a **Democratic shift in urban politics** in Connecticut, which would become a lasting trend throughout the mid-20th century.

---

### Political Significance

Roosevelt’s victory in Connecticut was part of a **national landslide**, where he won 46 of 48 states and captured over 60% of the national popular vote. The results confirmed the political durability of the New Deal coalition and signaled a profound **realignment in American party politics**.

In Connecticut, the 1936 results illustrated the electorate’s growing openness to Democratic leadership amid economic crisis. While the state would swing back to the Republicans in subsequent elections, Roosevelt's breakthrough laid the foundation for increased Democratic competitiveness in the region.

Nationally, the election deepened divisions within the Republican Party, which struggled to redefine itself in opposition to the New Deal. In contrast, the Democratic Party emerged from the election as the dominant force in American politics for the next several decades, wielding significant influence over domestic policy and the structure of the federal government.

---

### Aftermath

Although Roosevelt’s policies remained controversial among conservatives and business elites, his electoral mandate in 1936—including in traditionally Republican states like Connecticut—allowed him to press forward with additional New Deal initiatives. However, resistance from Congress and the Supreme Court limited some of these efforts, and political challenges to Roosevelt’s authority would emerge after 1937.

In Connecticut, the election foreshadowed a gradual **realignment of party loyalties**, particularly as urban populations grew and industrial labor movements gained strength. The state’s political evolution over the next half-century would reflect broader national trends in urbanization, labor politics, and social reform.
