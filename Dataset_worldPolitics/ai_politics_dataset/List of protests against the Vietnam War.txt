**List of Protests Against the Vietnam War**

The **list of protests against the Vietnam War** chronicles the major demonstrations, marches, and acts of civil disobedience that occurred throughout the United States and around the world in opposition to American military involvement in Vietnam. Spanning from the early 1960s through the war’s end in 1975, the anti-war movement became one of the most significant mass mobilizations in modern American history, influencing public opinion, political discourse, and policy decisions.

The Vietnam War protests emerged gradually as U.S. engagement escalated, peaking in the late 1960s and early 1970s. A wide range of individuals and groups participated in the movement, including students, clergy, civil rights activists, academics, veterans, and artists. Their motivations varied—from opposition to the draft and moral objections to American interventionism, to critiques of Cold War policy and solidarity with the Vietnamese people.

### Historical Context

The Vietnam War, a conflict between the communist government of North Vietnam and the U.S.-backed Republic of South Vietnam, became a major theater of Cold War confrontation. U.S. involvement steadily increased after 1964, following the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, which granted President Lyndon B. Johnson broad authority to escalate military operations. By the late 1960s, more than 500,000 American troops were deployed in Vietnam.

As the war dragged on and American casualties mounted, public confidence in the government’s strategy eroded. The anti-war movement gained strength through media coverage, political activism, and widespread concern over the draft and civilian casualties in Southeast Asia.

### Major Protests and Demonstrations

#### **March on Washington for Peace in Vietnam (April 17, 1965)**

Organized by the Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), this early protest drew around 20,000 people to the capital. It was one of the first large-scale demonstrations against the war, emphasizing the growing student-led opposition to U.S. foreign policy.

#### **International Days of Protest (October 15–16, 1965)**

Coordinated by the National Coordinating Committee to End the War in Vietnam, these protests occurred in dozens of U.S. cities and on college campuses, as well as in several foreign countries. Demonstrators condemned the escalation of the war and U.S. bombing campaigns in North Vietnam.

#### **Burning of Draft Cards (1965–1968)**

Draft resistance became a prominent form of protest, particularly among students and conscientious objectors. Burning draft cards in public was both a symbolic act of defiance and, after a 1965 federal law criminalizing the act, a potential legal risk. High-profile burnings occurred in cities such as New York, San Francisco, and Boston.

#### **The Pentagon March (October 21, 1967)**

One of the most iconic anti-war protests, this demonstration drew over 100,000 participants to Washington, D.C., culminating in a march on the Pentagon. Organized by the National Mobilization Committee to End the War in Vietnam, the event featured civil disobedience and arrests, and was famously chronicled by journalist Norman Mailer.

#### **Columbia University Protests (April 1968)**

Students at Columbia University in New York City protested the university's ties to military research and its support for the war effort. The protests escalated into a campus occupation and police crackdown, signaling the intensifying role of student activism in the anti-war movement.

#### **Moratorium to End the War in Vietnam (October 15 and November 15, 1969)**

These nationwide demonstrations were among the largest in U.S. history. On October 15, millions participated in local actions, including teach-ins and vigils. The November 15 march in Washington, D.C., attracted over half a million demonstrators and was widely covered by the media.

#### **Kent State Shootings and Nationwide Student Strikes (May 1970)**

Following President Richard Nixon’s announcement of the U.S. invasion of Cambodia, campuses erupted in protest. At Kent State University in Ohio, National Guard troops shot and killed four students during a demonstration. The incident led to widespread outrage and a national student strike involving over 4 million students at more than 450 campuses.

#### **Veterans Against the War (1971–1972)**

Vietnam Veterans Against the War (VVAW) organized several powerful protests, including the 1971 "Winter Soldier Investigation" in Detroit, which documented alleged war crimes, and the "Dewey Canyon III" protest in Washington, where veterans threw their medals onto the Capitol steps.

#### **May Day Protests (May 1–3, 1971)**

These mass civil disobedience actions in Washington, D.C., aimed to disrupt government functions to protest the war. Over 12,000 people were arrested, one of the largest mass arrests in U.S. history. The crackdown reflected government concern over the scale of anti-war mobilization.

### Global Protests

Anti-war demonstrations were not limited to the United States. Protests occurred in countries including the United Kingdom, France, Australia, Japan, and West Germany. In many cases, they combined criticism of U.S. foreign policy with broader concerns about militarism and imperialism.

### Political and Social Impact

The Vietnam War protest movement significantly influenced public opinion and helped shape the political climate of the era. It pressured political leaders, contributed to the erosion of support for the war, and was a key factor in President Johnson’s decision not to seek re-election in 1968. By the early 1970s, the anti-war consensus extended into the mainstream, and Congress began taking steps to limit executive authority in foreign interventions, including the War Powers Act of 1973.

The protests also left a lasting legacy on American civil society, contributing to broader movements for civil rights, women’s rights, and environmental reform.

### Conclusion

The list of protests against the Vietnam War reflects a critical era in U.S. history when civic activism, dissent, and public engagement challenged official narratives and helped alter the course of national policy. The movement demonstrated the power of organized protest and contributed to a rethinking of the role of the United States in global affairs.
