**2007 Ontario General Election**

The **2007 Ontario general election** was held on **October 10, 2007**, to elect members to the **Legislative Assembly of Ontario**, the provincial legislature of **Canada’s most populous province**. It was the 39th general election in Ontario’s history and resulted in the re-election of the governing **Ontario Liberal Party**, led by **Premier Dalton McGuinty**, to a second consecutive majority government.

Held concurrently with a **provincial referendum on electoral reform**, the election occurred during a period of relative economic stability and political continuity, though it was marked by public debates over education funding, healthcare, tax policy, and democratic reform. Despite widespread criticism of broken promises from McGuinty’s first term, the Liberals managed to secure a majority by capitalizing on a fragmented opposition and their perceived competence in managing provincial affairs.

---

### Historical and Political Context

Dalton McGuinty’s **Liberal Party** had won a commanding majority in the **2003 provincial election**, ending eight years of **Progressive Conservative (PC)** rule under **Mike Harris** and **Ernie Eves**. Upon taking office, McGuinty pledged to improve public services, particularly in **education** and **healthcare**, while maintaining fiscal discipline.

However, early in his first term, McGuinty’s government faced criticism for introducing a new **health premium** and failing to fulfill a pledge not to raise taxes. Despite this, the Liberals oversaw a relatively strong provincial economy, introduced major education reforms (including smaller class sizes and more school funding), and increased healthcare spending. These efforts helped rebuild confidence in public services following significant cutbacks during the Harris era.

The opposition was divided and struggled to gain traction. The **Progressive Conservatives**, led by **John Tory**, attempted to modernize their image and appeal to centrist voters. The **New Democratic Party (NDP)**, under **Howard Hampton**, maintained its traditional base but failed to significantly expand support. Both opposition parties targeted what they framed as Liberal mismanagement and broken promises.

---

### Electoral System Referendum

One of the most significant features of the 2007 election was the **Ontario electoral reform referendum**, held in parallel with the general election. The referendum asked voters whether they supported replacing the existing **first-past-the-post (FPTP)** electoral system with a **mixed-member proportional (MMP)** system, as recommended by the **Ontario Citizens' Assembly on Electoral Reform**.

For the proposed change to pass, it required a **supermajority**: 60% of the popular vote and majority support in at least 64 of Ontario’s 107 electoral districts. The proposal was defeated, with only 36.9% voting in favor. Most voters remained either unaware of the details of the proposal or unconvinced that the new system would improve representation.

---

### Results

The **Ontario Liberal Party** won **71 out of 107 seats**, down from 72 in 2003, but still comfortably above the 54-seat threshold for a majority government. The **Progressive Conservative Party** won **26 seats**, a decrease from their previous 24, while the **New Democratic Party** gained slightly, winning **10 seats**, up from 7.

| Party                     | Leader          | Seats Won | Popular Vote | Percentage |
| ------------------------- | --------------- | --------- | ------------ | ---------- |
| Ontario Liberal Party     | Dalton McGuinty | 71        | 1,867,273    | 42.2%      |
| Progressive Conservatives | John Tory       | 26        | 1,398,327    | 31.6%      |
| New Democratic Party      | Howard Hampton  | 10        | 741,465      | 16.8%      |
| Green Party of Ontario    | Frank de Jong   | 0         | 354,897      | 8.0%       |

The **Green Party of Ontario**, despite not winning any seats, made significant gains in the popular vote, reflecting growing environmental awareness in provincial politics.

Notably, PC leader **John Tory** failed to win a seat in his own riding of **Don Valley West**, an unusual and politically damaging result. His campaign was hindered by controversy over his proposal to extend public funding to **faith-based schools**, a policy that proved deeply unpopular with voters and was widely credited with undermining the Conservative campaign.

---

### Key Issues

The campaign focused on a range of domestic policy issues:

* **Education**: The Liberal government emphasized its record of reducing class sizes, improving test scores, and investing in schools, while the PCs’ proposal for faith-based school funding drew sharp criticism.
* **Healthcare**: Wait times and hospital funding remained important voter concerns. The Liberals touted new investments, while the opposition highlighted ongoing system inefficiencies.
* **Taxation and Fiscal Policy**: The Liberals were attacked for breaking their "no new taxes" pledge from 2003 but defended their spending priorities as necessary investments in public services.
* **Electoral Reform**: The referendum sparked limited public engagement. Many voters found the MMP proposal confusing or unnecessary, and few political leaders actively campaigned for or against it.

---

### Aftermath and Legacy

The 2007 election solidified **Dalton McGuinty**’s position as one of the most electorally successful Ontario premiers of the postwar period. His ability to secure consecutive majorities underscored his appeal among centrist and suburban voters, despite lingering questions about political accountability and broken promises.

For the **Progressive Conservatives**, the election was a disappointment. The defeat of their leader in his own riding and the divisiveness of the faith-based education issue prompted internal debate and eventually led to **John Tory’s resignation** as party leader in 2009.

The **referendum defeat** marked the end of the most serious attempt to reform Ontario’s electoral system in decades. While advocates continued to push for proportional representation, the outcome suggested that electoral change remained a low priority for most voters.

Overall, the **2007 Ontario general election** reflected a political landscape marked by cautious continuity, with voters opting to maintain the status quo while rejecting more radical changes to governance and electoral design.
