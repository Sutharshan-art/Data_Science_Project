**John Major** (born 29 March 1943) is a British politician who served as the 53rd Prime Minister of the United Kingdom from November 1990 to May 1997. A member of the Conservative Party, Major’s premiership oversaw the end of the Cold War’s early phase, recovery from economic recession, and significant constitutional and social reforms, while he navigated internal party divisions over Europe and public concerns about “sleaze” in government.

### Early Life and Political Rise

Born in Carshalton, Surrey, Major left school at 16 and worked as a banker and later a currency trader before entering politics. He was elected Member of Parliament for Huntingdonshire in 1979 (later Huntingdon) during Margaret Thatcher’s first government. Major quickly gained a reputation for diligence and commonsense: he served briefly as a junior minister in the Education and Science department (1983–1984) before being appointed Chairman of the Conservative Party (1985–1987), where he managed election campaigns during a period of strong Conservative majorities.

### Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster and Foreign Secretary

In January 1989, Major was appointed Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster with responsibility for No. 10 policy coordination—a role that brought him into the heart of Cabinet decision‐making. In July 1989, he became Foreign Secretary, managing British diplomatic missions during the collapse of communist regimes in Eastern Europe, German reunification, and the early stages of the Gulf crisis following Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait.

### Premiership (1990–1997)

#### Succession and “Back to Basics”

After the removal of Margaret Thatcher by her Cabinet colleagues in November 1990, Major was chosen as her successor, winning a leadership contest focused on unity and electoral appeal. As Prime Minister, he launched the “Back to Basics” campaign in 1993, invoking traditional values of community and personal responsibility, though the initiative was later overshadowed by several high‐profile scandals involving Conservative MPs.

#### Economic Challenges and Recovery

Major inherited a deep economic recession: unemployment had risen above 2.5 million, and Britain had been forced to withdraw from the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) on “Black Wednesday” (16 September 1992). Despite the political fallout, Major’s government benefited from a recovery in growth, falling inflation, and declining unemployment through the mid‐1990s, driven in part by lower interest rates and a strengthening global economy.

#### Europe and Maastricht

A central issue of Major’s tenure was Britain’s relationship with the European Community, later the European Union. He negotiated the Maastricht Treaty (1992), which created the EU and established the path to a single currency. To secure ratification, Major obtained protocols exempting the UK from the single currency and from elements of the Social Chapter. However, the Treaty caused deep rifts within his party, fueling Eurosceptic rebellions that undermined Conservative unity and parliamentary stability.

#### Social and Constitutional Reform

Major’s government enacted significant reforms, including the National Lottery Act (1993), which created a new source of funding for charities and the arts, and the Disability Discrimination Act (1995), extending civil‐rights protections to disabled people in employment and access to services. He also oversaw devolution legislation for Scotland and Wales, although referendums in 1997 occurred under his successor’s administration.

### 1997 Election and Later Career

Facing persistent public fatigue with Conservative government—exacerbated by economic dislocation and political scandal—Major called a general election in May 1997. The Conservatives suffered a landslide defeat to Tony Blair’s Labour Party, ending 18 years of Tory rule. Major continued as party leader and Leader of the Opposition until June 1997, when he retired as an MP and was ennobled as Baron Major of Hull in 2005.

### Political Context and Legacy

John Major’s premiership bridged the ideological shift from Thatcherism toward a more centrist “Compassionate Conservatism.” His leadership navigated the challenges of post‐Cold War foreign policy, European integration, and economic globalization, while striving to maintain traditionalist values at home. Major’s emphasis on pragmatic governance, social reform, and multilateral diplomacy has been recognized as stabilizing after a decade of ideological polarization. His memoirs and subsequent writings reflect on the complexities of leadership amid rapid geopolitical change and the demands of party management in a fractious parliamentary system.
