**Fikret Abdić** (born 29 September 1939) is a Bosnian businessman and politician known for his leadership of the Agrokomerc food conglomerate, his role in Bosnian politics during the breakup of Yugoslavia, and his later conviction for war crimes. As a prominent figure in the early 1990s, Abdić briefly governed the self-proclaimed Autonomous Province of Western Bosnia before his defeat and arrest. After serving a prison sentence in Croatia, he returned to political life in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

### Early Life and Business Career

Born in the village of Donji Vakuf in what was then the Kingdom of Yugoslavia, Abdić studied at the University of Zagreb’s Faculty of Economics, graduating in 1964. Returning to Bosnia, he joined Agrokomerc, a state-owned agricultural and food-processing enterprise based in Velika Kladuša. Under his management from 1971 onward, Agrokomerc expanded rapidly, earning Abdić a reputation as an innovative manager. By the mid-1980s, the company employed over 13,000 people, exported its products across Europe, and forged credit agreements with Western banks. The so-called “Agrokomerc Affair” of 1987—arising from undisclosed loans that Agrokomerc had obtained—sparked a nationwide financial scandal, but Abdić emerged largely unscathed and retained local popularity.

### Entry into Politics and 1990 Elections

With the introduction of multi-party politics in Yugoslavia, Abdić founded the **People’s Party of Bosnia and Herzegovina** in 1990, positioning himself as an alternative to the re-emerging nationalist parties. In Bosnia and Herzegovina’s first multiparty elections in November 1990, he secured 43 percent of the vote for the Bosnian presidency, defeating candidates from both the Party of Democratic Action (SDA) and the Croatian Democratic Union (HDZ BiH) in his home region. Although he placed third overall—after Alija Izetbegović (SDA) and Živko Radišić (SDS)—his strong showing demonstrated significant local support for his economic-development platform.

### Role in the Bosnian War and Autonomous Province of Western Bosnia

Following Bosnia and Herzegovina’s independence referendum in March 1992 and the outbreak of war that April, Abdić fell out with President Izetbegović. Citing perceived neglect of the Una-Sana Canton’s economic needs, he declared the **Autonomous Province of Western Bosnia** (APZB) on 27 September 1993. Backed by the Army of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina (ARBiH) commander Sefer Halilović and tacitly supported by Serb forces in Republika Srpska, the APZB controlled an enclave of approximately 160,000 people around Velika Kladuša. Abdić’s administration maintained order and continued Agrokomerc operations, but its alliance with Bosnian Serb and Croatian forces rendered it a de facto collaborator in a fragmentation of Bosnian territory.

The APZB engaged in armed clashes with ARBiH units under the central government’s command. In August 1994, ARBiH forces overran the enclave, ending Abdić’s de facto rule. Thousands of his supporters fled into Republika Srpska territory. Abdić himself sought refuge in Croatia, where he remained until his arrest in 2002.

### War Crimes Charges and Conviction

In exile, Abdić faced indictments from both the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) and Croatian courts, accused of war crimes including persecution, torture, and forced displacement of Bosniak civilians loyal to Sarajevo’s government. In February 2002, Croatian authorities detained him; in November 2002, he was convicted by a Croatian court for war crimes against civilians in the APZB and sentenced to 20 years’ imprisonment. The conviction was upheld on appeal in 2004, though his sentence was later reduced to 15 years on procedural grounds. Abdić served his sentence at Lepoglava Prison and was released on conditional parole in April 2012 after nearly ten years.

### Return to Political Life

After his release, Abdić returned to Velika Kladuša, where he retained a base of local support. In 2016 he founded the **Democratic People’s Union** (DNZ), campaigning on themes of economic development, local autonomy, and reconciliation. Despite controversy over his wartime role, he was elected mayor of Velika Kladuša in the October 2016 municipal elections, defeating candidates from the SDA and other mainstream parties. His victory underscored enduring regional loyalty and the complex legacy of wartime allegiance.

As mayor, Abdić has prioritized revitalizing Agrokomerc’s facilities, attracting foreign investment, and improving local infrastructure. Critics within Bosnia’s central institutions and human-rights organizations continue to challenge his participation in public office, arguing that his wartime actions remain at odds with post-Dayton reconciliation efforts. Nevertheless, his electoral success illustrates the resilience of local identity politics in the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina’s decentralized structure.

### Legacy and Significance

Fikret Abdić’s career embodies the intersection of economic entrepreneurship and nationalist politics during the disintegration of Yugoslavia. His rise from corporate manager to influential political figure in Bosnia’s western periphery highlights regional disparities within the wartime Bosnian state. Abdić’s establishment of a separate polity and subsequent conviction for war crimes mark him as one of the most controversial actors of the Bosnian War. His later political resurgence demonstrates the enduring potency of local leadership and collective memory in shaping Bosnia and Herzegovina’s post-conflict political landscape.
