**Flag of Bosnia and Herzegovina**

The flag of Bosnia and Herzegovina is the national banner officially adopted on 4 February 1998, following the end of the Bosnian War and the implementation of the Dayton Peace Agreement. It consists of a medium-blue field charged with a yellow right-angled triangle abutting the hoist, whose hypotenuse is edged by a row of nine full five-pointed white stars and two half-stars at the top and bottom. The design symbolizes the country’s three major constituent peoples—Bosniaks, Croats, and Serbs—as well as its aspiration toward stability, unity, and a European identity.

### Historical Context

From 1945 until 1992, Bosnia and Herzegovina was one of the six republics of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia and used the red, blue, and white Yugoslav tricolor defaced with a red star. Upon declaring independence in March 1992, the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina adopted a green flag bearing a central golden lilies motif, harking back to the medieval Kotromanić dynasty. During the subsequent war (1992–1995), the Bosnian Serb and Bosnian Croat authorities in their respective territories adopted separate flags: the Serbian tricolor for Republika Srpska and the Croatian chequerboard for the Croatian Republic of Herzeg-Bosnia. These conflicting banners underscored the fracturing of state symbols during the conflict.

Under the 1995 Dayton Peace Agreement, Bosnia and Herzegovina was reconstituted as a single sovereign state composed of two entities—the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina and Republika Srpska—and the Brčko District. However, the persistence of ethnically aligned flags at the national level hindered post-war reconciliation. In 1998, the Office of the High Representative commissioned a new neutral national flag to replace the wartime variant and reflect the multiethnic nature of the country.

### Design and Symbolism

The flag’s dominant blue background evokes the colors of the European Union, signaling Bosnia and Herzegovina’s commitment to Euro-Atlantic integration. The yellow triangle represents the geographical shape of the country and its three constituent peoples—Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims), Croats, and Serbs. The line of white stars along the triangle’s hypotenuse is arranged to suggest infinite continuity, with the half-stars at each end giving the impression of a longer sequence extending beyond the edges, symbolizing the country’s open embrace of diversity and its place in the broader European family.

The color scheme—blue, yellow, and white—was chosen for its neutrality, avoiding traditional pan-Slavic (red, white, blue) or pan-Islamic (green) palettes. This deliberate avoidance of overt ethnic or religious symbolism reflects the constitutional mandate for a shared state identity equalizing the constituent nations and “Others,” as recognized in the Dayton framework.

### Adoption and Legal Status

On 4 February 1998, the Parliamentary Assembly of Bosnia and Herzegovina formally adopted the new flag, along with a national coat of arms and anthem, under amendments imposed by the High Representative. The law stipulates that the flag be flown over all state institutions, diplomatic missions, and public buildings, and used at international events. Entities and cantons are entitled to display their own banners alongside the national flag but may not replace it at the state level.

The Constitutional Court of Bosnia and Herzegovina has affirmed the flag’s status as the sole national symbol, ruling against attempts by entity governments to adopt alternative national flags. Its legislation mandates respect for the flag in schools, government ceremonies, and public commemorations, reinforcing its role in fostering a unified civic identity.

### Political Controversies and Reception

Despite its intended neutrality, the flag’s adoption was met with mixed reactions. Some Bosnian Serb and Croat politicians criticized the removal of more familiar ethnic symbols, viewing the new design as diminishing their communities’ historical narratives. Occasional protests have occurred in Republika Srpska over perceived overreach by central institutions. Conversely, many citizens and international observers welcomed the flag as a long-overdue symbol of reconciliation and a visual break from the divisive iconography of the war years.

In public opinion surveys, younger generations born after the war tend to embrace the 1998 flag more readily than older citizens with stronger attachments to prewar symbols. Sports teams and cultural groups have generally adopted the flag without controversy, using it in international competitions and festivals to present a cohesive national image.

### Usage and Display

The flag of Bosnia and Herzegovina is displayed prominently at the Presidency building in Sarajevo, the Parliamentary Assembly, and other federal institutions. It is raised at border crossings, airports, and consulates abroad, where it serves as the country’s primary diplomatic emblem. During national holidays—such as Statehood Day (25 November) and Independence Day (1 March)—official ceremonies feature the flag alongside the national anthem.

Municipalities and cantons may fly their own flags in conjunction with the national flag, but not in place of it. Private citizens, businesses, and non-governmental organizations also employ the national flag in celebrations, sporting events, and cultural gatherings, contributing to its normalization as a symbol of shared sovereignty.

### Legacy

More than two decades after its adoption, the flag of Bosnia and Herzegovina continues to function as a key element of state-building in a complex multiethnic polity. It embodies the constitutional principle of equality among the constituent peoples and “Others,” and represents the country’s aspirations toward European integration and political stability. While debates over identity and symbolism persist, the flag remains the nation’s official banner, signifying both its troubled history and its hopes for a united future.
