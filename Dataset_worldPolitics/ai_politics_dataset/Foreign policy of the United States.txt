**Foreign Policy of the United States**

The foreign policy of the United States encompasses the strategies, decisions, and actions by which the federal government engages with other nations to advance national interests, security, economic prosperity, and values such as democracy and human rights. Rooted in the country’s founding principles and evolving in response to global developments, U.S. foreign policy has shifted from early isolationism to global leadership, shaping world affairs from the 19th century to the present.

### Early Foundations and 19th-Century Doctrines

In its first century, the United States largely adhered to a policy of non-entanglement in European affairs, as articulated by President George Washington’s 1796 Farewell Address. The **Monroe Doctrine** (1823) proclaimed that Western Hemisphere powers should no longer be subject to European colonization or intervention, establishing U.S. hegemony in the Americas. Throughout the 19th century, American foreign policy combined the pursuit of continental expansion (“Manifest Destiny”) with cautious trade and diplomatic relations abroad, while avoiding formal alliances.

### Turn to Global Engagement (1898–1945)

The Spanish–American War (1898) marked the United States’ emergence as an overseas power, acquiring territories such as Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines. During World War I, President Woodrow Wilson’s initial neutrality shifted to intervention in 1917 under the banner of “making the world safe for democracy.” After the war, the Senate’s rejection of the League of Nations reflected a return to isolationist sentiment in the interwar years. However, the attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941 ended isolationism definitively, leading the United States into World War II and, subsequently, to a permanent global leadership role through institutions such as the United Nations, the International Monetary Fund, and the World Bank.

### Cold War Containment (1947–1991)

In the aftermath of World War II, U.S. foreign policy was dominated by the **Cold War** competition with the Soviet Union. The Truman Doctrine (1947) committed the United States to supporting “free peoples” against communist insurgencies or expansion. The Marshall Plan (1948) rebuilt Western Europe economically, while NATO (1949) provided collective security. The doctrine of **containment**, articulated by George F. Kennan, guided U.S. involvement in the Korean War (1950–1953), the Vietnam War (1955–1975), and numerous covert and diplomatic efforts worldwide. Strategic arms control agreements (SALT, START) and periods of détente under Presidents Nixon and Carter sought to manage superpower rivalry without direct conflict.

### Post–Cold War Transition (1991–2001)

With the Soviet Union’s collapse in 1991, the United States became the sole superpower. Under President George H. W. Bush, U.S. forces led a broad coalition in the Gulf War (1990–1991) to expel Iraqi forces from Kuwait under U.N. auspices. The 1990s saw an emphasis on **liberal interventionism**, as the Clinton administration engaged in the Balkans to halt ethnic cleansing (Bosnia and Kosovo), promoted NATO enlargement into Eastern Europe, and advanced free-trade agreements such as NAFTA and the creation of the World Trade Organization. Peacekeeping and nation-building in Somalia, Haiti, and elsewhere tested the limits of humanitarian intervention.

### War on Terror and Unilateralism (2001–2009)

The September 11, 2001, terrorist attacks prompted President George W. Bush to launch the **Global War on Terror**, beginning with the invasion of Afghanistan (2001) to dismantle al-Qaeda and remove the Taliban from power. In 2003, a U.S.-led “coalition of the willing” invaded Iraq, toppling Saddam Hussein but igniting a protracted insurgency. The Bush administration emphasized preemptive self-defense, unilateral action when necessary, and a muscular projection of U.S. military power—strategies that provoked debate over international law, multilateralism, and American legitimacy.

### Renewal of Multilateralism (2009–2016)

President Barack Obama sought to recalibrate U.S. foreign policy with the slogan **“leading from behind,”** emphasizing alliances and multilateral institutions. He negotiated the New START treaty with Russia (2010), pursued a “pivot to Asia” to address China’s rise, and secured multilateral agreements such as the Paris Climate Accord (2015) and the Iran nuclear deal (Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action, 2015). While reducing troop levels in Iraq and Afghanistan, the administration also relied on special operations and drone strikes against terrorist networks in Pakistan, Yemen, and Somalia.

### Populism and Strategic Competition (2016–Present)

Under President Donald Trump, U.S. foreign policy shifted toward **“America First,”** prioritizing bilateral over multilateral engagements. The administration withdrew from the Trans-Pacific Partnership, the Paris Agreement, and the Iran deal, and renegotiated NAFTA as the USMCA. It also pursued rapprochement with North Korea, imposing maximum pressure through sanctions. Under President Joe Biden, the United States has rejoined the Paris Agreement, reaffirmed alliances (NATO, G7), and confronted rising great-power competition with China and Russia through a blend of deterrence, diplomacy, and economic measures such as the CHIPS and Science Act.

### Continuities and Debates

Across administrations, key debates persist over America’s role in the world: the balance between **hegemony and restraint**, the use of military force versus diplomacy, and the integration of democracy and human rights promotion with national interest. Economic globalization, technological change, and transnational challenges—climate change, pandemics, cyber threats—continue to shape U.S. foreign policy in the 21st century, as policymakers grapple with preserving security, projecting values, and maintaining leadership in an increasingly multipolar world.
