**Nuclear Warfare**

**Nuclear warfare** is armed conflict in which nuclear weapons are used, involving explosive devices whose destructive power derives from nuclear fission, fusion, or a combination of both. Since the first atomic bombs were detonated over Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945, the prospect of nuclear war has shaped international politics, military strategy, and arms‐control efforts.

## Origins and Early Use

The United States employed two fission bombs against Japan in August 1945—“Little Boy” over Hiroshima (6 August) and “Fat Man” over Nagasaki (9 August)—killing an estimated 200,000 people, largely civilians. The bombings precipitated Japan’s surrender on 15 August, ending World War II, but also inaugurated the nuclear age.

The Soviet Union detonated its first atomic device in 1949, ending the U.S. nuclear monopoly and triggering the **nuclear arms race**. In 1952, the U.S. tested the first hydrogen (“thermonuclear”) bomb; the USSR followed in 1953. The potential for mass destruction and radioactive fallout quickly became central to Cold War deterrence theory and superpower rivalry.

## Cold War Doctrines and Crises

### Mutual Assured Destruction (MAD)

By the late 1950s, both superpowers possessed enough warheads to destroy each other many times over. **Mutual Assured Destruction** emerged as a strategic doctrine: because any nuclear strike would invite a devastating retaliatory attack, neither side would risk initiating conflict. MAD underpinned U.S. and Soviet nuclear postures and informed arms‐control negotiations.

### Deterrence and Flexible Response

Under NATO’s **Flexible Response** strategy (mid-1960s), the West maintained a spectrum of nuclear and conventional options to deter Warsaw Pact aggression. The U.S. deployed tactical nuclear weapons to Europe to offset conventional inferiority, prompting domestic protests (e.g., the 1982 Nuclear Freeze movement).

### Crises

* **Berlin Crisis (1961):** Showcased nuclear brinkmanship over Germany.
* **Cuban Missile Crisis (1962):** Closest the world came to nuclear war; resolved through Soviet removal of missiles from Cuba in exchange for U.S. non‐invasion guarantees and secret removal of Jupiter missiles from Turkey.
* **Vietnam and Proxy Wars:** Nuclear arsenals deterred superpower direct engagement but fueled regional conflicts and covert interventions.

## Arms Control and Non‐Proliferation

To manage the nuclear threat, states negotiated treaties:

* **Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963):** Banned atmospheric and underwater tests.
* **Non‐Proliferation Treaty (NPT, 1968):** Committed nuclear‐weapon states to pursue disarmament and non‐nuclear states to forego weapons, while guaranteeing peaceful nuclear cooperation.
* **Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I 1972; SALT II 1979):** Capped strategic delivery systems.
* **Intermediate‐Range Nuclear Forces (INF, 1987):** Eliminated an entire class of missiles.
* **Strategic Arms Reduction Treaties (START I 1991; New START 2010):** Mandated deep cuts, verification measures, and transparency.

Despite these, the Cold War ended with over 60,000 warheads globally, many on high alert.

## Humanitarian and Environmental Impact

Nuclear detonations produce blast, heat, and ionizing radiation, causing immediate and long‐term casualties. The concept of **nuclear winter**—postulated in the 1980s—suggested that widespread nuclear exchanges could inject soot into the stratosphere, lowering temperatures and collapse of agriculture, threatening billions with famine. These catastrophic scenarios have fueled disarmament advocacy, including the 2017 **Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons**.

## Post–Cold War Developments

After 1991, U.S. and Russian arsenals declined under START and unilateral reductions. Newly independent states inherited weapons, leading to denuclearization agreements (Ukraine, Kazakhstan, Belarus). However, other states—India (1974), Pakistan (1998), North Korea (2006–)—acquired arsenals, complicating non‐proliferation.

Emerging concerns include:

* **Tactical Nuclear Weapons:** Lower‐yield “battlefield” nukes raise escalation risks.
* **Missile Defense:** U.S. and NATO defenses provoke accusations of undermining MAD.
* **Hypersonic Weapons:** Glide vehicles evade existing interceptors, destabilizing deterrence.
* **Proliferation Risks:** Iran’s nuclear program and illicit trafficking of fissile material sustain international tensions.

## Contemporary Policy and Outlook

Major nuclear‐armed states—United States, Russia, China, France, United Kingdom, India, Pakistan, Israel (undeclared), North Korea—maintain doctrines emphasizing deterrence, with varied emphasis on **no‐first‐use** commitments (China, India) or rejection thereof (U.S., Russia). The NPT Review Conferences (every five years) struggle to balance disarmament, non‐proliferation, and civil‐nuclear rights.

Civil society and some governments advocate **global zero**—complete nuclear abolition—via incremental steps (fissile material cutoff, de-alerting, minimum deterrence) and universalization of prohibition norms. Critics question feasibility given security dilemmas, technological diffusion, and verification challenges.

## Legacy

Nuclear warfare doctrine and armament have shaped international relations for decades, influencing alliance structures (NATO, Warsaw Pact), crisis management, and defense budgets. The enduring specter of nuclear conflict continues to drive arms‐control efforts and strategic dialogues, as well as creative responses in international law, risk reduction, and public advocacy aimed at preventing the unthinkable use of these weapons.
