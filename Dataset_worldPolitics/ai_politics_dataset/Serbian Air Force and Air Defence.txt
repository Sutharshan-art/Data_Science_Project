**Serbian Air Force and Air Defence**

**Overview**
The Serbian Air Force and Air Defence (Serbian: Ратно ваздухопловство и противваздушна одбрана, *Ratno vazduhoplovstvo i protivvazdušna odbrana*; RV i PVO) is the aerial warfare service branch of the Serbian Armed Forces. Responsible for the defense of Serbian airspace, close air support, reconnaissance, and airlift, it traces its origins to the Royal Yugoslav Air Force (1918–1941) and the post-war Yugoslav Air Force (1945–1992). Reconstituted after the breakup of Yugoslavia, the RV i PVO has modernized amid budget constraints and shifting strategic partnerships, maintaining a mix of legacy Soviet and Western equipment.

**Historical Context**

* **Royal Yugoslav Air Force (1918–1941):** Established after World War I, it developed indigenous design and training programs. During the Axis invasion of April 1941, it was overwhelmed by German air power.
* **Yugoslav Air Force (1945–1992):** Reformed under the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, it combined Soviet aircraft (e.g., Yakovlev fighters, Ilyushin bombers) with domestically produced jets like the Soko G-2 Galeb and G-4 Super Galeb. The force maintained non-aligned posture, hosting joint exercises with Eastern and Western blocs.
* **FR Yugoslavia (1992–2003) and Serbia and Montenegro (2003–2006):** Sanctions during the 1990s UN embargo limited training and maintenance. During the Kosovo War (1998–1999), RV i PVO dispersed assets and operated surface-to-air missiles against NATO aircraft; its headquarters and airbases suffered heavy bombing during Operation Allied Force.

**Reorganization and Independence (2006–Present)**
Following Montenegro’s independence in 2006, the Air Force and Air Defence merged former Yugoslav and Serbia-Montenegro assets under the newly formed RV i PVO. Early challenges included aging equipment, pilot shortages, and maintenance backlogs. From 2008 onward, Serbia pursued gradual modernization through domestic overhaul programs, limited foreign procurement, and multinational cooperation, notably with Russia and France.

**Structure and Organization**
The RV i PVO is organized into two main commands:

* **Air Force Command**, responsible for combat aviation, transport, and training.
* **Air Defence Command**, overseeing surface-to-air missile (SAM) brigades, radar stations, and integrated air-defence systems.

Key units include:

* **204th Fighter Aviation Regiment** (base at Batajnica Air Base) operating MiG-29s and L-159 Alca light combat aircraft.
* **241st Transport Squadron** with Mi-17 and An-26 aircraft.
* **Flight School** at Lađevci Air Base, operating G-4 Super Galeb and new basic trainers.
* **250th Air Defence Missile Brigade** based near Belgrade, equipped with S-125 Neva, S-75 Dvina, and modernized S-200 systems.

**Equipment and Capabilities**

* **Combat Aircraft**: The MiG-29 fulfills air-defence and interception roles; the Czech-built L-159 provides light attack and training capability, both procured in the 2010s.
* **Transport and Helicopters**: Mi-17V-5 helicopters and An-26 transports support troop movement, medevac, and disaster relief.
* **Air Defence**: A layered SAM network combines legacy Soviet systems (S-125, S-75) with upgraded S-200 launchers and mobile Osa-AKM batteries. Modernization plans include acquisition of the Russian S-400 system and Western radars under bilateral agreements.
* **Training and Support**: The domestic Soko aircraft industry in Utva provides maintenance and limited upgrades to domestic platforms.

**Political and Strategic Context**
Serbia’s air doctrine reflects its constitutional commitment to military neutrality, balancing relations with NATO, the European Union, and Russia.

* **NATO Cooperation**: Serbia participates in the Partnership for Peace program, contributing to joint exercises and Hosting Capacity Package initiatives, while maintaining interoperability with Western systems.
* **Russian Partnership**: Bilateral agreements (2008–present) include joint exercises (“Slavic Shield”), training exchanges, and negotiations for advanced air-defence acquisitions (S-400).
* **EU Integration**: Serbia’s EU candidate status since 2012 includes conditionalities for defense reform, transparency, and budgetary discipline, influencing procurement and interoperability priorities.

**Operational Deployments and Exercises**
The RV i PVO has participated in international exercises such as "Blue Phoenix" (with Italy), "Sokol-2008" (with Russia), and EU-led air policing support missions. It has provided humanitarian airlift during floods (2014) and wildfire responses in neighboring countries, demonstrating noncombat roles.

**Challenges and Modernization Efforts**
Budget constraints have limited large-scale acquisitions, leading to prioritization of incremental upgrades: avionics retrofits for MiG-29, phased introduction of L-159, and digitalization of command-and-control networks. Pilot training remains under pressure due to retirements and the shift to modern fast-jets. Efforts to refurbish air bases and radar infrastructure aim to enhance readiness and national coverage.

**Future Prospects**
The RV i PVO’s long-term plans include:

* **Acquiring multirole fighters**, with bids considered from Russia (MiG-35) and France (Rafale).
* **Expanding air-defence capabilities** through S-400 deployment and integration of mobile Western SAMs.
* **Developing unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)** reconnaissance assets under domestic and foreign collaboration.
* **Strengthening training pipelines** via partnerships with allied air forces and potential joint procurement of modern trainers.
