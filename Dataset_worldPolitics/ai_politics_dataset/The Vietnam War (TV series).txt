**The Vietnam War (TV Series)**

**Overview**
*The Vietnam War* is a ten‐part documentary series co‐directed by Ken Burns and Lynn Novick, first broadcast on PBS in the United States from September 17 to November 12, 2017, and on BBC Two in the United Kingdom beginning October 24, 2017. Spanning eighteen hours of screen time, the series provides a comprehensive examination of the conflict in Vietnam from its roots after World War II through the war’s aftermath and enduring legacies. Combining archival footage, photographs, and more than 80 interviews with participants on all sides, *The Vietnam War* has been acclaimed for its depth, balance, and cinematic scope.

**Historical and Political Context**
The Vietnam War (circa 1945–1975) was one of the defining conflicts of the Cold War era. Following the defeat of Japan in World War II, Vietnamese nationalists under Hồ Chí Minh declared independence, triggering a struggle against French colonial forces and, later, a division at the 17th Parallel into communist North Vietnam and anti-communist South Vietnam. U.S. involvement escalated after the 1964 Gulf of Tonkin incident, culminating in a major military commitment by the late 1960s. The war polarized public opinion at home—with anti-war protests, draft resistance, and political upheaval—and resulted in some 58,000 U.S. military deaths, millions of Vietnamese military and civilian casualties, and profound regional destabilization.

**Production**
PBS and Florentine Films announced the project in 2015 as Burns’s first major series since *The Civil War* (1990). Novick, a long‐time collaborator, co-directed and co‐produced. Researchers scoured over a half‐million archival materials, including recently declassified U.S. military films, North Vietnamese and Viet Cong footage, and personal photographs. The production team conducted over 160 interviews—80 of which appear in the series—with U.S. and Vietnamese combatants, civilians, journalists, policymakers (including Henry Kissinger and John McCain), and anti‐war activists. Filming took place over two years, with studios in Washington, D.C., and New York City utilizing custom‐built sets to replicate archival interview settings.

**Structure and Content**
The series is divided into ten chronological episodes:

1. **Déjà Vu (1945–1957):** Postwar decolonization struggles and the First Indochina War against France.
2. **Rising Storm (1958–1963):** Early U.S. advisory role and the Diệm regime’s repression in South Vietnam.
3. **The River Styx (1963–1965):** The Buddhist crisis, Kennedy’s assassination, and the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution.
4. **Resolve (1966–1967):** America’s deepening commitment, the Tet Offensive planning, and domestic dissent.
5. **This Is What We Do (1968):** The Tet Offensive, shifting U.S. strategy, and growing anti‐war movement.
6. **Things Fall Apart (1969–1970):** Vietnamization, Cambodian incursions, and polarized American politics.
7. **The Veneer of Civilization (1971–1973):** Peace negotiations in Paris and the scandal of Pentagon Papers.
8. **The History of the World (1973–1975):** U.S. withdrawal, the collapse of South Vietnam, and refugee crises.
9. **The Weight of Memory (1971–2001):** Veterans’ survivor guilt, PTSD, and assimilation challenges.
10. **Legacies (1973–2017):** Vietnamese reconstruction, American reconciliation efforts, and ongoing debates over war responsibility.

Each episode interweaves Vietnamese and American perspectives, presenting testimony from North and South Vietnamese soldiers and civilians alongside U.S. marines, helicopter pilots, and anti‐war protestors.

**Political and Cultural Impact**
The series rekindled national conversations about the Vietnam War’s contested legacies: the morality of U.S. intervention, the responsibilities of political leaders, and the human costs of war. Aided by extensive companion materials—including a best‐selling book,*The Vietnam War: An Intimate History* (2017)—and a curated music soundtrack featuring period songs, the project reached more than 20 million viewers in its U.S. broadcast run. Educational screenings at high schools and universities fostered debate in classrooms, while community forums organized by local PBS affiliates connected veterans and activists.

**Critical Reception**
*The Vietnam War* received widespread critical acclaim. It holds a 98% “Fresh” rating on Rotten Tomatoes and garnered Peabody and Emmy Awards for Outstanding Documentary or Nonfiction Series. Reviewers praised its ambition, narrative complexity, and unflinching use of graphic battlefield footage. Some critics, however, argued that the series occasionally privileged American viewpoints in its early episodes or gave undue weight to certain interviewees.

**Controversies and Criticism**
The series faced criticism from some Vietnam veterans’ groups and conservative commentators who argued it presented an overly critical view of U.S. policy and insufficiently credited American soldiers’ sacrifices. Conversely, certain Vietnam War scholars and anti‐war activists contended that the series did not fully explore imperialism’s broader contexts, such as French colonialism’s legacies and U.S. economic interests in Southeast Asia.

**Historical Significance**
By presenting both strategic and personal dimensions of the war, *The Vietnam War* stands as a landmark documentary that reshaped popular understanding of the conflict. Its balanced juxtaposition of opposing narratives has influenced subsequent media portrayals of war, set new standards for documentary research and production, and underscored the importance of preserving oral histories. In revisiting a deeply polarizing chapter of 20th‐century history, the series contributes to ongoing reflections on war, memory, and national identity.
