**United States Involvement in Regime Change**

**Overview**
The United States has engaged in efforts to influence, overthrow, or install foreign governments—commonly termed “regime change”—through a variety of means including covert operations, military intervention, economic pressure, and diplomatic isolation. Spanning the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, these efforts have been driven by strategic, ideological, economic, and humanitarian considerations. U.S. policy on regime change has evolved from overt military occupations to more nuanced forms of political, economic, and information warfare.

**Early Interventions (1890s–1945)**
In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, America’s emergence as a global power included interventions in Latin America and the Caribbean. Under the Monroe Doctrine and later the Roosevelt Corollary, the United States justified military occupations and political meddling in countries such as Cuba (1898–1902), Panama (1903), Nicaragua (1912–1933), Haiti (1915–1934), and the Dominican Republic (1916–1924). These interventions sought to protect American commercial interests, secure strategic waterways, and forestall European influence.

During World War II, while U.S. troops occupied Axis-aligned or liberated territories in Europe and Asia, formal regime change was typically conducted in cooperation with Allied powers rather than unilateral American action.

**Cold War Era (1945–1991)**
The global ideological struggle with the Soviet Union framed much of U.S. regime-change activity after 1945. The Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) played a central role in covert operations designed to remove or destabilize governments deemed too close to Moscow or perceived as obstacles to American strategic interests:

* **Iran (1953):** Operation Ajax engineered the removal of Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh after his nationalization of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, restoring the Shah’s power.
* **Guatemala (1954):** Operation PBSUCCESS deposed President Jacobo Árbenz amid fears that his land reforms threatened U.S. business interests and could inspire communist expansion.
* **Congo (1960–1965):** Covert support for the 1961 removal of Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba and backing for Joseph Mobutu’s rise, motivated by Cold War realpolitik and mineral access.
* **Dominican Republic (1961–1965):** U.S. encouragement of the assassination of Rafael Trujillo (1961) followed by military intervention to prevent perceived communist takeover (1965).
* **Chile (1970s):** Support for opposition groups against President Salvador Allende, including economic pressure and covert backing for the 1973 coup that brought General Augusto Pinochet to power.

Throughout Southeast Asia, U.S. military intervention in Vietnam (1965–1973) and support for anti-communist regimes in Laos and Cambodia constituted indirect attempts at regime change, even where no direct overthrow occurred.

**Post-Cold War and Humanitarian Interventions (1991–2001)**
With the collapse of the Soviet Union, explicit anti-communist rationale gave way to concerns over ethnic conflict, genocide, and regional instability. U.S. involvement in regime change during this period included:

* **Panama (1989):** Operation Just Cause deposed General Manuel Noriega, who faced international indictment on drug-trafficking charges and was seen as a threat to the Panama Canal and regional security.
* **Somalia (1992–1994):** Initially a humanitarian mission (UNOSOM II), U.S. forces sought to stabilize a failed state, leading indirectly to political reconfigurations though without a clear regime replacement.
* **Haiti (1994):** Intervention (Operation Uphold Democracy) restored democratically elected President Jean-Bertrand Aristide after his 1991 ouster by a military junta.

**“War on Terror” and the Twenty-First Century (2001–Present)**
After the September 11, 2001, attacks, the Bush administration pursued regime change under the banner of counterterrorism and democratization:

* **Afghanistan (2001):** Operation Enduring Freedom ousted the Taliban for harboring al-Qaeda, installing the Afghan Transitional Authority.
* **Iraq (2003):** Operation Iraqi Freedom removed President Saddam Hussein on grounds of alleged weapons of mass destruction and links to terrorism, leading to an extended occupation and eventual emergence of a new Iraqi government.

Beyond large-scale invasions, the U.S. has supported opposition movements and imposed sanctions in countries such as Libya (2011), Syria (post-2011), Venezuela (ongoing since 2014), and Iran (through economic and cyber operations).

**Methods and Instruments**
U.S. regime-change efforts have employed a spectrum of tools:

* **Covert Action:** CIA funding, training, propaganda, and support for opposition groups.
* **Economic Pressure:** Sanctions, asset freezes, and trade embargoes intended to weaken incumbent regimes.
* **Military Intervention:** Direct invasion, air campaigns, or special-operations raids.
* **Diplomatic Isolation:** United Nations resolutions and international pressure to delegitimize governments.
* **Cyber Operations:** Disruption of communications and election-influence campaigns.

**Criticism and Debate**
Regime-change policies have drawn scrutiny over legality, effectiveness, and unintended consequences. Critics argue that interventions often violate state sovereignty, undermine international law, and foster long-term instability. Proponents contend that removing oppressive or hostile regimes can prevent human rights abuses, reduce security threats, and foster democratization. The mixed outcomes in Iran, Guatemala, Iraq, and Libya fuel ongoing debate over the judicious use of American power.

**Legacy and Impact**
U.S. involvement in regime change has reshaped global politics, sometimes realigning strategic balances but frequently engendering anti-American sentiment and blowback—including insurgencies, refugee crises, and regional power vacuums. Contemporary policy increasingly emphasizes multilateral action, “smart power” approaches, and post-intervention stabilization efforts to address lessons learned from prior interventions.
