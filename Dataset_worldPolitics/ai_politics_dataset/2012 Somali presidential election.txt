**2012 Somali Presidential Election**

The **2012 Somali presidential election** was held on **September 10, 2012**, marking a pivotal moment in the country’s post-conflict political reconstruction. It was the first presidential election held in Somalia since the collapse of the central government in 1991 that involved a formal parliamentary vote under a transitional constitutional framework. The election followed the expiration of the **Transitional Federal Government (TFG)** and the establishment of a new **Federal Government of Somalia**, intended to lead the country out of decades of civil war, insurgency, and political fragmentation.

The election resulted in the surprise victory of **Hassan Sheikh Mohamud**, a university professor and civil society activist, who defeated incumbent **President Sharif Sheikh Ahmed** in a second-round runoff. The vote was widely praised by the international community for its relative transparency and peaceful conduct, despite occurring in a security environment still challenged by insurgency from the militant group **al-Shabaab**.

---

### Historical and Political Context

Somalia had lacked a functioning central government since the fall of **Siad Barre’s regime** in 1991, which triggered a prolonged civil war involving warlords, clan-based militias, and later Islamist insurgents. The **Transitional National Government (TNG)** was formed in 2000 but struggled to establish authority. It was succeeded by the **Transitional Federal Government (TFG)** in 2004, under a framework backed by the **African Union (AU)**, **United Nations (UN)**, and regional actors through the **Intergovernmental Authority on Development (IGAD)**.

The TFG, based in **Mogadishu**, remained weak and heavily dependent on international military support, particularly from the **African Union Mission in Somalia (AMISOM)**. Though the TFG made limited progress in state-building, it was frequently criticized for corruption, lack of legitimacy, and internal divisions.

In 2011, AMISOM and Somali forces drove **al-Shabaab** out of Mogadishu, creating conditions for a political transition. The **Djibouti Peace Process** and the **Kampala Accord** paved the way for the adoption of a **provisional constitution** in August 2012 and the formation of a new **Federal Parliament of Somalia**, which was tasked with electing the president.

---

### Electoral System

The 2012 election was indirect, conducted through a vote by members of the newly appointed **Federal Parliament**. The selection of MPs followed a **clan-based power-sharing formula** known as the **4.5 system**, which allocated parliamentary seats proportionally among Somalia’s four major clans and a coalition of minority groups.

A total of **275 Members of Parliament (MPs)**, chosen by clan elders and vetted by a **Technical Selection Committee** to exclude warlords and human rights violators, were sworn in ahead of the vote. Due to security concerns and limited institutional capacity, universal suffrage was not feasible.

Candidates for the presidency were required to meet criteria including Somali citizenship, a minimum age, and a nomination fee. Over 20 candidates registered, reflecting a mix of former government officials, academics, and diaspora figures.

---

### Candidates and Campaign

The election featured a diverse field, with key contenders including:

* **Sharif Sheikh Ahmed**: The incumbent president, a former Islamic Courts Union leader, who had led the TFG since 2009 and enjoyed support from some regional power brokers.

* **Hassan Sheikh Mohamud**: A civil society activist, education advocate, and co-founder of the **Peace and Development Party (PDP)**. He had a reputation for integrity and technocratic leadership.

* **Abdiweli Mohamed Ali**: The outgoing Prime Minister, an economist and diaspora returnee who played a key role in financial reforms and was backed by segments of the TFG.

* **Mohamed Abdullahi Mohamed**: Another former Prime Minister with strong diaspora support and an anti-corruption stance.

Despite the opaque nature of Somali politics, the campaign was considered relatively open by local standards. Allegations of vote-buying and clan-based lobbying persisted, but observers noted improvement compared to past processes.

---

### Election Results

In the **first round**, no candidate secured an absolute majority. The top two finishers were:

* **Hassan Sheikh Mohamud** – 60 votes
* **Sharif Sheikh Ahmed** – 64 votes

In the **second round**, Hassan Sheikh Mohamud secured a decisive victory with **190 votes** to Sharif Sheikh Ahmed’s **79**. The result was accepted peacefully by the incumbent, marking a rare example of a non-violent transfer of power in Somalia’s modern history.

---

### Domestic and International Reactions

The outcome was met with optimism domestically and abroad. Somalis widely celebrated the election, seeing it as a break from entrenched leadership and a move toward reform. Hassan Sheikh Mohamud’s background in civil society and education inspired hopes for inclusive governance and institutional development.

Internationally, the election was hailed by the **UN**, **African Union**, **European Union**, and key partners such as the **United States** and **United Kingdom**. UN Secretary-General **Ban Ki-moon** called the vote a “historic moment” for Somalia, and the U.S. formally recognized the Somali government in early 2013 for the first time since 1991.

However, challenges remained. Al-Shabaab denounced the new government and launched a suicide attack on a Mogadishu hotel two days after the election, targeting the president’s compound. The incident underscored the fragile security situation and the need for continued stabilization efforts.

---

### Legacy

The **2012 Somali presidential election** marked a turning point in Somalia’s long journey toward political normalization. While indirect and constrained by clan-based structures, it represented the first step in a broader roadmap toward representative governance under a new federal constitution.

Hassan Sheikh Mohamud’s election inaugurated a period of relative political stability and international engagement. It set the stage for further institutional development, reconciliation, and efforts to establish a more inclusive electoral process, including aspirations for universal suffrage in future elections.
