**Steel Curtain**

**Operation Steel Curtain** was a major military offensive conducted by the United States-led coalition forces and Iraqi government troops in November 2005 during the Iraq War. Its objective was to eliminate insurgent forces operating near the Syrian border, particularly in the western part of **Al Anbar Province**, and to reestablish Iraqi control over border towns that had become havens for foreign fighters and militants affiliated with **al-Qaeda in Iraq**.

The operation formed part of a broader counterinsurgency and stabilization strategy by coalition forces during the post-invasion occupation of Iraq. It highlighted the challenges of securing Iraq’s porous western frontier and reflected the shifting focus of U.S. military policy in Iraq from conventional combat operations to localized counterinsurgency and support for Iraqi security forces.

---

### Background

Following the 2003 U.S.-led invasion of Iraq and the toppling of **Saddam Hussein’s regime**, a violent insurgency emerged, comprising former regime elements, Sunni Arab nationalists, and foreign jihadists. Western Iraq, particularly **Al Qaim**, **Husaybah**, **Karabilah**, and **Ubaydi**, became critical entry points for foreign fighters crossing into Iraq from Syria.

These border towns were used as logistical and operational bases by militant groups, including al-Qaeda in Iraq (AQI), which sought to establish a presence in Sunni-dominated regions. AQI utilized the area to stage attacks against coalition forces and Iraqi civilians and to support the broader insurgency throughout the country.

In this context, U.S. commanders identified the need to clear and hold these towns and cut off the infiltration routes used by foreign fighters. Operation Steel Curtain was developed as part of this strategy and built upon earlier operations such as **Operation Iron Fist** and **Operation River Gate**, which had targeted nearby regions.

---

### Objectives and Planning

Operation Steel Curtain was launched on **November 5, 2005**, led by the **2nd Marine Division** of the **United States Marine Corps**, supported by elements of the **82nd Airborne Division**, the **1st Brigade of the Iraqi Army**, and local Iraqi police forces. The operation aimed to:

* Eliminate insurgent strongholds along the Syrian border
* Interdict the movement of foreign fighters into Iraq
* Support the expansion of Iraqi security force control over western Anbar
* Establish a secure environment ahead of the **December 2005 Iraqi parliamentary elections**

The name “Steel Curtain” symbolized the intent to block insurgent infiltration and establish an impenetrable security barrier along the border.

---

### Course of the Operation

The offensive began with intense fighting in **Husaybah**, where insurgents had entrenched themselves within residential and urban terrain. U.S. and Iraqi forces employed a strategy of sequential clearing, block by block, using combined arms tactics and door-to-door sweeps. The combat involved significant house-to-house urban warfare, one of the most challenging environments for modern military operations.

After securing Husaybah, coalition forces moved eastward to **Karabilah** and then to **Ubaydi**, encountering sporadic but fierce resistance from insurgents employing small arms, improvised explosive devices (IEDs), and suicide bombers.

The operation lasted until **November 22, 2005**, resulting in the reported death or capture of over **150 insurgents**, the discovery of weapons caches, and the dismantling of AQI safe houses. Approximately **10 coalition soldiers** were killed, and dozens were wounded. Civilian casualties were reported, though precise figures remain contested due to the fog of war and limited media access.

---

### Strategic and Political Significance

Operation Steel Curtain had both military and political implications. Strategically, it demonstrated the evolving nature of coalition operations, with greater emphasis placed on joint U.S.–Iraqi military actions and the development of Iraqi security forces capable of assuming operational responsibility.

Politically, the operation was timed to coincide with efforts to stabilize Iraq ahead of the **December 15, 2005, parliamentary elections**, seen as a critical step in the country’s democratic transition. By clearing insurgent elements from the border region, coalition forces aimed to create conditions conducive to voter participation and the extension of central government authority.

The operation was also intended to send a message to regional actors—particularly Syria—that Iraq’s borders would no longer serve as unmonitored entry points for foreign militants.

---

### Criticism and Aftermath

Despite its tactical success, Operation Steel Curtain drew criticism from some humanitarian and political observers, who argued that military offensives in civilian-populated areas caused displacement and infrastructure damage. Concerns were also raised about the sustainability of the gains, given the insurgency’s ability to regroup in rural and urban environments.

In the months following the operation, U.S. forces attempted to implement a “clear, hold, and build” strategy in the region. Iraqi forces established checkpoints and patrols, and reconstruction projects were initiated. However, the area remained unstable for years, reflecting the broader difficulties of counterinsurgency in Iraq.

---

### Legacy

Operation Steel Curtain became a key episode in the larger narrative of the Iraq War, reflecting the complexity of fighting a decentralized insurgency in a politically fragmented country. It underscored the challenges of border security, the need for strong local partnerships, and the limits of military power in achieving long-term political stability.

The operation also served as a case study in modern urban combat and coalition warfare, influencing U.S. military doctrine on joint operations and counterinsurgency tactics in subsequent conflicts.
