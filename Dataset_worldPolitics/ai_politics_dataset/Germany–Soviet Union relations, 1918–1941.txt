**Germany–Soviet Union Relations, 1918–1941**

Germany and the Soviet Union navigated a complex and often contradictory relationship from the aftermath of World War I to the eve of Operation Barbarossa. Their interactions were shaped by ideological enmity, pragmatic cooperation, and shifting strategic imperatives.

**Post–World War I and the Weimar Republic (1918–1922)**
Following Germany’s defeat in 1918 and the Bolshevik Revolution, both the Weimar Republic and the newly founded Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic were isolated diplomatically. In 1918, the Bolsheviks unilaterally annulled the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, but Allied powers continued to recognize this peace. Weimar Germany, burdened by Allied occupation and reparations, found a willing interlocutor in Soviet Russia.

Despite widespread domestic anti‐Bolshevik sentiment, the two pariah states negotiated the **Treaty of Rapallo** on 16 April 1922. Under Rapallo, Germany and Soviet Russia renounced all financial and territorial claims arising from World War I and reestablished diplomatic and economic relations. This agreement enabled German manufacturers to export machinery in exchange for Soviet raw materials and facilitated clandestine military collaboration, as the Reichswehr used Soviet territory to develop and test weapons prohibited by the Treaty of Versailles.

**Consolidation and Cooperation (1922–1933)**
Building on Rapallo, Germany and the Soviet Union signed the **Treaty of Berlin** on 24 April 1926. Mirroring Rapallo’s mutual-renunciation clauses, Berlin reaffirmed neutrality in the event either was attacked by a third party and extended the cooperation for five years. Economic ties deepened: German firms—such as Krupp and Siemens—undertook large infrastructure projects in the Soviet Union, including the construction of the Turkestan–Siberian Railway and industrial complexes in Magnitogorsk.

Simultaneously, secret military cooperation continued. German pilots trained in Soviet flight schools; armored units tested tanks in Kazakh steppes; and chemical weapons research progressed at Soviet facilities—all in violation of Versailles but justified by both sides as bolstering defense capabilities against potential aggressors.

**Nazi Rise to Power and Ideological Rupture (1933–1939)**
The Nazi seizure of power in January 1933 marked a sharp ideological break. The regime’s virulent anti‐Communism and racial doctrine labeled "Judeo‐Bolshevism" as Germany’s existential enemy. On 14 April 1933, Germany withdrew from the Treaty of Versailles clauses that had underpinned Rapallo-era cooperation, and on 18 April, it withdrew from the Geneva Disarmament Conference.**Diplomatic relations continued**, but mutual distrust intensified.

Economic links persisted briefly: the Soviet Union remained Germany’s main grain supplier, while German firms continued limited arms‐industry assistance. However, by 1935, Hitler’s rearmament and vision of Lebensraum in the East made deep cooperation politically untenable. The July 1939 Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact’s secret protocols—with planned partition of Poland and spheres of influence in the Baltic—would mark a sudden, tactical rapprochement after years of mutual hostility.

**Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and Prelude to Conflict (1939–1941)**
On 23 August 1939, Foreign Minister Joachim von Ribbentrop and Soviet Foreign Minister Vyacheslav Molotov signed the Non‐Aggression Pact. Publicly, both governments pledged neither to attack the other for ten years. Privately, secret protocols divided Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres: eastern Poland, the Baltic states, and parts of Romania were assigned to Soviet control; western Poland and Lithuania to Germany.

In the subsequent Polish campaign (September 1939), German and Soviet armies occupied their respective zones in coordinated fashion. Trade agreements followed, with the Soviets supplying oil, grain, and minerals critical to the German war economy, while Germany provided machinery and military technology. Economic exchanges sustained Germany’s munitions production during the early phases of World War II.

**Breakdown of Rapprochement and Operation Barbarossa**
Despite the pact, German–Soviet relations remained one of convenience rather than genuine alliance. Hitler viewed the East as the ultimate theater for his racial conquest and Lebensraum policy. On 22 June 1941, Germany launched **Operation Barbarossa**, the largest land invasion in history, violating the Non‐Aggression Pact and precipitating the Eastern Front’s six-year slaughter.

The invasion ended any remaining cooperation: diplomatic ties were severed, and wartime propaganda cast the other as the embodiment of evil—“Judeo‐Bolshevik” Germany’s mirror image of Soviet “Hitlerism.” The Eastern Front would account for the majority of military casualties in World War II, reshaping both nations irrevocably.

**Historical Significance**
Germany–Soviet Union relations from 1918 to 1941 illustrate the interplay between ideological antagonism and realpolitik. The Rapallo era reveals how mutual isolation can foster collaboration, even between political opposites. The Nazi era underscores the limits of ideological accommodation when one party embraces an expansionist, genocidal worldview. Finally, the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact epitomizes short‐term pragmatism giving way to catastrophic conflict.

These decades laid the groundwork for Cold War boundaries and animosities, as post-1945 Europe was shaped by wartime alliances and betrayals. Understanding this period illuminates how shifting political imperatives can drive temporary convergence, only to collapse when foundational ideological and strategic differences resurface.
