**List of Presidents of Russia**

The **President of Russia** is the head of state and the highest official of the Russian Federation. The office was established in 1991 following the dissolution of the Soviet Union, replacing the role previously held by the General Secretary of the Communist Party as the de facto leader of the Soviet state. The president serves as the commander-in-chief of the Armed Forces, oversees foreign policy, and plays a key role in shaping domestic legislation and governance.

This list includes individuals who have served as president since the position's creation in the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic (RSFSR) in 1991 and continued through the establishment of the Russian Federation as the successor state of the USSR. The presidency has evolved significantly over time, reflecting changes in Russia's political system, constitution, and balance of power.

### Historical Context

In June 1991, the RSFSR held its first direct presidential election, a landmark event amid the waning days of the Soviet Union. The post-Soviet Russian presidency was shaped by the 1993 Constitution, which created a strong presidential system with substantial executive authority. Since then, Russia has seen periods of political reform, economic upheaval, and increasing centralization of power.

Presidents are elected by popular vote for a term that was originally four years but was extended to six years following constitutional amendments in 2008. A two-consecutive-term limit was in place until a 2020 constitutional change effectively reset the term count for the sitting president.

### List of Presidents

#### **1. Boris Yeltsin (1991–1999)**

**Term:** July 10, 1991 – December 31, 1999
**Political Affiliation:** Independent (formerly Communist Party of the Soviet Union)
Boris Yeltsin was the first democratically elected president of Russia. His presidency began during the final stages of the Soviet Union and continued into the newly established Russian Federation. Yeltsin oversaw major economic reforms, including the transition to a market economy, the privatization of state assets, and liberalization policies collectively known as “shock therapy.” His tenure was also marked by political instability, a constitutional crisis in 1993, and the First Chechen War. Yeltsin resigned on December 31, 1999, appointing Vladimir Putin as acting president.

#### **2. Vladimir Putin (1999–2008; 2012–present)**

**First Term:** December 31, 1999 (acting); elected in 2000 and re-elected in 2004
**Second Term:** May 7, 2012 – present
**Political Affiliation:** United Russia (de facto), Independent
Vladimir Putin first became acting president following Yeltsin’s resignation and was elected president in 2000. His early years focused on restoring state control, stabilizing the economy, and reasserting Russia's global influence. During his presidency, Putin implemented legal reforms, centralized power in the executive, and benefited from a period of economic growth fueled by rising oil prices. After serving two consecutive terms, Putin was constitutionally barred from a third and supported Dmitry Medvedev as his successor. He returned to the presidency in 2012 after a four-year interval as prime minister. Constitutional changes in 2020 reset his term count, allowing him the possibility to remain in office until 2036.

#### **3. Dmitry Medvedev (2008–2012)**

**Term:** May 7, 2008 – May 7, 2012
**Political Affiliation:** United Russia
Medvedev succeeded Putin and served one term, during which he advocated for modernization, anti-corruption efforts, and judicial reform. Despite his more liberal rhetoric, he maintained close alignment with Putin, who served as his prime minister. Medvedev oversaw the 2008 war with Georgia and the global financial crisis, both of which shaped his foreign and domestic policies. He did not seek a second term and supported Putin’s return to the presidency in 2012, later serving as prime minister under Putin until 2020.

### Constitutional Framework

Under the 1993 Constitution, the president has broad powers, including the ability to issue decrees, veto legislation, dissolve the State Duma under certain conditions, and appoint the prime minister (with parliamentary consent). While Russia is nominally a semi-presidential republic, the political system has increasingly been characterized as a strong presidential regime.

Presidential terms were originally limited to four years. A 2008 constitutional amendment extended the term to six years, starting with the 2012 election. The 2020 constitutional amendments, approved via a national vote, allowed the reset of term limits for sitting president Vladimir Putin, effectively permitting him to run for two additional six-year terms.

### Political Landscape

The presidency has been central to Russia’s political system, with each occupant shaping the nation’s domestic structure and foreign posture. Yeltsin’s era marked a chaotic transition from communism, while Putin’s tenure has been associated with a resurgence of centralized power, nationalism, and assertive foreign policy.

Critics argue that the presidential system under Putin has led to democratic backsliding, suppression of dissent, and electoral manipulation. Supporters contend that the system has provided stability, restored state authority, and enhanced Russia’s international standing.

Elections for the presidency remain nominally competitive but have been criticized by international observers for a lack of transparency, media bias, and limited opposition access.

### Legacy and Influence

The list of Russian presidents highlights the transformation of Russia from a fledgling post-Soviet democracy to an increasingly centralized and authoritarian political system. The presidency remains the most powerful office in the country, and its occupants have left a lasting imprint on Russian society, governance, and international relations.

As of 2025, Vladimir Putin remains in office, with the next presidential election scheduled for 2030 under the amended constitutional framework. The future of the Russian presidency will likely continue to reflect both internal political dynamics and the country's role on the global stage.
