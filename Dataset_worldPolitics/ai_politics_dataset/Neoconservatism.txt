**Neoconservatism** is a political movement and foreign-policy philosophy that emerged in the United States during the late 1960s and gained prominence in subsequent decades. Characterized by an assertive promotion of democracy and American national interest abroad, neoconservatism blends a belief in the moral clarity of democratic values with a readiness to use military power to confront perceived threats. While often associated with the Republican Party, its intellectual lineage spans multiple political traditions.

## Origins and Intellectual Foundations

Neoconservatism originated among a cohort of former liberal intellectuals who grew disillusioned with the New Left, the counterculture of the 1960s, and President Lyndon B. Johnson’s Great Society programs. Many early figures—such as Irving Kristol, Norman Podhoretz, and Daniel Bell—had roots in socialist or social-democratic circles but came to view expansive welfare programs and anti-communist détente as misguided. They argued that liberalism’s emphasis on domestic social reform had undermined both America’s moral purpose and its willingness to confront totalitarian regimes.

Key intellectual influences on neoconservatism included political realist thinkers (e.g., Reinhold Niebuhr), who combined Christian ethics with recognition of humanity’s capacity for evil, and early Cold War strategists who emphasized containment of the Soviet Union. Unlike classical conservatives who distrusted large government and ideological crusades, neoconservatives retained a pragmatic faith in government as an agent of moral and political transformation.

## Political Development in the 1970s and 1980s

During the 1970s, neoconservatives organized around journals and think tanks—most notably *The Public Interest* and the American Enterprise Institute—to critique both liberal welfare statism and the perceived failures of Jimmy Carter’s foreign policy. They championed a robust defense budget, resisted arms-control agreements seen as favoring the Soviet Union, and advocated for support of anti-communist movements worldwide.

Under President Ronald Reagan (1981–1989), neoconservative ideas found receptive audience among policymakers. While not holding formal office en masse, neoconservative advisers influenced key initiatives: they supported Reagan’s Strategic Defense Initiative, endorsed aid to Afghan mujahideen against the Soviet invasion, and backed confrontations with leftist governments in Central America. Nonetheless, Reagan’s preference for Realpolitik and arms negotiations tempered full neoconservative ascendancy.

## Neoconservatism and the Post–Cold War Era

With the Soviet collapse in 1991, neoconservatives reoriented their focus to the perceived challenges of regional tyrannies, humanitarian crises, and the rise of radical Islamism. During the 1990s, they backed interventions in the Balkans to halt ethnic cleansing in Bosnia and Kosovo—arguing that unchecked atrocities threatened broader stability and America’s credibility. They also warned of the dangers posed by rogue states (e.g., Iraq, North Korea) potentially acquiring weapons of mass destruction.

The administration of President George W. Bush (2001–2009) marked the apogee of neoconservative influence. In the wake of the September 11 attacks, leading neoconservatives—such as Paul Wolfowitz, Elliott Abrams, and Richard Perle—occupied senior positions in the Departments of Defense and State and on the National Security Council. They advocated the “Bush Doctrine,” which combined preventive war against perceived threats with promotion of democratic regime change. The 2003 invasion of Iraq exemplified these principles: the administration argued that ousting Saddam Hussein would advance both U.S. security and the cause of democracy in the Middle East.

## Principles and Policy Prescriptions

Core tenets of neoconservatism include:

* **Moral Clarity**: Democracies possess inherent moral superiority over authoritarian regimes and therefore have a duty to spread democratic governance.
* **Primacy of Military Power**: The United States must maintain overwhelming conventional and strategic capabilities to deter adversaries and, if necessary, project force.
* **Unilateral Action**: While multilateral institutions have their place, the U.S. should not be constrained by international bodies when vital national interests or humanitarian imperatives are at stake.
* **Democratic Internationalism**: American security is tied to the political character of other states, making democracy promotion a strategic imperative.

## Criticism and Controversy

Neoconservatism has attracted sustained criticism from across the political spectrum.

* **Liberal Critics** argue that its unilateralism undermines international law and multilateral cooperation, and that its faith in military solutions overestimates American power and underestimates foreign cultures.
* **Realists** contend that neoconservative democracy promotion provokes anti-American backlash, overextends U.S. commitments, and ignores the limits of military force in nation-building.
* **Conservative Critics** fault its willingness to expand government power and to intervene abroad, viewing it as a departure from traditional conservative emphasis on limited government and national sovereignty.
* **Humanitarian Skeptics** point to the human costs and unintended consequences of interventions—particularly the prolonged instability in Iraq (post-2003) and challenges in post-conflict reconstruction.

## Legacy and Evolution

By the late 2000s, setbacks in Iraq and growing war fatigue diminished neoconservative dominance in U.S. foreign policy. The Obama administration (2009–2017) largely eschewed large-scale regime-change interventions in favor of multilateral diplomacy and limited regional engagements. Nonetheless, neoconservative networks continue to shape debates over U.S. posture toward China, Iran, and global terrorism.

In recent years, a “neo-neoconservative” resurgence has emerged among commentators advocating strong support for Ukraine against Russian aggression. While borrowing from classical neoconservatism’s emphasis on moral clarity and military deterrence, these voices operate in a transformed geopolitical environment, illustrating the enduring influence and adaptability of neoconservative thought.

## Conclusion

Neoconservatism has been one of the most influential schools of American foreign-policy thought over the past half-century. Its blend of idealism and realism, emphasis on American power, and willingness to use force in defense of democratic values have left a lasting imprint on U.S. strategy and global politics. Though its prominence has waxed and waned, the core debates it sparked—over the balance between military strength and diplomatic cooperation, moral imperatives and national interests—continue to shape policy discussions in Washington and beyond.
