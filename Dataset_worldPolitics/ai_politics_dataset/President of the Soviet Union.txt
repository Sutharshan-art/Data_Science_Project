**President of the Soviet Union**

The **President of the Soviet Union** was the head of state and the highest official of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) during the final years of its existence. The office was established on **March 15, 1990**, and was held only by **Mikhail Gorbachev** until the Soviet Union was officially dissolved on **December 25, 1991**. The presidency marked a significant shift in the Soviet political structure, concentrating executive authority in a single, directly empowered office that replaced the collective leadership model dominated by the Communist Party.

The creation of the presidency occurred during a period of sweeping political and economic reform known as **Perestroika**, initiated by Gorbachev in an effort to revitalize the Soviet system. The position was introduced as part of broader constitutional changes aimed at democratizing Soviet governance, but ultimately coincided with the unraveling of the Soviet state.

---

### Historical Background

Before the establishment of the presidency, the **General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU)** was the de facto leader of the country, while the **Chairman of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet** served as the formal head of state. This dual structure ensured that real power rested with the party, rather than with state institutions.

By the late 1980s, under mounting economic pressures and rising political dissent, Gorbachev sought to reform the system. As General Secretary since 1985, he promoted greater openness (**Glasnost**) and economic restructuring (**Perestroika**), leading to a gradual erosion of one-party dominance.

To facilitate further reform and project authority beyond the Communist Party apparatus, Gorbachev proposed the creation of an executive presidency, modeled in part on Western political systems.

---

### Establishment of the Office

The office of President of the Soviet Union was created through constitutional amendments passed by the **Congress of People's Deputies** in 1990. Unlike the ceremonial presidencies of other socialist states, this new office had substantial powers, including:

* Command of the armed forces;
* Authority to declare states of emergency or martial law;
* Oversight of foreign policy and internal security;
* The right to issue decrees with the force of law (subject to parliamentary oversight).

Gorbachev was elected by the Congress of People's Deputies rather than by popular vote, though future presidents were to be chosen through national elections. His assumption of the presidency on **March 15, 1990**, marked the formal end of the supremacy of the Communist Party over the executive branch.

---

### Powers and Responsibilities

As president, Gorbachev sought to consolidate control over an increasingly fragmented political system. The position was intended to serve as a stabilizing force during a time of political liberalization and growing nationalist movements within the republics.

The presidency included the appointment of a **Vice President**, a **Cabinet of Ministers**, and a **Presidential Council** to advise on policy. In July 1990, Gorbachev resigned from his position as General Secretary of the CPSU to separate the state presidency from the party leadership—an unprecedented move in Soviet politics.

Despite its sweeping powers on paper, the effectiveness of the presidency was limited by institutional resistance, a collapsing economy, and growing autonomy movements in the constituent republics.

---

### Crisis and Collapse

Gorbachev’s tenure as president was dominated by efforts to maintain the unity of the USSR amid rising centrifugal forces. His attempts to negotiate a new Union Treaty that would restructure the USSR into a looser federation were disrupted by growing demands for independence, particularly from the Baltic states, Ukraine, and Georgia.

In **August 1991**, a failed coup by hardline members of the CPSU and the KGB attempted to depose Gorbachev and reverse reforms. Although the coup collapsed after three days, it significantly weakened Gorbachev’s authority and accelerated the disintegration of Soviet institutions.

In the aftermath, several republics declared independence, and the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic, led by **Boris Yeltsin**, began assuming control over key functions of the Soviet government. Gorbachev, increasingly sidelined, announced his **resignation as president** on **December 25, 1991**, formally marking the end of the Soviet presidency and the dissolution of the USSR.

---

### Legacy

The presidency of the Soviet Union represented an attempt to modernize and democratize the governance of a superpower in crisis. While it was envisioned as a powerful institution capable of guiding the country through reform, its creation came too late to stem the centrifugal forces tearing the union apart.

Mikhail Gorbachev remains the only person to have held the title of President of the Soviet Union. His leadership style, international diplomacy, and reformist agenda earned him global recognition, including the **Nobel Peace Prize in 1990**, but his domestic legacy remains contentious, particularly among those who associate his presidency with the collapse of Soviet power and the hardships of the post-Soviet transition.

The brief existence of the Soviet presidency reflected the broader political turmoil of the late 1980s and early 1990s, serving both as a symbol of attempted reform and as a marker of the end of one of the 20th century's most powerful states.
