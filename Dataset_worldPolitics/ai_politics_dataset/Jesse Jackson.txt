**Jesse Louis Jackson Sr.** (born October 8, 1941) is an American civil rights leader, Baptist minister, and two‐time presidential candidate whose career has spanned the civil rights movement of the 1960s through the present. As a protégé of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., Jackson has played a significant role in American social and electoral politics, advocating for racial and economic justice, expanded voting rights, and greater African-American representation in government.

### Early Life and Education

Born in Greenville, South Carolina, Jackson was the son of Noah Louis Robinson, a WWII veteran, and Helen Burns, who worked as a domestic. Raised by his mother and stepfathers in poverty, Jackson attended Sterling High School in Greenville before enrolling at the University of Illinois on a football scholarship in 1960. He transferred to North Carolina A\&T State University, where he became active in the campus NAACP chapter. Jackson left college after the Greensboro sit-ins in February 1960, joining the civil rights protests that sought to desegregate lunch counters.

### Civil Rights Activism

After returning to North Carolina A\&T, Jackson graduated in 1964 and enrolled at the Chicago Theological Seminary, studying Marxist theory alongside his religious training. In 1966, he joined Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.’s Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC) as a field organizer focusing on voter registration in the South. He directed Operation Breadbasket—an initiative targeting discriminatory hiring in Chicago’s Black communities—becoming national director in 1967 and later leading the SCLC’s Chicago office. His success in mobilizing economic boycotts and negotiating with business leaders established his reputation as a pragmatic activist.

### Founding Operation PUSH and the Rainbow Coalition

In 1971, Jackson left the SCLC to found Operation PUSH (People United to Save Humanity), later renamed People United to Serve Humanity. PUSH sought to expand civil rights activism into the economic and political spheres by pressuring corporations to hire more minorities and supporting urban development. In 1984, Jackson co-founded the National Rainbow Coalition, a multiracial political organization that united African Americans, Latinos, labor unions, and progressive whites around shared social and economic justice goals. The coalition merged with the Liberty Lobby in 1996 to form People United for Peace and Justice, further broadening Jackson’s base.

### Presidential Campaigns

Jackson’s national political prominence rose with his presidential campaigns in 1984 and 1988 as a Democrat. In 1984, he won 3.5 million primary votes (18 percent) and carried the Michigan caucus. Jackson’s platform advocated for universal health care, a comprehensive jobs program to combat unemployment, and debt relief for developing nations—positions that presaged later Democratic priorities. In 1988, he improved on this performance, winning five primaries and caucuses with 7.3 million votes (29 percent). Although he did not secure the nomination—lost to Walter Mondale in 1984 and Michael Dukakis in 1988—Jackson’s campaigns helped diversify the Democratic coalition by engaging African-American, Hispanic, and youth voters, and pushing progressive economic policies into the mainstream.

### Political and Social Influence

Throughout the 1990s and 2000s, Jackson remained an influential Democratic Party figure and political broker. He served as a surrogate speaker at national conventions, hosted voter-registration drives, and campaigned for Democratic candidates. His close relationships with foreign leaders—most notably Fidel Castro of Cuba—garnered both praise for his diplomatic outreach and criticism over engagement with authoritarian regimes. Jackson’s repeated peace delegations to the Middle East and Africa, including negotiations for hostages in Syria and Uganda, underscored his role as an informal envoy on racial and human-rights issues.

### Controversies and Criticism

Jackson’s career has not been without controversy. His praise for Castro and rhetorical insistence on African-American reparations drew criticism from conservatives and some within the civil rights community. Financial mismanagement accusations plagued PUSH in the 1990s, leading to internal reforms. Additionally, his comments on political and social issues sometimes sparked backlash—for example, his 2007 remark suggesting that newly elected Senator Barack Obama’s skin color caused “crazy white folks” to oppose him. Jackson apologized and reaffirmed his commitment to multiracial politics.

### Legacy and Continuing Work

In the 2010s and 2020s, Jackson continued to advocate for voting rights, economic equity, and criminal-justice reform. Through the Rainbow/PUSH Coalition, he launched programs promoting minority business development and police-community relations. He supported movements such as Black Lives Matter, linking contemporary racial-justice struggles to the civil rights era. Jackson’s impact is reflected in the expanded political engagement of African Americans and other minority groups, the adoption of progressive economic policies by mainstream Democrats, and the institutionalization of diversity initiatives in politics and business.

### Honors and Recognition

Jackson has received numerous awards, including the NAACP’s Spingarn Medal (1980) and the Jefferson Award for Public Service (2003). Colleges and community centers bear his name, and documentaries and biographies have chronicled his life. While his methods and rhetoric have generated debate, Jesse Jackson remains a seminal figure in the evolution of modern American politics, illustrating how grassroots activism can translate into broader electoral and policy influence.
