**Reagan (2024 film)**

**Overview**
*Reagan* is a 2024 American biographical political drama directed by Sean McNamara, with a screenplay by Howard Klausner adapted from Paul Kengor’s 2006 book *The Crusader: Ronald Reagan and the Fall of Communism*. The film stars Dennis Quaid as President Ronald Reagan, Penelope Ann Miller as First Lady Nancy Reagan, and Jon Voight in the composite role of Viktor Petrovich, a fictional KGB agent. Produced on a budget of approximately \$25 million, the film was released theatrically in the United States on August 30, 2024, by ShowBiz Direct and went on to gross over \$30 million worldwide.

**Development and Production**
The idea for a modern, feature‐length portrayal of Ronald Reagan’s life emerged in 2010, when producer Mark Joseph secured adaptation rights after dissatisfaction with earlier television depictions. Klausner and co‐writer Jonas McCord crafted a script that emphasized Reagan’s journey from Hollywood actor and union leader to transformative Cold War president. Sean McNamara joined the project as director in 2016. Principal photography began in September 2020 in Guthrie, Oklahoma, but was suspended during the autumn surge of the COVID-19 pandemic before resuming later that year. Filming recreated key locations such as the White House Rose Garden, the steps of the Brandenburg Gate, and Depression-era Illinois farm country. Independent backers and conservative philanthropists ultimately financed the production after initial funding challenges.

**Narrative Structure**
The film employs a two‐pronged framing device: it opens in Moscow in 2001, where a Russian politician interviews Viktor Petrovich about his years monitoring Reagan; this contemporary perspective provides dramatic counterpoint and retrospective insight. Flashbacks then trace Reagan’s life chronologically: his childhood in Illinois, ascension in Hollywood as the president of the Screen Actors Guild during the blacklist era, the watershed “A Time for Choosing” speech in 1964, his governorship of California, and the 1980 presidential campaign. The core of the film focuses on Reagan’s presidency—highlighting key events such as the 1981 assassination attempt, the “evil empire” speech, the Strategic Defense Initiative, high-stakes summits with Mikhail Gorbachev, and the Iran-Contra controversy—culminating in his 1987 speech at the Berlin Wall and the Soviet Union’s dissolution.

**Historical and Political Context**
*Reagan* situates its biographical arc within the broader ideological contest of the late Cold War. Reagan is portrayed as a staunch anti-communist who believed that moral clarity, strong defense spending, and unwavering rhetoric would hasten the collapse of Soviet authoritarianism. The film dramatizes his alliance with Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher, the U.S. military buildup, and diplomatic negotiations in Geneva and Reykjavik, underscoring his conviction that American strength and optimism could lift the Iron Curtain. While it acknowledges domestic controversies, such as stagflation and the Iran-Contra affair, the narrative emphasizes Reagan’s role in reshaping global geopolitics and restoring American confidence.

**Cast and Characterization**
Dennis Quaid channels Reagan’s warm affability, trademark humor, and oratorical cadences, capturing both public magnetism and private doubts. Penelope Ann Miller’s portrayal of Nancy Reagan highlights her influence on White House social life, her role in crafting her husband’s image, and her support during periods of political and health crises. Jon Voight’s Viktor Petrovich, though entirely fictional, serves as the audience’s window into Soviet decision‐making and the impact of Reagan’s policies on the KGB’s strategic calculations. Supporting performances include Dan Lauria as House Speaker Tip O’Neill, Lesley-Anne Down as Margaret Thatcher, Robert Davi as Leonid Brezhnev, and Skip Schwink as President Gerald Ford, each anchoring the story in its wider international and bipartisan context.

**Reception**
Critical reviews were mixed to negative, with many mainstream reviewers faulting the film’s hagiographic tone and lack of deeper exploration of policy failures. Commentators noted that the film tended to gloss over complex scandals, portraying them more as footnotes than substantive challenges to Reagan’s legacy. However, conservative audiences and Reagan supporters praised its uplifting portrayal of American leadership and its affirmation of Cold War victory narratives. The film found a second life on streaming platforms and home-video markets, where it attracted viewership among political interest groups and prompted renewed discussions of Reagan’s impact on contemporary political discourse.

**Legacy and Impact**
*Reagan* joins a growing wave of presidential biopics that seek to reassess the Reagan era in light of twenty‐first century debates over executive power, partisanship, and national identity. Its commercial success among niche audiences underscores the enduring appeal of Reagan’s persona as an emblem of American optimism and conservative principles. While scholars and critics debate its historical precision, the film has spurred renewed interest in archival documentaries, scholarly works, and educational curricula on the late Cold War period. By foregrounding the interplay between rhetoric and policy, *Reagan* contributes to ongoing conversations about leadership, public memory, and the cinematic portrayal of political figures.
