**Draft Evasion in the Vietnam War**

**Draft evasion** during the Vietnam War (1955–1975) refers to the avoidance of compulsory military service by U.S. citizens subject to Selective Service registration. As the conflict escalated in the 1960s, draft evasion emerged as a significant facet of domestic opposition to the war, influencing public policy, legal precedent, and American political culture.

## Historical and Political Context

The Selective Service Act of 1948 established peacetime conscription, requiring all male U.S. citizens aged 18–26 to register for the draft. Early in the Vietnam conflict, the draft primarily relied on local draft boards’ evaluations, leading to perceived inequalities. Under President Lyndon B. Johnson, U.S. troop levels surged from 16,000 in 1963 to more than 500,000 by 1968. Against this backdrop, antiwar sentiment grew, especially among college students, intellectuals, and civil rights activists.

## Mechanisms of Draft Evasion

### Legal Deferments and Exemptions

* **Student Deferments**: College enrollment granted a II-S deferment, postponing induction; critics argued this favored middle- and upper-class youth.
* **Occupational Deferments**: II-A deferments applied to “essential” occupations (e.g., teachers, farmers).
* **Medical and Conscientious Objection**: Those with documented physical or mental health issues received deferments; conscientious objectors (COs) performed alternative service under the II-O classification, but acceptance criteria were stringent.

### Illicit Evasion

* **Failure to Register**: Some men missed registration deadlines or used false identities, risking felony charges.
* **Burning Draft Cards**: Symbolic acts of protest that also nullified one’s draft eligibility, leading to prosecution under the 1965 amendment to 50 U.S.C. § 462(b).
* **Medical or Psychiatric Subterfuge**: Some sought fraudulent diagnoses or exploited draft board corruption.

### Emigration

An estimated 30,000–100,000 draft-eligible men fled to Canada; fewer than 1,000 went to Sweden. Canadian immigration policies, while not openly advertised, allowed entry under family sponsorship or as skilled workers. Upon arrival, many applied for landed immigrant status; Sweden granted asylum to about 1,000 American draft evaders and deserters.

## Legal Response and Prosecution

President Richard Nixon’s administration sought to curtail draft evasion through stricter enforcement. Between 1965 and 1973, approximately 30,000 men were indicted for willful draft evasion, with around 3,000 convicted and sentenced to prison. High-profile prosecutions included:

* **United States v. O’Brien (1968)**: The Supreme Court upheld the conviction of David Paul O’Brien for burning his draft card, establishing that the government’s interest in maintaining an efficient draft system outweighed his First Amendment challenge.
* **Conscientious Objector Appeals**: CO applicants often faced appeals by local draft boards, culminating in landmark cases (e.g., Gillette v. United States, 1971), which restricted CO status to objections against all wars rather than specific conflicts.

## Political and Social Impact

Draft evasion intensified public debate over the war’s morality and the fairness of conscription. College campuses became centers of resistance, with organizations such as the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) and Students for a Democratic Society (SDS) assisting draft-eligible men. The draft’s perceived inequities fueled civil rights critiques, linking racial disparities in deferments to broader systemic injustice.

Politically, draft resistance contributed to President Johnson’s decision not to seek re-election in 1968. The issue remained salient in Nixon’s 1968 and 1972 campaigns, leading to his promise and eventual implementation of “Vietnamization” and troop withdrawals. In 1971, the Selective Service Act was amended to raise the draft age to 19–26 and eliminate many deferments.

## The End of the Draft and Amnesty

The last U.S. draft call occurred in 1973, following the end of U.S. combat operations under the Paris Peace Accords; conscription officially ended in 1975. President Jimmy Carter’s 1977 **Pardon Attorney General Order** granted conditional amnesty to draft evaders who performed public service or registered for alternative service, restoring their citizenship rights. Canada offered retroactive immigration status to many American exiles in subsequent decades.

## Legacy

Draft evasion during the Vietnam War left enduring legacies in American politics and society:

* **All-Volunteer Force**: In 1973, Congress replaced conscription with the all-volunteer U.S. Armed Forces, reshaping civil-military relations.
* **First Amendment Jurisprudence**: Cases like *O’Brien* and *Gillette* continue to influence speech and dissent law.
* **Cultural Depictions**: Literature, film (e.g., *The Deer Hunter*), and memoirs chronicled the draft’s personal and societal impacts.
* **Transnational Migration**: The Canadian “draft refuge” phenomenon forged enduring American-Canadian cultural ties and influenced asylum policies.

Draft evasion in the Vietnam era exemplifies the intersection of individual conscience, legal authority, and political protest, highlighting how compulsory military service can catalyze broader debates on equity, citizenship, and the legitimacy of wartime governance.
