**Soviet Occupation of Bessarabia and Northern Bukovina**

The **Soviet occupation of Bessarabia and Northern Bukovina** occurred in **June and July 1940**, when the **Soviet Union**, invoking the secret protocols of the **Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact**, issued an ultimatum to **Romania** demanding the cession of these territories. Under intense diplomatic and military pressure, Romania ceded the regions without armed resistance. The occupation marked a significant expansion of Soviet territory on its western frontier and represented a key episode in the geopolitical reshuffling of Eastern Europe during the early stages of **World War II**.

The annexed territories were subsequently incorporated into the **Moldavian Soviet Socialist Republic** (formed from parts of Bessarabia and the Moldavian ASSR) and the **Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic** (which absorbed Northern Bukovina and northern Bessarabia). The occupation was accompanied by deportations, political repression, and socio-economic restructuring, and remains a subject of historical dispute and national memory in both **Romania** and **Moldova**.

---

### Historical Background

**Bessarabia**, a historical region bounded by the Dniester and Prut rivers, was part of the **Principality of Moldavia** until it was annexed by the **Russian Empire** in **1812** following the Russo-Turkish War. After World War I and the collapse of the Russian Empire, Bessarabia declared union with Romania in **1918**, a move recognized by several Western powers but never accepted by Soviet Russia.

**Northern Bukovina**, historically part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, was incorporated into Romania after World War I. Though not included in the original Russian imperial claims, the Soviet Union later targeted Bukovina for strategic and ethnic reasons.

The Soviet Union maintained a policy of non-recognition of Romania’s sovereignty over Bessarabia throughout the interwar period. As the international situation deteriorated in 1939–1940, the USSR sought to reclaim these areas as part of its broader territorial ambitions in Eastern Europe.

---

### Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and Soviet Claims

On **23 August 1939**, the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany signed the **Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact**, which included secret protocols dividing Eastern Europe into respective spheres of influence. Under this agreement, Bessarabia was assigned to the Soviet sphere. Although Northern Bukovina was not explicitly mentioned in the pact, the Soviet Union later demanded it as well, citing security concerns and the presence of Ukrainian populations.

In June 1940, with France defeated by Nazi Germany and the international focus shifting westward, the Soviet Union moved to assert its claims. On **26 June 1940**, the USSR delivered an ultimatum to Romania demanding the immediate evacuation of Bessarabia and Northern Bukovina. Facing isolation and unwilling to risk war with the USSR, Romania complied and began withdrawing its forces.

---

### Occupation and Annexation

Soviet troops entered Bessarabia and Northern Bukovina on **28 June 1940**, facing minimal resistance. The occupation was swift and largely unopposed, though there were isolated skirmishes and reports of abuse and disorganization. The Soviet Union seized an area of approximately **50,000 square kilometers**, with a population of around **3.7 million people**.

On **2 August 1940**, the **Moldavian SSR** was established, combining central and eastern parts of Bessarabia with parts of the pre-existing **Moldavian Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic (MASSR)** on the left bank of the Dniester. The northern and southern portions of Bessarabia, along with Northern Bukovina and the Hertsa region, were incorporated into the Ukrainian SSR.

---

### Political and Social Impact

The Soviet occupation was followed by sweeping political and social changes. Communist authorities dismantled existing Romanian administrative structures and imposed Soviet governance, including nationalization of land and industry, collectivization of agriculture, and suppression of political opposition.

The Soviet regime also initiated mass arrests, executions, and deportations. Between **1940 and 1941**, tens of thousands of people—especially members of the former Romanian administration, landowners, intellectuals, and alleged anti-Soviet elements—were deported to **Siberia** and **Central Asia**. These repressive measures created deep resentment among local populations and contributed to enduring anti-Soviet sentiment.

The cultural impact was also significant. Romanian-language education was curtailed, the Latin alphabet was replaced with Cyrillic in Moldavia, and efforts were made to cultivate a distinct **Moldovan identity** separate from Romanian nationalism.

---

### World War II and the Aftermath

In **June 1941**, Romania, aligned with **Nazi Germany**, entered the war against the Soviet Union and reoccupied Bessarabia and Northern Bukovina. However, the Soviet Union reconquered the territories in **1944** during its advance westward. Following World War II, the annexation of the regions was formalized under the postwar Soviet order.

Romania was compelled to renounce its claims in the **1947 Paris Peace Treaty**, though the issue remained a source of national grievance. The Soviet Union maintained control of the territories until its dissolution in **1991**, after which **Moldova** declared independence, and Northern Bukovina remained part of **Ukraine**.

---

### Legacy

The Soviet occupation of Bessarabia and Northern Bukovina remains a contentious issue in Romanian and Moldovan historical memory. Romanian historiography often portrays it as a **violation of sovereignty** and a **forcible annexation**, while Soviet and later Russian interpretations have framed it as a **reunification of territories** with historically Russian or Ukrainian populations.

In Moldova, the legacy of Soviet rule is part of a broader debate over national identity—whether Moldovans constitute a separate nation or are part of the Romanian people. The occupation and its consequences continue to influence regional politics, identity formation, and Romanian-Moldovan-Ukrainian relations to this day.
