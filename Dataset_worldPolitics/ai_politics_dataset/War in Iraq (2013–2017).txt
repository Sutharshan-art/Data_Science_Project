**War in Iraq (2013–2017)**

**Introduction**
The War in Iraq from 2013 to 2017, often termed the Iraqi Civil War or the campaign against the Islamic State of Iraq and al-Sham (ISIS), was a multifaceted conflict involving the Iraqi government, Kurdish forces, various militia groups, and an international coalition. It followed the withdrawal of U.S. combat troops in 2011 and was precipitated by the rapid territorial gains of ISIS in 2014, leading to a concerted military effort to recapture lands seized in northern and western Iraq.

**Background and Origins**
After the U.S.-led invasion of 2003 and the subsequent sectarian insurgency, Iraq endured years of violence under a U.S. occupation and a fragile national government. Following the December 2011 departure of U.S. forces, Prime Minister Nouri al-Maliki’s administration faced widespread Sunni disaffection over perceived marginalization. The Syrian Civil War (beginning in 2011) provided fertile ground for extremist groups. In early 2013, remnants of al-Qaeda in Iraq and other militant factions coalesced into what became known as ISIS, exploiting local grievances and ungoverned spaces along the Iraq–Syria border.

**Rise of ISIS and Seizure of Territory**
In January 2014, ISIS captured the Iraqi city of Fallujah, marking its first major victory within Iraq’s internationally recognized borders. By June, the group launched a lightning offensive, seizing Mosul—Iraq’s second-largest city—and Tikrit, and declaring a “caliphate” across swathes of Iraq and Syria. The collapse of Iraqi Army units, low morale, and allegiance of some tribal elements to ISIS underscored the central government’s vulnerabilities. The rapid fall of Mosul prompted hundreds of thousands of civilian displacements and signaled an existential threat to Iraq’s territorial integrity.

**Formation of the International Coalition**
In response to ISIS’s advances and human rights abuses—including mass executions of Yazidis, Christians, and Shi‘a Muslims— the United States formed a coalition in August 2014 to conduct airstrikes and provide training, intelligence, and equipment to Iraqi and Kurdish forces. Over fifty states contributed to various capacities, from aerial refueling and reconnaissance to special operations support. The coalition’s intervention aimed not to reoccupy Iraq but to enable local forces to reclaim lost territory and prevent ISIS’s expansion.

**Iraqi Government and Popular Mobilization Forces**
Prime Minister Haider al-Abadi, who succeeded Maliki in September 2014, reorganized the Iraqi security apparatus and bolstered the Popular Mobilization Forces (PMF)—an umbrella of predominantly Shi‘a militias sanctioned by the state. While the PMF played a crucial role in recapturing areas such as Jurf al-Sakhar and Tikrit, their involvement raised concerns over human rights abuses, sectarian reprisals, and the influence of Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps. The Kurdistan Regional Government’s Peshmerga also secured and administered northeastern regions, contributing to the broader effort against ISIS.

**Major Campaigns and Turning Points**
Key military operations unfolded in successive phases:

* **Fall of Ramadi (2015):** Retaken by Iraqi forces with coalition air support after weeks of fighting.
* **Battle for Baiji (2015–2016):** Intensive urban combat to retake Iraq’s largest oil refinery.
* **Liberation of Fallujah (May–June 2016):** A protracted offensive culminating in full government control.
* **Siege and Fall of Mosul (October 2016 – July 2017):** A large-scale operation involving Iraqi Army divisions, Peshmerga, PMF, local police, and coalition airpower, resulting in Mosul’s recapture after nine months of urban warfare.
* **Capture of Tal Afar (August 2017) and Hawija (October 2017):** Final pockets of resistance dismantled, marking the end of ISIS’s conventional presence in Iraq.

**Humanitarian and Civilian Impact**
The conflict precipitated one of the largest displacement crises since World War II, with over three million Iraqis uprooted. ISIS’s imposition of strict social codes, slavery of women, and targeted executions generated widespread atrocities. Coalition bombardments and ground fighting inflicted damage on civilian infrastructure, historical sites, and neighborhoods, complicating post-conflict reconstruction and reconciliation.

**Political and Regional Dynamics**
The war in Iraq intersected with broader regional rivalries. Iran provided advisory and logistical support to Shi‘a militias, enhancing its influence in Baghdad. Turkey expressed concern over Kurdish territorial gains and deployed forces to northern Iraq to prevent Kurdish unification. Gulf Arab states, wary of Iranian ascendancy, contributed to coalition efforts. The conflict tested U.S. policy on military engagement, burden-sharing, and the limits of airpower in counterinsurgency.

**End of Major Combat Operations and Aftermath**
On December 9, 2017, Prime Minister al-Abadi formally declared victory over ISIS in Iraq. Although ISIS continued as an insurgent movement—carrying out bombings and guerrilla attacks—the loss of its territorial “state” marked a strategic defeat. The Iraqi government faced pressing challenges: rebuilding infrastructure, repatriating and rehabilitating displaced populations, addressing sectarian tensions, and integrating former combatants. U.S. troops remained in Iraq in an advisory capacity, while the PMF sought formal incorporation into state security structures.

**Legacy and Significance**
The 2013–2017 War in Iraq underscored the complexity of contemporary conflict, where non-state actors can seize and govern territory, provoking multinational responses short of large-scale Western ground invasions. It highlighted the imperative of inclusive governance in preventing extremist resurgence and demonstrated both the capabilities and limitations of coalition airpower. The conflict reshaped Iraq’s political landscape, cemented Iran’s regional role, and influenced global counterterrorism doctrine, leaving enduring implications for state sovereignty, sectarian relations, and the future of Middle East stability.
