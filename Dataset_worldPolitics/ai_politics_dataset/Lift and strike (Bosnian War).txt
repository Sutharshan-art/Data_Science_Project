**Lift and Strike** was a policy proposal during the Bosnian War (1992–1995) advocating that the United Nations arms embargo on Bosnia and Herzegovina be lifted (“lift”) and that NATO or allied air strikes be carried out against Bosnian Serb forces (“strike”). The concept emerged amid growing frustration in Sarajevo and Western capitals over the inability of the Bosnian government to defend itself against attacks by the Army of Republika Srpska (VRS) and the widespread humanitarian crises—particularly the sieges of Sarajevo and Gorazde.

**Origins of the Arms Embargo and Calls to Lift It**
In September 1991, the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 713, imposing a general embargo on all deliveries of weapons and military equipment to the territory of the former Yugoslavia. Though applied equally to all parties, the embargo disproportionately disadvantaged the newly independent Bosnian government, which lacked a legacy arsenal, while Bosnian Serb forces inherited equipment from the Yugoslav People’s Army. By mid-1992, Bosnian leaders and humanitarian agencies were pressing the UN and key Western governments to lift the embargo as a means of restoring a rough balance of military power.

**Evolution of “Lift and Strike”**
The term “lift and strike” first gained currency in international policy circles in late 1992 and early 1993. It combined two linked propositions:

1. **Lifting the Embargo**: Remove or selectively suspend the UN arms embargo to permit defensive arms supplies to the Army of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina (ARBiH), enabling it to hold territory and deter further aggression.
2. **Air Strikes**: Authorize targeted air strikes against VRS heavy weapons—artillery, armor, surface-to-air missile installations—especially around civilian concentrations under siege, to degrade the Serb capacity to shell towns and to signal Western resolve.

Proponents argued that only a credible threat of air power, backed by an armed Bosnian defense, would compel Bosnian Serb leaders to negotiate seriously and cease ethnic cleansing campaigns.

**Debate Among Western Governments**

* **United States**: The U.S. initially resisted calls to lift the embargo, fearing escalation and deepening involvement. In January 1993, President Clinton’s incoming administration expressed openness to lifting the arms restrictions but remained cautious on strikes.
* **United Kingdom and France**: Both governments supported strengthening UNPROFOR’s mandate but were divided over air operations without explicit UN Security Council approval under Chapter VII of the UN Charter.
* **Germany**: As one of the few states willing to supply nonlethal military assistance to Bosnia in 1994, Germany’s position illustrated Europe’s gradual shift toward a more interventionist stance.

**UN Security Council Position**
Repeated draft resolutions to lift or suspend the arms embargo stalled in the Security Council due to opposition from China and Pakistan, who feared setting a precedent for breaching general embargoes. Likewise, attempted mandates for robust air enforcement—beyond the limited “close air support” for UNPROFOR convoys—failed to achieve unanimity.

**Military and Humanitarian Context**
Through 1993, Sarajevo endured near-daily shelling by VRS heavy guns, resulting in thousands of civilian casualties. In eastern Bosnia, towns like Srebrenica and Zepa faced siege conditions. Relief efforts were hampered by sniper fire and artillery. Advocates of “lift and strike” asserted that without decisive action, Bosnian civilians would continue to suffer siege warfare and ethnic cleansing.

**Partial Shifts and Covert Aid**
By late 1994, the U.S. Congress pushed for conditional lifting of the embargo. In practice, some Balkan governments and private networks began covert arms transfers to the ARBiH. NATO established “air corridors” to deliver humanitarian supplies and conducted occasional show-of-force overflights, laying groundwork for later enforcement.

**Operation Deliberate Force and Aftermath**
Following the Markale marketplace massacre in Sarajevo in August 1995, NATO secured UN authorization (Resolution 998) to enforce a no-fly zone and to conduct air strikes against VRS positions. Operation Deliberate Force (late August – September 1995) degraded Serb air defenses and heavy artillery, significantly reducing shelling of Sarajevo. Simultaneously, the U.S.-brokered lifting of the arms embargo in July 1995 enabled more robust Bosnian rearmament.

The combined air campaign and strengthened Bosnian Army helped shift momentum, contributing to the military pressure that led Bosnian Serb leaders to join peace talks. The resulting Dayton Accords (November 1995) ended hostilities and established Bosnia and Herzegovina as a single state composed of two entities.

**Legacy and Significance**
“Lift and strike” remains a case study in crisis decision-making and the limits of UN peacekeeping under Chapter VI mandates. Its ultimate partial implementation—lifting the embargo late in 1995 and carrying out robust NATO air strikes—demonstrated that Western air power, coupled with local forces bolstered by arms supplies, could alter battlefield dynamics. The policy controversy illustrated tensions between principles of state sovereignty, impartial peacekeeping, and the emerging norm of humanitarian intervention. It also influenced later debates on the use of force in Kosovo (1999) and on the Responsibility to Protect doctrine in the twenty-first century.
