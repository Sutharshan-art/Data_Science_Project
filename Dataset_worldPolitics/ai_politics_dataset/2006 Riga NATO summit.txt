**2006 Riga NATO Summit**

The **2006 Riga NATO Summit** was held on **November 28–29, 2006**, in **Riga, Latvia**, and represented a significant moment in the North Atlantic Treaty Organization’s post–Cold War evolution. As the first NATO summit hosted in a former **Soviet republic**, it symbolized the deepening integration of Central and Eastern European countries into the Euro-Atlantic security structure and the continued eastward expansion of NATO’s political influence.

The summit focused on key strategic issues, including the **ongoing NATO-led mission in Afghanistan**, the need for enhanced military capabilities, the **future of NATO enlargement**, and the alliance's role in addressing new global security threats. It also marked a turning point in discussions on transforming NATO into a more agile and expeditionary force capable of projecting stability beyond its traditional area of operations.

---

### Historical Context

By 2006, NATO had undergone profound transformation since the end of the Cold War. It had expanded significantly, admitting seven new members in **2004**—Bulgaria, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Romania, Slovakia, and Slovenia—and had engaged in operations beyond Europe, most notably in the **Balkans** and **Afghanistan**. The **2002 Prague Summit** and the **2004 Istanbul Summit** had set the stage for operational and institutional reforms to respond to emerging threats such as **terrorism**, **cybersecurity**, and **weapons of mass destruction**.

The Riga Summit occurred at a time of growing concern about the deteriorating situation in Afghanistan, where NATO had assumed command of the **International Security Assistance Force (ISAF)**. It also took place in the shadow of renewed tensions between NATO and **Russia**, particularly following the **Orange Revolution in Ukraine (2004–2005)** and the **Russia–Ukraine gas dispute of 2006**, which raised fears about Moscow’s use of energy as a political tool.

---

### Main Topics and Outcomes

#### 1. **Afghanistan Mission**

The top priority at the Riga Summit was the **NATO-led ISAF mission** in Afghanistan, which had expanded significantly in 2006 to cover the entire country. NATO leaders focused on the need for:

* Improved coordination between military and civilian efforts.
* Greater contributions of troops and resources from all member states.
* Reduced national caveats—restrictions placed by member governments on how their forces could be deployed.

The summit declaration emphasized long-term commitment to stabilizing Afghanistan, building local security forces, and supporting governance and development. However, divisions remained over the uneven burden-sharing among allies, with the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and the Netherlands taking on most of the combat operations in volatile regions like the south.

#### 2. **Transformation and Capabilities**

Another central theme was the transformation of NATO's military capabilities to meet modern security challenges. The Riga Summit endorsed initiatives to:

* Further develop the **NATO Response Force (NRF)**, a rapid-deployment, multinational unit capable of responding to crises within days.
* Implement the **Comprehensive Political Guidance (CPG)**, a strategic document adopted to align NATO's planning with contemporary security challenges.
* Strengthen airlift and strategic transport capabilities, particularly to support out-of-area operations.

These efforts aimed to make NATO more flexible, deployable, and responsive, especially in regions where terrorism, instability, or humanitarian crises might require rapid intervention.

#### 3. **NATO Enlargement and Open Door Policy**

While no new countries were invited to join NATO at Riga, the summit reaffirmed the alliance's **open-door policy** and expressed continued support for countries aspiring to membership, including:

* **Albania**, **Croatia**, and the **former Yugoslav Republic of Macedonia** (now North Macedonia), all of which were in NATO’s **Membership Action Plan (MAP)**.
* **Georgia** and **Ukraine**, whose Euro-Atlantic aspirations had gained momentum following democratic movements and political reforms.

The alliance reiterated that enlargement would be based on performance and the fulfillment of democratic and military criteria, rather than geopolitical pressure or geographic limits.

#### 4. **Energy Security**

The Riga Summit included a discussion on the growing strategic importance of **energy security**, driven by recent energy supply disruptions and increasing dependence on foreign energy sources. NATO leaders agreed that the alliance should play a role in supporting the protection of critical energy infrastructure and improving the resilience of member states to energy-related threats.

However, the alliance remained cautious about taking on a central role in energy policy, emphasizing that any action would complement—not replace—efforts by the **European Union** and other international organizations.

---

### Political Significance

The 2006 Riga Summit underscored NATO's commitment to adapting to a complex and evolving security environment. It highlighted the alliance’s expanding geographic and operational scope, while also revealing internal challenges, particularly in terms of unity, burden-sharing, and political will.

The decision to hold the summit in Latvia—a country that had joined NATO only two years earlier—was a symbolic reaffirmation of NATO's commitment to the security of its eastern members. It also sent a message to Russia that the alliance would continue to support the sovereignty and Euro-Atlantic integration of countries in the former Soviet sphere.

---

### Legacy

The **2006 Riga NATO Summit** is remembered for reinforcing NATO’s expeditionary mission, advancing military modernization, and supporting further enlargement. It exposed persistent challenges within the alliance, particularly regarding operational cohesion and divergent threat perceptions among members.

The summit laid the groundwork for further enlargement in **2008**, deeper involvement in Afghanistan, and the continued evolution of NATO as a political and military alliance capable of addressing both traditional and unconventional threats in the 21st century.
