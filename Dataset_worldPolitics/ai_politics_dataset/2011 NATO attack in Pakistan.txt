**2011 NATO Attack in Pakistan**

The **2011 NATO attack in Pakistan**, also known as the **Salala incident**, occurred on **November 26, 2011**, when **North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO)** forces, operating in **Afghanistan**, conducted an airstrike on two **Pakistan Army border posts** located in the **Mohmand Agency** region of Pakistan’s **Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA)**. The attack resulted in the deaths of **24 Pakistani soldiers** and injuries to **13 others**, triggering a severe diplomatic crisis between **Pakistan** and the **United States-led NATO coalition** in Afghanistan.

The incident marked one of the lowest points in U.S.–Pakistan relations during the post-9/11 era and intensified Pakistani concerns over violations of its sovereignty, particularly in the context of NATO’s expanding military operations along the **Afghanistan–Pakistan border**. The fallout from the attack led to the closure of NATO supply routes through Pakistan, the eviction of U.S. forces from a strategic airbase, and a reevaluation of bilateral military cooperation.

---

### Background

Since the onset of the **War in Afghanistan (2001–2021)**, Pakistan had played a complex role as both a frontline ally in the U.S.-led “**Global War on Terror**” and a country with deep strategic interests in regional stability and sovereignty. The porous border between Pakistan and Afghanistan had become a zone of military tension, with NATO accusing militant groups of using Pakistani territory as a sanctuary, and Pakistan objecting to cross-border operations and drone strikes.

Tensions had been building in 2011, particularly after the **U.S. Navy SEAL raid in Abbottabad** on **May 2**, which killed **Osama bin Laden** without prior notification to Pakistani authorities. This event significantly eroded trust between the two allies and heightened scrutiny of Pakistani cooperation with NATO.

NATO had also increased its reliance on **air support and border operations**, sometimes involving incursions into Pakistani territory. Pakistani military officials had repeatedly warned of the risks such operations posed to bilateral relations and on-the-ground coordination.

---

### The Incident

In the early hours of **November 26, 2011**, NATO forces—comprising U.S. and Afghan troops—conducted an operation near the Afghanistan–Pakistan border, reportedly targeting suspected insurgent positions. During the operation, NATO helicopters and aircraft fired on two Pakistani military outposts—**Salala 1 and Salala 2**—located in the Baizai subdivision of Mohmand Agency.

The airstrike lasted for nearly **two hours**, and multiple requests to NATO command to cease fire reportedly went unheeded or were delayed due to communication breakdowns. In total, **24 Pakistani soldiers** were killed, including two officers, and **13 others** were wounded.

NATO officials initially described the attack as a **“regrettable incident”**, claiming it was a case of **“mistaken identity”**, with coalition forces believing they were engaging hostile militants. The Pakistani military, however, stated that its positions were well known and had been clearly marked, suggesting that the strike was deliberate or, at best, grossly negligent.

---

### Political and Diplomatic Fallout

The Pakistani government reacted with outrage. In the immediate aftermath of the attack, Islamabad took several decisive actions:

* **Closure of NATO supply routes**: Pakistan blocked all ground supply routes to NATO forces in Afghanistan, which had been vital for delivering fuel and equipment. These routes remained closed for over seven months.
* **Eviction from Shamsi Airbase**: The government ordered the U.S. to vacate **Shamsi Airbase**, a remote facility reportedly used for launching drone operations in Pakistan’s tribal areas.
* **Diplomatic protests**: Pakistan lodged formal protests with both NATO and the United Nations and demanded a full apology and accountability.
* **Boycott of the Bonn Conference**: Pakistan pulled out of the **Bonn Conference on Afghanistan**, held in December 2011, citing the attack as a violation of its sovereignty.

Public sentiment in Pakistan was deeply affected, with anti-American protests erupting in major cities. The incident amplified existing opposition to Pakistan’s cooperation with the U.S. in counterterrorism efforts.

---

### NATO and U.S. Response

NATO officials expressed regret for the loss of life and promised an investigation. The **U.S. Department of Defense** conducted a review, which concluded that both NATO and Pakistani forces bore blame, citing poor coordination, miscommunication, and faulty maps. However, the report stopped short of offering a full apology.

Pakistan rejected the findings, maintaining that its forces had done nothing to provoke the attack. For months, diplomatic relations remained frozen, with military and intelligence cooperation suspended or curtailed.

It was not until **July 2012** that the standoff eased, when U.S. Secretary of State **Hillary Clinton** issued an official statement expressing **“deep regret”** and extending **“sincere condolences”** to the Pakistani people and families of the victims. Following the statement, Pakistan agreed to reopen NATO supply routes.

---

### Strategic and Political Impact

The 2011 NATO attack in Pakistan had long-lasting repercussions for regional security and U.S.–Pakistan relations:

* **Erosion of Trust**: The incident severely undermined strategic trust between Washington and Islamabad, leading both sides to reassess their military cooperation.
* **Shift in Regional Alliances**: Pakistan increasingly explored alternatives to U.S. reliance, strengthening ties with **China**, **Russia**, and **Iran** in subsequent years.
* **Military Sovereignty Emphasis**: The Pakistani military adopted a more assertive stance in protecting national sovereignty, leading to stricter rules of engagement near the border.
* **Operational Constraints on NATO**: The temporary closure of supply routes forced NATO to rely on more expensive northern routes through Central Asia, affecting logistics and strategy.

---

The **Salala incident** remains a defining moment in the turbulent relationship between NATO and Pakistan, underscoring the challenges of coalition warfare, cross-border coordination, and the political sensitivities of operating in complex, overlapping conflict zones.
