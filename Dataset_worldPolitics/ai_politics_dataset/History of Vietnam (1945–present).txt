**History of Vietnam (1945–present)**

The modern political history of Vietnam begins in 1945, with the collapse of Japanese occupation, and spans the emergence of a divided nation, a protracted war for reunification, socialist consolidation, economic renovation, and integration into the global economy.

---

## Democratic Republic of Vietnam and First Indochina War (1945–1954)

On 2 September 1945, Hồ Chí Minh proclaimed the **Democratic Republic of Vietnam** (DRV) in Hanoi, following the August Revolution that overthrew French colonial rule and Japanese occupation. The DRV, led by the Indochinese Communist Party (ICP), faced immediate challenge as French forces attempted to reassert control.

Between 1946 and 1954, the **First Indochina War** pitted the Việt Minh against French Union troops. Political mobilization combined nationalist and socialist agendas, as land reform and mass organization secured peasant support. The Battle of Điện Biên Phủ (March–May 1954) ended in French defeat. At the **Geneva Conference** (July 1954), Vietnam was provisionally divided at the 17th parallel: the DRV in the north under Hồ’s communist government, and a non-communist State of Vietnam in the south, pending national elections planned for 1956 (subsequently canceled).

---

## Republic of Vietnam and Escalation of Conflict (1954–1975)

In South Vietnam, Prime Minister Ngô Đình Diệm consolidated power by sidelining rival nationalist and religious factions. With U.S. backing, he canceled nationwide elections and established the **Republic of Vietnam** (RVN) in 1955. Political repression of the Buddhist majority and the communist **National Liberation Front** (NLF, or Viet Cong) insurgency fueled instability.

From 1959, the NLF, supported by the DRV, launched guerrilla warfare in the south. U.S. military advisors, deployed since the mid-1950s, were supplemented by combat troops after the Gulf of Tonkin incident (1964). Under Presidents Lyndon Johnson and Richard Nixon, the United States pursued a strategy of attrition, peaking at over 500,000 troops by 1969. Peace negotiations began in Paris in 1968.

Following the **Paris Peace Accords** (January 1973), U.S. forces withdrew. South Vietnamese resistance collapsed during the **Spring Offensive**; on 30 April 1975, tanks entered Saigon, marking the end of the Republic of Vietnam and achieving reunification under communist rule.

---

## Socialist Republic of Vietnam and Post-war Reconstruction (1976–1986)

On 2 July 1976, the **Socialist Republic of Vietnam** (SRV) was officially proclaimed, merging North and South under the Vietnam Workers’ Party (renamed the Communist Party of Vietnam, CPV, in 1976). The new government nationalized industry and collective agriculture, pursuing central planning across the reunified territory.

Challenges included widespread infrastructure devastation, refugee crises, and economic isolation. The SRV intervened in Cambodia (1978) to oust the Khmer Rouge, provoking Chinese punitive raids in 1979. Border conflicts with China and strains with Cambodia and the Soviet Union compounded diplomatic and economic pressures.

---

## Đổi Mới Reforms and Economic Opening (1986–2000)

At its 6th National Congress (December 1986), the CPV launched **Đổi Mới** (“renovation”), shifting from orthodox central planning toward a **socialist-oriented market economy**. Measures included decollectivization of agriculture, encouragement of private enterprise, foreign-investment incentives, and gradual trade liberalization.

Economic growth accelerated through the 1990s, lifting millions out of poverty. Diplomatic normalization with the United States in 1995 and accession to the **Association of Southeast Asian Nations** (ASEAN) in 1995 signaled a broader international integration. In 2000, Vietnam joined the **World Trade Organization** (WTO) accession process, further anchoring economic reforms.

---

## Political Continuity and Emerging Challenges (2000–present)

Vietnam’s one-party system remains under the CPV’s leadership, with National Congresses every five years determining policy direction and personnel. Economic liberalization continued, with GDP growth averaging over 6 percent annually between 2000 and 2019. Urbanization, foreign direct investment, and export-led manufacturing—particularly electronics, textiles, and agriculture—have driven transformation.

Challenges persist in balancing economic dynamism with political control. The government maintains restrictions on political dissent, media freedom, and civil society organization. Anti-corruption campaigns under General Secretaries Nông Đức Mạnh (2001–2011), Nguyễn Phú Trọng (2011–2021), and Nguyễn Phú Trọng (re-elected 2021) have targeted both low-level bureaucrats and high-profile officials. Human-rights groups cite continued imprisonment of political bloggers and religious activists.

Environmental degradation, climate vulnerability in the Mekong Delta, and rising inequality have prompted policy responses, including sustainable development goals and investment in renewable energy. Vietnam’s strategic location between rival great powers—China and the United States—has led to a **“bamboo diplomacy”** of nuanced balancing: deepening trade ties with China while enhancing security cooperation with the U.S., Japan, and ASEAN partners.

---

Over seven decades, Vietnam has transformed from a war-torn battlefield of competing ideologies into a rapidly developing nation under one-party communist rule. Its history reflects enduring themes of colonial resistance, national reunification, socialist experimentation, market-oriented reform, and careful navigation of global power dynamics.
