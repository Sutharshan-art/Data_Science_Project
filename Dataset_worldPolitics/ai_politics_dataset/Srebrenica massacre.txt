**Srebrenica Massacre**

The **Srebrenica massacre**, also known as the **Srebrenica genocide**, was the mass killing of more than 8,000 Bosniak (Bosnian Muslim) men and boys by units of the **Army of Republika Srpska (VRS)** in and around the town of **Srebrenica**, Bosnia and Herzegovina, in **July 1995**, during the **Bosnian War**. It is regarded as the worst atrocity on European soil since World War II and was later classified as genocide by international courts.

The massacre occurred in a declared **UN "safe area"** and exposed the failure of international peacekeeping and diplomacy during the Yugoslav Wars. It also had profound political and legal ramifications, influencing international humanitarian law, post-conflict justice, and the political landscape of post-war Bosnia and Herzegovina.

---

### Background

The **Bosnian War (1992–1995)** followed the breakup of Yugoslavia and was marked by ethnic conflict among Bosniaks, Croats, and Serbs. The town of Srebrenica, located in eastern Bosnia near the border with Serbia, became a focal point of violence between Bosnian Serb forces and Bosniak communities.

In **1993**, the **United Nations** declared Srebrenica a “safe area” under the protection of **UNPROFOR**, the United Nations Protection Force. A lightly armed Dutch battalion (Dutchbat) was stationed to monitor and protect the enclave, which had become overcrowded with thousands of displaced Bosniak civilians.

Despite the UN designation, the enclave was effectively besieged by the VRS, under the command of **General Ratko Mladić** and the political leadership of **Radovan Karadžić**, the president of the self-declared **Republika Srpska**. Food and medical supplies were scarce, and the enclave's population was vulnerable to attack.

---

### Events of July 1995

In early July 1995, Bosnian Serb forces launched a coordinated offensive against the Srebrenica enclave. On **July 11**, the VRS entered Srebrenica with little resistance, and thousands of civilians fled to the UN compound in nearby **Potočari**, seeking protection from the Dutch peacekeepers.

Over the next several days, Serb forces systematically separated men and boys from women and children. While women and children were deported to Bosniak-held territory, the males were detained, executed, and buried in mass graves in surrounding areas. Many victims were later reburied in secondary graves in an effort to conceal the extent of the crime.

The massacre was planned and executed with logistical precision. Victims were transported to killing sites, shot, and buried using heavy machinery. Survivors who attempted to flee through the surrounding forests were ambushed or captured and subsequently killed.

---

### International Reaction and Aftermath

The Srebrenica massacre drew widespread condemnation from the international community. However, at the time, the **UN and NATO** were criticized for their inaction. The Dutch peacekeepers were unable to prevent the massacre, and their limited mandate and resources highlighted the weaknesses of UN peacekeeping operations in conflict zones.

The massacre was a turning point in international efforts to resolve the conflict. In its aftermath, NATO intensified its airstrikes against Bosnian Serb positions, which, combined with Croatian and Bosniak ground offensives, eventually forced the Serbs to negotiate. The war formally ended with the signing of the **Dayton Accords** in **December 1995**, which established the political structure of post-war Bosnia and Herzegovina.

---

### Legal and Political Consequences

The Srebrenica massacre was investigated and prosecuted by the **International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY)**, based in The Hague. In 2001, the ICTY ruled in the case of **Prosecutor v. Krstić** that the killings constituted genocide. This finding was affirmed in later cases, including the trials of **Radovan Karadžić** and **Ratko Mladić**, who were convicted of genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity.

In **2007**, the **International Court of Justice (ICJ)** confirmed that the events in Srebrenica were genocide under international law, marking the first time a state was held responsible for failing to prevent and punish genocide committed within its borders.

The massacre remains a politically sensitive and divisive issue in the Balkans. While it is widely recognized as genocide in the international community, some political figures in **Serbia** and **Republika Srpska** dispute this characterization or minimize the scale of the crime. Efforts to achieve reconciliation and justice have been complicated by ethnic tensions and contested historical narratives.

---

### Commemoration

Each year on **July 11**, thousands gather at the **Srebrenica-Potočari Memorial and Cemetery** to commemorate the victims. The site includes a memorial center and mass graves where newly identified remains are buried. The identification of victims through **DNA analysis** continues, coordinated by the **International Commission on Missing Persons (ICMP)**.

The legacy of Srebrenica has become emblematic of the failures of international protection in conflict zones and the enduring challenge of preventing mass atrocities. It also serves as a focal point in educational and remembrance initiatives across Europe and beyond.

---

### Conclusion

The Srebrenica massacre stands as a stark reminder of the consequences of ethnic hatred, failed diplomacy, and international inaction. Its recognition as genocide has contributed to the development of international legal norms and accountability mechanisms, while its memory continues to shape post-war politics, reconciliation, and identity in Bosnia and Herzegovina and the wider Balkans.
