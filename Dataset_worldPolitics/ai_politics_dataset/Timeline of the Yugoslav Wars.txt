**Timeline of the Yugoslav Wars (1991–2001)**

The Yugoslav Wars were a series of ethnically rooted conflicts and insurrections following the disintegration of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia. Between 1991 and 2001, five principal wars and insurgencies reshaped Southeast Europe’s political map and precipitated the creation of seven independent states.

---

### Background

After Josip Broz Tito’s death in 1980, rising economic strains and surging nationalism among Yugoslavia’s six republics and two autonomous provinces eroded federal cohesion. In 1990, the reintroduction of multiparty politics allowed nationalist parties to gain power in Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Serbia, setting the stage for secessionist conflicts.

---

## 1991

* **June 25–July 7:** **Slovenian Ten-Day War**
  Slovenia and Croatia declare independence. The Yugoslav People’s Army (JNA) intervenes in Slovenia. Rapid negotiations brokered by the European Community lead to a withdrawal of JNA forces and a cease-fire.

* **June 25:** **Croatian War of Independence Begins**
  Following its independence declaration, Croatia faces a Serb insurgency in the Krajina region, backed by the JNA. Ethnic Serbs reject Croatian sovereignty, seize towns, and proclaim the Republic of Serbian Krajina.

* **August 1991:** **Battle of Vukovar**
  The JNA and Serb militias besiege Vukovar for 87 days. The city falls in November, followed by mass expulsions and executions of Croatian civilians.

* **December 1991:** **International Recognition**
  Slovenia, Croatia, and Bosnia and Herzegovina gain diplomatic recognition from the European Community and the United States, further deepening divisions.

---

## 1992

* **March 3:** **Bosnian Independence Referendum**
  Bosniak and Croat–majority electorates vote overwhelmingly to secede; Bosnian Serbs boycott.

* **April 6:** **Bosnian War Begins**
  Following international recognition of Bosnian sovereignty, ethnic Serb forces—backed by the JNA—begin a campaign of siege warfare (including Sarajevo), ethnic cleansing, and mass atrocities.

* **May 30:** **UNPROFOR Deployment**
  The United Nations Protection Force is deployed in Bosnia to safeguard “safe areas” and escort humanitarian convoys. Similar deployments follow in Croatia.

---

## 1993

* **January–July:** **Croat–Bosniak Conflict**
  Tensions between Bosnian Croats and Bosniaks in central Bosnia and Herzegovina erupt into open warfare, complicating the broader conflict and leading to a tripartite struggle among Serb, Croat, and Bosniak forces.

* **July 22:** **UN Safe Areas Established**
  Under UN Security Council Resolution 819, Srebrenica is designated a “safe area,” later followed by other enclaves—though their protection proves inadequate.

---

## 1994

* **March 18:** **Washington Agreement**
  Croatia and Bosnia and Herzegovina sign a U.S.-brokered accord ending Croat–Bosniak fighting and forming the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina, isolating Bosnian Serb forces.

* **NATO Air Patrols Begin**
  NATO commences aerial surveillance over Bosnia under Operation Sharp Guard, enforcing the UN arms embargo.

---

## 1995

* **May 1–4:** **Operation Flash (Croatia)**
  Croatian forces retake Western Slavonia from Serb control, precipitating mass Serb civilian flight.

* **August 4–7:** **Operation Storm**
  Croatia launches a decisive offensive, recapturing Krajina and effectively ending Serb resistance in Croatian territory. Over 200,000 Serbs flee.

* **August 30–September 20:** **Operation Deliberate Force**
  NATO conducts sustained airstrikes against Bosnian Serb positions following the Srebrenica massacre (July) and Markale marketplace shelling (August), compelling Serb leaders to negotiate.

* **November 1–21:** **Dayton Peace Conference**
  Negotiated at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base near Dayton, Ohio, and formalized in Paris, the General Framework Agreement ends the Bosnian War by dividing Bosnia and Herzegovina into two entities: the Federation and Republika Srpska.

---

## 1998–1999

* **March 1, 1998:** **Kosovo Conflict Intensifies**
  The Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA) escalates attacks against Serbian authorities, prompting harsh reprisals and civilian displacement.

* **September 23, 1998:** **Rambouillet Peace Talks**
  International efforts to negotiate autonomy for Kosovo fail after Serbia refuses full acceding to proposed accords.

* **March 24–June 10, 1999:** **NATO Bombing of Yugoslavia (Operation Allied Force)**
  Without UN authorization, NATO conducts a 78-day air campaign against FR Yugoslavia (Serbia and Montenegro) to halt human rights abuses in Kosovo. NATO and Yugoslavia agree to a cease-fire on June 10.

* **June–July 1999:** **KFOR Deployment and UNMIK Establishment**
  NATO’s Kosovo Force deploys to maintain security; the UN Interim Administration Mission in Kosovo begins governing the province.

---

## 2000–2001

* **October 5, 2000:** **Overthrow of Slobodan Milošević**
  Mass protests in Belgrade lead to the ouster of President Milošević, paving the way for democratic reforms.

* **2001:** **Insurgency in Macedonia**
  Ethnic Albanian rebels in the Republic of North Macedonia (then FYROM) launch an insurgency seeking greater rights. The Ohrid Framework Agreement in August 2001 grants constitutional changes and ends the conflict.

---

**Aftermath and Legacy**
The Yugoslav Wars resulted in approximately 140,000 deaths, 4 million displaced persons, and profound economic destruction. They prompted the first prosecutions for genocide and war crimes by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY). The conflicts dissolved a once‐unified federation into seven sovereign states—Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro, North Macedonia, and Kosovo (partially recognized)—and underscored the challenges of post-communist transitions, ethnic reconciliation, and international peacekeeping in the post–Cold War era.
