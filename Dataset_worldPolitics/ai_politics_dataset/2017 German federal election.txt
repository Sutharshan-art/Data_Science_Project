**2017 German Federal Election**

The **2017 German federal election** was held on **September 24, 2017**, to elect the members of the **19th Bundestag**, the federal parliament of **Germany**. The election resulted in a fourth consecutive term for **Chancellor Angela Merkel**, leader of the **Christian Democratic Union (CDU)**, though her party and its Bavarian sister party, the **Christian Social Union (CSU)**, suffered significant losses. The election was notable for the entry of the **Alternative for Germany (AfD)** into the Bundestag, marking the first time a far-right party had won seats in the national legislature since the 1950s.

The election took place against a backdrop of political fragmentation, growing polarization, and heightened debate over immigration, national identity, and Germany’s role in Europe. Although the CDU/CSU remained the largest parliamentary bloc, the emergence of six parliamentary parties in the Bundestag made coalition-building more complex, reflecting a broader shift in German political culture toward multiparty governance.

---

### Historical and Political Context

The 2017 election followed Merkel's third term (2013–2017), during which Germany experienced a series of major political developments. Merkel's decision in 2015 to allow over one million refugees and asylum seekers—primarily from Syria, Afghanistan, and Iraq—into the country dominated the political discourse in the years leading up to the vote. While some praised her humanitarian stance, others criticized the move as a trigger for social unrest, security concerns, and the rise of populist sentiment.

Internationally, the election came at a time of growing uncertainty in global politics. The **United Kingdom’s vote to leave the European Union (Brexit)** in 2016 and the election of **Donald Trump** in the United States contributed to a sense of shifting geopolitical alignments. Merkel, often described as the de facto leader of Europe, was seen by many as a stabilizing figure in an era of political upheaval.

Domestically, Germany enjoyed relative economic stability, with low unemployment and strong export performance. However, issues such as wage inequality, digital infrastructure, climate change, and the future of the European Union featured prominently in party platforms and debates.

---

### Electoral System

Germany’s federal elections are conducted using a **mixed-member proportional representation system**, in which each voter casts two votes:

* The **first vote** elects a candidate from a single-member constituency via first-past-the-post.
* The **second vote** determines the proportional allocation of seats among parties at the federal level.

A party must win at least **5% of the second vote** or secure three constituency seats to qualify for proportional representation. The Bundestag normally has **598 seats**, but overhang and compensatory seats often increase the final total. In 2017, the Bundestag expanded to **709 seats**, the largest in German history at the time.

---

### Major Parties and Leaders

* **Christian Democratic Union (CDU)/Christian Social Union (CSU)** – Angela Merkel (Chancellor since 2005)
* **Social Democratic Party (SPD)** – Martin Schulz (former President of the European Parliament)
* **Alternative for Germany (AfD)** – Alexander Gauland and Alice Weidel (lead candidates)
* **Free Democratic Party (FDP)** – Christian Lindner
* **The Left (Die Linke)** – Sahra Wagenknecht and Dietmar Bartsch
* **Alliance 90/The Greens (Grüne)** – Katrin Göring-Eckardt and Cem Özdemir

The election campaign featured televised debates, town halls, and increased use of digital media. The SPD briefly gained momentum after nominating Schulz, but this "Schulz effect" faded by mid-2017, and Merkel maintained a significant lead in public opinion polls.

---

### Election Results

| Party      | Vote Share (%) | Seats Won |
| ---------- | -------------- | --------- |
| CDU/CSU    | 32.9           | 246       |
| SPD        | 20.5           | 153       |
| AfD        | 12.6           | 94        |
| FDP        | 10.7           | 80        |
| The Left   | 9.2            | 69        |
| The Greens | 8.9            | 67        |

The CDU/CSU recorded its **worst result since 1949**, while the SPD also posted its lowest vote share in the postwar period. The most dramatic development was the rise of the **AfD**, which capitalized on anti-immigration sentiment and Euroscepticism to become the **third-largest party** and the first far-right faction to enter the Bundestag in over half a century.

The **FDP** returned to parliament after being excluded in 2013, while **The Left** and **The Greens** maintained steady support.

---

### Coalition Negotiations and Government Formation

The results ruled out another **grand coalition** between the CDU/CSU and SPD in the short term, as the SPD initially announced it would enter opposition. Merkel then attempted to form a so-called "**Jamaica coalition**" with the FDP and the Greens, named after the parties’ colors. However, negotiations collapsed in November 2017 when the FDP withdrew, citing irreconcilable differences.

After renewed talks and pressure from President **Frank-Walter Steinmeier**, the SPD agreed to reopen negotiations with the CDU/CSU. A new **grand coalition** was formed in early 2018, and Merkel was re-elected as chancellor on **March 14, 2018**.

---

### Political Significance

The **2017 German federal election** signaled the end of the traditional two-party dominance in Germany and the beginning of a more fragmented and polarized political landscape. The entry of the AfD into parliament reflected rising populist and nationalist sentiment within parts of the electorate, challenging Germany’s postwar political norms.

At the same time, the election reaffirmed Angela Merkel’s role as a key figure in global diplomacy, though her weakened mandate foreshadowed the eventual end of her political career. The election also underscored growing demands for generational change and policy innovation within both major parties, ultimately setting the stage for a broader reconfiguration of German politics in the years that followed.
