**Republika Srpska**

**Overview**
Republika Srpska (Serbian Cyrillic: Република Српска; “Serb Republic”) is one of two constitutional and legal entities of Bosnia and Herzegovina, the other being the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Established by the General Framework Agreement for Peace (Dayton Peace Agreement) in December 1995, it covers approximately 49 percent of the country’s territory and is home to a predominantly ethnic Serb population. Its capital is Banja Luka, and its official languages are Serbian, Bosnian, and Croatian, all written in both Cyrillic and Latin scripts.

**Historical Background**
The territory of today’s Republika Srpska has been part of various political entities: medieval Serbian principalities; the Ottoman Vilayet of Bosnia (15th–19th centuries), which introduced Islam alongside existing Orthodoxy and Catholicism; the Austro-Hungarian Condominium (1878–1918), which modernized administration and infrastructure; and the Kingdom of Yugoslavia (1918–1941), followed by the socialist federation of post-World War II Yugoslavia (1945–1992). Under communist rule, the Socialist Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina recognized Serbs, Croats, and Muslims (Bosniaks) as constituent “nations,” but centralized governance limited regional autonomy.

**Bosnian War and Creation**
In the dissolution of Yugoslavia, Bosnia and Herzegovina’s 1992 independence referendum—boycotted by many Bosnian Serbs—was followed by the outbreak of war. Bosnian Serb leaders declared the “Serb Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina” on 9 January 1992, claiming areas with Serb majorities. Through military campaigns, sieges (notably Sarajevo), and ethnic cleansing, Serb forces seized roughly 70 percent of Bosnia’s land. In November 1995, the Dayton Peace Agreement ended hostilities and reconstituted Bosnia and Herzegovina as a single state composed of two entities: Republika Srpska and the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Republika Srpska inherited the wartime Serb-controlled territory under its internationally recognized boundaries.

**Constitutional Status and Governance**
Republika Srpska’s entity-level constitution establishes a parliamentary-presidential system. The highest office is the President of Republika Srpska, elected by popular vote for a four-year term, alongside a National Assembly (Narodna skupština) composed of 83 deputies. Executive authority lies with a Council of Ministers, led by a Prime Minister nominated by the president and confirmed by the assembly. Republika Srpska retains competencies over internal affairs—such as education, healthcare, policing, and local economic development—while foreign policy, defense, monetary policy, and customs are under the central state government of Bosnia and Herzegovina.

**Demographics**
According to the 2013 census, Republika Srpska’s population was approximately 1.2 million, of whom around 81 percent identify as Serbs, 13 percent as Bosniaks, 3 percent as Croats, and the remainder as others or undeclared. The entity’s population declined during and after the war due to displacement, emigration, and lower birth rates. Banja Luka is the largest urban center, with significant regional hubs in Bijeljina, Prijedor, and Trebinje.

**Political Landscape**
Political life in Republika Srpska is dominated by parties that explicitly represent ethnic Serb interests, most notably the Alliance of Independent Social Democrats (SNSD), which has held the presidency and prime ministership for much of the post-Dayton period. Other parties include the Serbian Democratic Party (SDS), the Party of Democratic Progress (PDP), and smaller Bosniak and Croat caucus groups. Power-sharing arrangements at the state level require Republika Srpska to participate in a tripartite presidency and in joint institutions, but political tensions often arise over interpretations of entity versus state competencies.

**Economy and Infrastructure**
Republika Srpska’s economy is diversified across agriculture, manufacturing, energy, and services. Key industries include timber, metals, petroleum processing, and food production. Post-war reconstruction and privatization have attracted foreign investment, particularly in banking and telecommunications. Infrastructure projects have focused on road and railway modernization, hydroelectric power development, and cross-entity connectivity. Nonetheless, unemployment—especially among youth—and rural underdevelopment remain challenges.

**Culture and Education**
Cultural policy emphasizes the preservation of Serbian heritage, Orthodox Christian traditions, and the Serbian language. Institutions such as the Republic Institute for Protection of Cultural, Historical, and Natural Heritage oversee restoration of religious and historical monuments damaged during the war. Republika Srpska University in Banja Luka, established in 1992, coordinates higher education across the entity. Primary and secondary schools follow curricula set by the entity ministry of education, with parallel systems in the Federation reflecting Bosnia and Herzegovina’s complex administrative structure.

**Inter-Entity Relations and Challenges**
Relations between Republika Srpska and the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina—and between Republika Srpska and central state institutions—are shaped by ongoing debates over constitutional reform, especially regarding fiscal federalism, judicial appointments, and the role of the Office of the High Representative. Republika Srpska’s leadership periodically threatens secession, citing alleged encroachments on entity autonomy. These tensions are compounded by external influences, notably political and economic ties with Serbia proper, and periodic interference by international actors aiming to preserve Bosnia and Herzegovina’s territorial integrity.

**International Engagement**
Republika Srpska participates in international cooperation through entity-level offices in Brussels and Istanbul, focusing on economic development, cultural exchange, and diaspora relations. While only Bosnia and Herzegovina holds UN and EU membership, Republika Srpska’s representatives engage with European integration initiatives, such as regional development funds and cross-border projects with Serbia, Croatia, and Montenegro.

**Legacy and Outlook**
As one of two entities within Bosnia and Herzegovina, Republika Srpska embodies both the achievements of peace-building and the enduring legacies of wartime division. Its future hinges on balancing entity autonomy with effective participation in state-level reforms required for EU accession, sustainable economic growth, and reconciliation among Bosnia and Herzegovina’s constituent peoples.
