**Cold War (1962–1979)**

The period from **1962 to 1979** represents a pivotal phase in the Cold War, characterized by both heightened global competition and efforts at strategic stability between the **United States** and the **Soviet Union**. Following the near-catastrophic **Cuban Missile Crisis** in 1962, both superpowers became increasingly aware of the dangers of nuclear escalation, leading to a series of arms control agreements and a general policy of détente during the 1970s. However, this era also witnessed the intensification of **proxy wars**, the assertion of independence by countries in the developing world, and the eventual unraveling of détente by the end of the 1970s.

---

### Aftermath of the Cuban Missile Crisis

The **Cuban Missile Crisis** in October 1962 marked the closest the Cold War came to direct nuclear conflict. In its aftermath, both Washington and Moscow sought to reduce the risk of accidental war. The **"hotline"** between the White House and the Kremlin was established in 1963 to improve crisis communication. The **Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty** (1963) banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, outer space, and underwater, reflecting a mutual desire to reduce public fear and environmental damage from nuclear fallout.

Despite these developments, the underlying ideological and strategic rivalry continued to shape global events.

---

### Vietnam War and U.S. Containment Strategy

One of the most significant Cold War conflicts during this period was the **Vietnam War**. The United States escalated its military involvement in **South Vietnam** in the mid-1960s to prevent the spread of communism, believing in the **domino theory**—that a communist victory in one country would lead to the fall of others in the region.

The war became a protracted and divisive conflict. **North Vietnam**, supported by the Soviet Union and China, ultimately achieved victory in **1975** with the fall of **Saigon**, resulting in the reunification of Vietnam under communist control. The U.S. withdrawal, combined with the collapse of the South Vietnamese government, was widely viewed as a strategic setback for American foreign policy.

The Vietnam War also catalyzed domestic opposition to U.S. Cold War interventions and fueled skepticism toward military and intelligence institutions.

---

### Sino-Soviet Split and Changing Alliances

During the 1960s, a major realignment occurred within the communist bloc with the **Sino-Soviet Split**. Ideological differences and geopolitical rivalry between the **People’s Republic of China** and the **Soviet Union** led to open hostility, culminating in border clashes in 1969.

This division weakened the cohesion of global communism and created opportunities for the United States to exploit the rivalry. In 1972, U.S. President **Richard Nixon** made a historic visit to China, initiating a process of normalization of relations. The move was part of a broader strategy to counterbalance Soviet power and reduce global Cold War tensions.

---

### Détente and Arms Control

The 1970s saw the emergence of **détente**, a policy aimed at easing tensions between the U.S. and the Soviet Union through diplomacy and arms control. Both sides pursued agreements to limit the nuclear arms race and promote cooperation.

Key milestones of détente included:

* **Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I)**: Signed in **1972**, this agreement imposed limits on the number of intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) and submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs).
* **Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty**: Also signed in 1972, this treaty restricted the deployment of missile defense systems, aiming to preserve the doctrine of mutual deterrence.
* **Helsinki Accords (1975)**: A multilateral agreement signed by 35 countries, including the U.S. and USSR, which recognized European borders and included commitments to human rights and economic cooperation.

Détente reflected a pragmatic acceptance of the balance of power and an attempt to manage rather than eliminate Cold War competition.

---

### Proxy Conflicts and the Developing World

Despite détente at the superpower level, Cold War rivalry continued to manifest in **proxy wars** and ideological competition in the **Third World**. The U.S. and the USSR provided military, economic, and political support to client states and movements aligned with their respective ideologies.

Key flashpoints during this period included:

* **Chile (1973)**: The U.S. supported the coup that overthrew socialist president **Salvador Allende**, replacing him with the military dictatorship of **Augusto Pinochet**.
* **Angola and Mozambique**: After Portuguese decolonization, both countries became battlegrounds for competing Cold War interests, with the U.S. and South Africa supporting anti-communist factions and the Soviets and Cubans backing Marxist governments.
* **Horn of Africa (1977–1978)**: The **Ogaden War** between **Somalia** and **Ethiopia** saw the U.S. and Soviet Union switch allegiances based on strategic calculations, highlighting the fluidity of alliances in the Global South.

These conflicts demonstrated that while direct superpower confrontation was avoided, the Cold War remained very much alive in the postcolonial world.

---

### End of Détente and Soviet Invasion of Afghanistan

By the late 1970s, détente began to unravel. Critics in the U.S. argued that the Soviet Union was using détente to expand its influence without meaningful concessions. Human rights concerns, particularly over Soviet treatment of dissidents and Jewish emigration, became increasingly prominent under the advocacy of groups within the U.S. and Europe.

The **Soviet invasion of Afghanistan** in **December 1979** marked a definitive end to the détente era. The intervention, intended to support a faltering communist government in Kabul, was perceived in the West as aggressive expansionism. The U.S., under President **Jimmy Carter**, responded with economic sanctions, a boycott of the **1980 Moscow Olympics**, and increased support for Afghan mujahideen fighters.

---

### Conclusion

The period from **1962 to 1979** was marked by a complex interplay of **strategic restraint and ideological conflict**. While superpowers engaged in arms control and diplomacy, the Cold War expanded geographically through proxy conflicts in Asia, Africa, and Latin America. The end of this period, punctuated by the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, signaled a return to heightened confrontation and set the stage for renewed Cold War tensions in the 1980s.
