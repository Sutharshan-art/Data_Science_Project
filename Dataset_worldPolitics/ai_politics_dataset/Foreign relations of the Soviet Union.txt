**Foreign Relations of the Soviet Union**

The foreign relations of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) were a central instrument of its communist ideology and great-power ambitions from its founding in 1922 until its dissolution in 1991. Shaped by revolutionary objectives, realpolitik, and superpower rivalry, Soviet diplomacy evolved through phases of ideological export, wartime alliance, Cold War confrontation, détente, and eventual rapprochement, leaving a profound impact on international affairs.

### Early Soviet Diplomacy and Ideological Export (1917–1939)

After the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution, Soviet Russia was diplomatically isolated by the Entente powers, prompting the young regime to establish the **People’s Commissariat for Foreign Affairs** in 1918. The 1920 Treaty of Tartu with Finland opened its first bilateral relations, followed by peace treaties with Poland (1921) and Turkey (1921). In 1922, the USSR was formally established, inheriting the **Comintern** (Communist International) as a tool for supporting communist movements abroad. Soviet foreign policy combined recognition of de jure sovereignty with clandestine backing for labor strikes and communist parties, aiming to trigger world revolution while simultaneously securing diplomatic ties with pragmatic states like Germany (1922 Treaty of Rapallo).

### World War II and the Grand Alliance (1939–1945)

The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact (1939) temporarily aligned the USSR with Nazi Germany, enabling the Soviet annexation of eastern Poland, the Baltic states, and parts of Romania. German betrayal in 1941 propelled the USSR into the **Grand Alliance** with Britain and the United States. Soviet diplomacy during World War II was characterized by high-stakes conferences at Tehran (1943) and Yalta (1945), where Joseph Stalin negotiated the post-war division of Europe. The USSR emerged as one of the two superpowers, establishing a sphere of influence in Eastern Europe through the creation of communist “people’s republics.”

### Cold War and Bloc Formation (1947–1962)

From 1947, Soviet foreign relations were dominated by the **Cold War** confrontation with the United States and its allies. The 1949 formation of the **North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO)** prompted the USSR to consolidate its own military bloc, the **Warsaw Pact** (1955). Through the **Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (Comecon)** (1949), the USSR engineered economic integration of Eastern Bloc countries. Soviet diplomacy reinforced ideological solidarity among socialist states, intervened militarily in Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968) to suppress liberalization, and provided extensive aid and advisers to revolutionary movements in Asia, Africa, and Latin America.

### Relations with the Non-Aligned and Developing World

Rejecting outright isolation of developing nations, the USSR cultivated ties with newly independent states during the decolonization wave of the 1950s and 1960s. Leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru (India), Gamal Abdel Nasser (Egypt), and Kwame Nkrumah (Ghana) received Soviet economic and technical assistance in exchange for political alignment. The USSR’s support for national liberation movements—ranging from the Viet Minh in Indochina to the MPLA in Angola—aimed to expand socialist influence and secure strategic footholds, often bringing Moscow into proxy conflicts with Western powers.

### Détente and Arms Control (1962–1979)

Following the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962), the superpowers pursued **détente**, a period of reduced tensions. Key achievements included the **Nuclear Test Ban Treaty** (1963), the **Non-Proliferation Treaty** (1968), and the **Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I)** agreement (1972). The USSR joined the **Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE)** through the Helsinki Final Act (1975), committing to respect human rights in exchange for recognition of post-war borders. Soviet foreign policy during détente balanced arms-control commitments with continued support for client states and surveillance of Western political developments.

### Renewed Confrontation and Afghan Intervention (1979–1985)

The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 marked the end of détente and a return to Cold War hostility. Western condemnation led to U.S. sanctions, the boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics, and renewed military competition. The USSR’s Afghan intervention, justified as support for a socialist government, embroiled it in a costly counter-insurgency against U.S.-backed mujahideen, draining resources and tarnishing its international image.

### Perestroika, Glasnost, and Rapprochement (1985–1991)

Under Mikhail Gorbachev’s leadership (1985–1991), Soviet foreign policy shifted dramatically. **Glasnost** (openness) and **perestroika** (restructuring) accompanied a doctrine of **“new thinking”** that emphasized common security over ideological rivalry. Gorbachev withdrew troops from Afghanistan (1989), accepted arms-reduction treaties such as **INF** (1987) and **START I** (1991), and facilitated the peaceful reunification of Germany (1990). Soviet support for reform movements contributed to the collapse of communist regimes across Eastern Europe in 1989. Gorbachev’s engagement with Presidents Reagan and Bush fostered the end of the Cold War and a rapprochement with the West.

### Dissolution and Legacy (1991)

Economic strains and nationalist movements within the USSR culminated in its formal dissolution on 26 December 1991. The newly independent **Russian Federation**, under Boris Yeltsin, assumed the USSR’s United Nations seat, nuclear arsenal, and treaty obligations. Soviet foreign relations left a mixed legacy: the establishment of the bipolar world order and its detente-era arms treaties, contrasted with the repression of Eastern Europe and costly foreign interventions. The Soviet diplomatic record continues to inform Russia’s contemporary foreign policy and global perceptions of superpower engagement.

---

Throughout its existence, the Soviet Union’s foreign relations were characterized by a tension between revolutionary idealism and strategic pragmatism, shaping landmark developments in international law, alliance systems, and the global distribution of power. Its rise and fall underscore the dynamic interplay between ideology, security, and economic capacity in great-power diplomacy.
