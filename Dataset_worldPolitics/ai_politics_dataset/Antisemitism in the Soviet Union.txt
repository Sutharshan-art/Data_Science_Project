**Antisemitism in the Soviet Union**

Antisemitism in the **Soviet Union** refers to the systemic and societal discrimination, hostility, and persecution directed against Jewish people within the USSR from its establishment in 1922 until its dissolution in 1991. Although the Soviet state officially condemned racism and ethnic discrimination, antisemitic attitudes and policies persisted in various forms, influenced by political, ideological, and social factors. The complex relationship between Soviet ideology, nationalism, and Jewish identity shaped the experiences of Soviet Jews across different historical periods.

---

### Historical and Political Context

The Soviet Union was formed in the aftermath of the **Russian Revolution of 1917**, during a period marked by widespread social upheaval and ethnic tensions. Pre-revolutionary Russia had a long history of antisemitism, including **pogroms** and legal restrictions against Jewish communities. The Bolsheviks officially rejected antisemitism, emphasizing internationalism and class solidarity over ethnic divisions.

However, the Soviet approach to Jewish identity was complicated by the regime’s suspicion of all religious and national groups perceived as potentially disloyal or bourgeois. The Soviet state promoted atheism and sought to suppress religious institutions, including Jewish religious life.

---

### Early Soviet Period (1920s–1930s)

In the 1920s, the Soviet government established the **Jewish Autonomous Oblast** in the Russian Far East as part of its nationality policy, encouraging Jewish cultural autonomy within a socialist framework. Yiddish culture was promoted while Hebrew and Zionism were banned, as Zionism was viewed as nationalist and counter-revolutionary.

Despite official condemnation of antisemitism, Jews faced increasing repression during **Joseph Stalin’s** rule. The late 1930s saw purges that disproportionately affected Jewish intellectuals, party members, and cultural figures. State propaganda sometimes invoked antisemitic tropes, especially linking Jews to alleged “cosmopolitanism” and “rootlessness,” which conflicted with Soviet nationalist narratives.

---

### World War II and the Holocaust

During **World War II**, the Soviet Union was invaded by Nazi Germany, which carried out the genocide of millions of Jews in the territories it occupied. The Holocaust devastated the Soviet Jewish population.

The Soviet government initially downplayed the specifically Jewish nature of Nazi atrocities, referring broadly to Soviet citizens suffering under fascism. After the war, Soviet Jews faced both recognition as victims and suspicion, particularly as Stalin’s regime grew increasingly paranoid about “Zionist” influence and loyalty to the state.

---

### Postwar Antisemitism (Late 1940s–1950s)

The late Stalin era witnessed a marked intensification of antisemitism within the Soviet state. Campaigns such as the **"anti-cosmopolitan"** purge targeted Jewish intellectuals and professionals accused of disloyalty or ties to the West. The infamous **Doctors’ Plot** of 1952–1953 alleged a Jewish conspiracy to harm Soviet leaders, fueling widespread fear and discrimination.

Although Stalin’s death in 1953 ended the most overt state-sponsored antisemitic campaigns, structural discrimination against Jews continued. Restrictions on Jewish cultural and religious expression persisted, and Jews were often excluded from sensitive government and military positions.

---

### The Khrushchev and Brezhnev Periods (1953–1980s)

Under **Nikita Khrushchev** and later **Leonid Brezhnev**, antisemitism remained a significant factor in Soviet policy and society. The state continued to restrict Jewish religious activities, Hebrew language education, and Jewish emigration.

Jewish citizens frequently faced barriers in education and employment, particularly in prestigious universities and scientific institutions. The Soviet government justified many of these restrictions by alleging Jewish “Zionist” sympathies or connections to capitalist countries, framing Jewish identity as potentially subversive.

Despite these challenges, a vibrant underground Jewish cultural and religious life persisted, and international pressure increased in favor of Soviet Jews’ right to emigrate, particularly to Israel and the United States.

---

### Emigration and the Refusenik Movement

From the late 1960s through the 1980s, thousands of Soviet Jews sought to leave the USSR, often facing official denial of exit visas and persecution. Those denied permission became known as **refuseniks**, and many activists publicly protested discrimination and human rights abuses.

The Soviet refusal to permit Jewish emigration became a major point of contention in East-West relations during the Cold War. Western governments, human rights organizations, and Jewish communities worldwide campaigned vigorously for the rights of Soviet Jews, framing their struggle within the broader context of political and religious freedom.

---

### Perestroika and the End of the Soviet Union

With **Mikhail Gorbachev’s** policies of **glasnost** and **perestroika** in the late 1980s, restrictions on Jewish cultural expression and emigration were relaxed. Large numbers of Soviet Jews emigrated, and Jewish organizations began to operate openly.

The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 marked a significant turning point, allowing for the revival of Jewish religious, cultural, and political life in the newly independent states. However, antisemitic attitudes and incidents persisted in various forms within post-Soviet societies.

---

### Legacy and Contemporary Issues

Antisemitism in the Soviet Union was a complex phenomenon shaped by the interplay of ideology, nationalism, and political control. While the regime officially denounced racism and promoted internationalism, its policies often institutionalized discrimination against Jews.

The Soviet experience of antisemitism has influenced contemporary discussions about ethnic relations, minority rights, and historical memory in Eastern Europe and the former Soviet republics. It also remains a reference point in global dialogues about human rights and religious freedom.

---

### Conclusion

Antisemitism in the Soviet Union reflected contradictions between official ideology and practice, marked by episodes of intense repression and structural discrimination. The experiences of Soviet Jews during this period highlight the challenges faced by minority groups under authoritarian regimes and the enduring impact of state policies on ethnic and religious identities.
