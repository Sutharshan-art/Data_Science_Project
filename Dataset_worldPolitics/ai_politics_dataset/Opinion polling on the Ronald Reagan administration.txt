**Opinion Polling on the Ronald Reagan Administration**

Public opinion polling on the administration of Ronald Reagan, the 40th president of the United States (1981–1989), provides insight into American political attitudes during the 1980s. Reagan’s presidency was marked by significant shifts in economic, foreign, and domestic policy, and public support for his administration fluctuated in response to major events such as the recession of the early 1980s, economic recovery, Cold War diplomacy, and the Iran-Contra affair. Overall, Reagan maintained relatively high levels of public approval throughout his two terms, with his popularity peaking during periods of economic optimism and perceived foreign policy success.

## Early Approval and Economic Challenges (1981–1983)

Ronald Reagan entered office in January 1981 with high public expectations following a landslide victory over incumbent President Jimmy Carter. Early polling in 1981 showed Reagan’s approval rating around 60%, bolstered by strong public support for his anti-inflationary economic agenda, often referred to as "Reaganomics." However, his approval rating declined sharply as the U.S. economy entered a deep recession in 1981–1982.

High unemployment, reaching over 10% by late 1982, and controversial budget cuts contributed to a drop in Reagan’s approval rating to the low 40s. Many Americans expressed skepticism about his tax policies and reductions in social welfare programs. Despite these setbacks, Reagan remained personally popular with segments of the electorate who viewed him as a strong and principled leader.

## Economic Recovery and Rising Popularity (1983–1985)

As the economy began to recover in 1983, Reagan’s approval rating rebounded significantly. Strong GDP growth, declining inflation, and falling unemployment rates helped restore public confidence. By mid-1984, his approval rating had risen above 60%, and his re-election campaign benefited from a positive economic outlook captured in the slogan “It’s morning again in America.”

Reagan’s decisive victory in the 1984 presidential election, winning 49 of 50 states against Democratic challenger Walter Mondale, reflected broad public support. Exit polls indicated that economic recovery and a strong national identity were decisive factors, particularly among independent voters and so-called “Reagan Democrats.”

## Foreign Policy and Public Opinion

Reagan’s foreign policy also shaped public opinion. His aggressive rhetoric toward the Soviet Union in the early 1980s, including calling it the “Evil Empire,” polarized the American public. Supporters viewed him as a firm defender of Western values, while critics feared an escalation of the Cold War. The 1983 U.S. invasion of Grenada received majority approval, with polls showing support around 60%, reinforcing perceptions of Reagan as a decisive commander-in-chief.

Public skepticism arose around his handling of the 1983 Beirut barracks bombing, where over 200 U.S. Marines were killed. His decision to withdraw American forces from Lebanon was controversial, but did not significantly damage his long-term approval.

Reagan's second term witnessed a shift in public perception of his foreign policy, particularly following improved U.S.–Soviet relations. His summits with Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, and eventual signing of the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty in 1987, were viewed positively. Polling in the late 1980s showed growing public support for arms control and détente, aligning with Reagan’s diplomatic turn.

## Iran-Contra Affair and Declining Trust (1986–1987)

The Iran-Contra affair, revealed in late 1986, became the most serious political scandal of Reagan’s presidency. The administration’s covert sale of arms to Iran, and the illegal diversion of funds to support Contra rebels in Nicaragua, triggered widespread criticism.

Reagan’s approval rating fell from over 60% to the mid-40s in early 1987. Congressional hearings and media investigations raised doubts about Reagan’s awareness and leadership. However, public opinion was divided: while many disapproved of the administration’s actions, Reagan personally retained significant goodwill, with polls suggesting the public was willing to give him the benefit of the doubt. His affable demeanor and consistent messaging helped contain long-term political damage.

## Final Years and Legacy in Public Opinion

By the end of his second term in January 1989, Reagan’s approval rating had stabilized in the mid-60s. His farewell address and reflections on the achievements of the 1980s contributed to a favorable public image. Polls indicated that a majority of Americans credited him with restoring national confidence, ending the Cold War on favorable terms, and reviving the U.S. economy.

In retrospective polling, Reagan’s legacy has remained strong among Republican and independent voters. Gallup and other longitudinal studies often rank him among the most admired U.S. presidents. However, opinions remain divided on the long-term impact of his economic policies, particularly concerning wealth inequality and federal deficits.

## Conclusion

Opinion polling throughout Ronald Reagan’s presidency reflected the political and social complexities of the 1980s. While his administration faced economic downturns and political scandal, Reagan consistently maintained a strong base of support. His ability to connect with the public, adapt his rhetoric, and project optimism contributed to sustained approval, shaping his enduring reputation in American political history.
