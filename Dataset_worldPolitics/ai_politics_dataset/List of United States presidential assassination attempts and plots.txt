**List of United States Presidential Assassination Attempts and Plots**

The **list of United States presidential assassination attempts and plots** documents the known efforts—both successful and unsuccessful—to kill sitting U.S. presidents, as well as planned attacks that were thwarted or failed to materialize. These incidents span the history of the presidency, reflecting moments of political extremism, personal grievance, mental illness, and broader societal unrest.

Since the founding of the United States, **four presidents have been assassinated**, and several others have survived attempts on their lives. The threat to the presidency has shaped the development of modern security protocols, particularly the role of the **United States Secret Service**, which assumed formal responsibility for presidential protection in 1901 following the assassination of President William McKinley.

This list highlights both successful assassinations and credible plots or attempts, offering insight into the political climate of different eras and the evolving challenges of safeguarding the nation’s highest office.

---

### Historical and Political Context

Presidential assassination attempts often reflect deep divisions in American society. From early partisan conflicts and civil strife to Cold War tensions and domestic terrorism, many attackers have been motivated by political ideologies, opposition to specific policies, or personal delusions. In some cases, foreign influence or broader ideological movements played a role.

The assassination of a president has profound implications not only for national governance but also for public morale and the continuity of democratic institutions. Consequently, the federal government has developed layered security mechanisms to protect the executive, including legal penalties for threatening the president and expanded intelligence coordination for detecting threats.

### Successful Assassinations

1. **Abraham Lincoln** – April 14, 1865
   **Assassin:** John Wilkes Booth
   **Context:** Killed at Ford’s Theatre just days after the Confederate surrender in the Civil War. Booth, a Confederate sympathizer, believed he was striking a blow against Union tyranny.

2. **James A. Garfield** – July 2, 1881
   **Assassin:** Charles J. Guiteau
   **Context:** Guiteau, a disgruntled office seeker, shot Garfield in a Washington, D.C., train station. Garfield died of complications two months later.

3. **William McKinley** – September 6, 1901
   **Assassin:** Leon Czolgosz
   **Context:** An anarchist opposed to U.S. imperialism, Czolgosz shot McKinley at the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, New York.

4. **John F. Kennedy** – November 22, 1963
   **Assassin:** Lee Harvey Oswald (official account)
   **Context:** Kennedy was shot while riding in a motorcade in Dallas, Texas. The circumstances of his death remain the subject of public debate and conspiracy theories.

### Unsuccessful Assassination Attempts and Plots

#### **Andrew Jackson** – January 30, 1835

**Assailant:** Richard Lawrence
**Method:** Pistols (both misfired)
**Context:** The first recorded attempt on a president. Jackson subdued the mentally ill attacker himself.

#### **Theodore Roosevelt** – October 14, 1912 (after presidency)

**Assailant:** John Schrank
**Method:** Gunshot
**Context:** Shot while campaigning for a third term on the Progressive Party ticket. Roosevelt survived and continued his speech with the bullet still in his chest.

#### **Harry S. Truman** – November 1, 1950

**Assailants:** Oscar Collazo and Griselio Torresola
**Method:** Armed attack at Blair House
**Context:** Puerto Rican nationalists attempted to assassinate Truman during a campaign for Puerto Rican independence. One assailant and one police officer were killed.

#### **Richard Nixon** – 1972 (bomb plot)

**Plotter:** Arthur Bremer (initial target before attempting to assassinate George Wallace)
**Context:** Bremer’s diary suggests Nixon was considered as a target, although no attempt was made.

#### **Gerald Ford** – September 1975 (two attempts)

* **Lynette “Squeaky” Fromme** (September 5): A member of the Manson Family; pistol did not fire.
* **Sara Jane Moore** (September 22): Fired one shot, missed; quickly subdued.

#### **Jimmy Carter** – May 1979

**Plotter:** Raymond Lee Harvey
**Context:** Claimed he was part of a four-man plot to kill Carter; the evidence was inconclusive, and he was later found mentally unfit.

#### **Ronald Reagan** – March 30, 1981

**Assailant:** John Hinckley Jr.
**Method:** Gunshot
**Context:** Reagan was seriously wounded outside a Washington, D.C., hotel. The attack also injured three others, including Press Secretary James Brady. Hinckley was found not guilty by reason of insanity.

#### **George H. W. Bush** – April 1993 (foiled plot)

**Plotter:** Alleged Iraqi intelligence agents
**Context:** A failed car bomb attempt during a visit to Kuwait, allegedly in retaliation for the Gulf War. The U.S. retaliated with a missile strike.

#### **George W. Bush** – May 2005

**Assailant:** Vladimir Arutyunian
**Method:** Grenade (failed to detonate)
**Context:** During a speech in Tbilisi, Georgia. The device was thrown near the stage but did not explode.

#### **Barack Obama** – Multiple threats (2008–2016)

**Context:** Obama, as the first African American president, received an unusually high number of threats. Several individuals were arrested for plotting or making direct threats against him. Most were intercepted before posing an immediate risk.

### Security and Legal Measures

In response to recurring threats, Congress expanded the Secret Service’s role and increased criminal penalties for threatening or attempting to harm the president. Presidential motorcades, travel routes, and public appearances are now tightly controlled, and advanced technologies (including surveillance, counter-sniper teams, and encrypted communications) are employed to mitigate risk.

The **Presidential Threat Protection Act of 2000** further authorized enhanced capabilities for threat assessment, while reforms following the Kennedy and Reagan shootings led to better coordination between local and federal agencies.

### Conclusion

The **list of United States presidential assassination attempts and plots** underscores the persistent vulnerabilities faced by the office and the individuals who occupy it. These incidents—whether driven by ideology, delusion, or desperation—reflect deeper tensions within American political culture and society. They also illustrate the evolving challenge of protecting democratic institutions while maintaining open and accessible governance.
