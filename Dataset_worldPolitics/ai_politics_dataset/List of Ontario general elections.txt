**List of Ontario General Elections**

The list of Ontario general elections provides a chronological overview of the provincial elections held in Ontario, Canada, to elect members to the Legislative Assembly of Ontario. Since Confederation in 1867, Ontario has conducted regular general elections, typically every four years, although the frequency has varied due to political and constitutional factors. These elections determine the composition of the provincial legislature and, in turn, the party or coalition that forms the government.

The elections have played a central role in shaping the province’s political landscape, reflecting shifting public attitudes, economic conditions, and demographic changes. Over time, the electoral process and party system in Ontario have undergone significant evolution, transitioning from a limited franchise and a two-party system to a modern, pluralistic democracy with universal suffrage.

### Early Elections (1867–1905)

The first general election in Ontario was held on September 3, 1867, shortly after Canadian Confederation. It resulted in a victory for the Ontario Liberal Party, led by John Sandfield Macdonald, who became the province’s first premier. Initially, the political landscape in Ontario was dominated by the Liberals and the Conservatives, with governments alternating between the two.

During this early period, elections were often influenced by issues such as education, railway development, and relations with the federal government. Voting rights were limited to male property owners, and electoral practices such as open balloting were common until the introduction of the secret ballot in 1874.

The Liberals, under Oliver Mowat, maintained power for nearly 24 years (1872–1896), marking one of the longest unbroken tenures in Ontario's political history. Mowat’s premiership significantly expanded provincial rights within the Canadian federation, and his dominance in elections was a reflection of Liberal strength in rural Ontario.

### Conservative Ascendancy and Reform (1905–1943)

The early 20th century saw the rise of the Ontario Conservative Party, led by figures such as James P. Whitney and later Howard Ferguson. The Conservatives won the 1905 election and governed for the majority of the period until the early 1940s, promoting industrial development, hydroelectric power, and temperance legislation.

The 1919 election was a notable deviation from the two-party pattern. The United Farmers of Ontario, a new agrarian movement, won a surprise victory and formed a short-lived government under Premier E.C. Drury. Their success reflected growing dissatisfaction among rural voters and the impact of post-World War I social and economic pressures.

The onset of the Great Depression and public concern over economic mismanagement contributed to political instability. The Liberals briefly regained power under Mitchell Hepburn in 1934, capitalizing on public discontent and promising economic reform.

### The Progressive Conservative Era (1943–1985)

One of the most defining periods in Ontario’s electoral history was the dominance of the Progressive Conservative Party (PC), which held power continuously from 1943 to 1985. The PCs, under premiers such as George Drew, Leslie Frost, John Robarts, and Bill Davis, maintained a centrist and pragmatic approach to governance, often described as “Red Toryism.”

The PCs won successive elections by appealing to a broad coalition of voters across urban and rural constituencies. This era was marked by expansion of public education, infrastructure development, and the creation of Ontario Hydro as a major public utility. Ontario’s electoral system remained first-past-the-post, and voter turnout remained relatively high during this time.

Despite challenges from the Liberals and the New Democratic Party (NDP), the PCs maintained a steady grip on power through effective organization and moderate policymaking. However, by the early 1980s, growing dissatisfaction and economic recession eroded their support.

### Liberal Resurgence and NDP Breakthrough (1985–1995)

In the 1985 general election, the Progressive Conservatives lost their majority, and the Liberals, led by David Peterson, formed a minority government with the support of the NDP. This marked the beginning of a more competitive multi-party era in Ontario politics.

The Liberals won a majority in 1987 but were defeated in 1990 by the NDP under Bob Rae, who formed the first NDP government in Ontario history. The 1990 election was influenced by a public desire for change, though the Rae government later faced criticism during the economic downturn of the early 1990s.

### Progressive Conservative Return and Liberal Dominance (1995–2018)

The Progressive Conservatives returned to power in the 1995 election under Mike Harris, who introduced a platform known as the "Common Sense Revolution." This era was characterized by tax cuts, welfare reform, and reductions in public spending.

The Liberals regained power in 2003 under Dalton McGuinty and remained in office for 15 years, continuing under Kathleen Wynne. This period saw increased investment in health care and education, as well as progressive social reforms such as the legalization of same-sex marriage and a push for green energy.

### Recent Developments (2018–Present)

In the 2018 general election, the Progressive Conservatives, led by Doug Ford, won a majority government, ending 15 years of Liberal rule. The election was marked by widespread voter dissatisfaction with the Liberals, particularly regarding rising electricity costs and budget deficits.

Doug Ford was re-elected in the 2022 general election with another majority. His government has focused on economic growth, infrastructure development, and health care system reform. Recent elections have reflected increased polarization and the impact of populist political rhetoric, alongside issues such as the COVID-19 pandemic response and affordability concerns.

### Electoral System and Reforms

Ontario uses the first-past-the-post electoral system for general elections. Electoral boundaries are reviewed regularly to reflect population changes. The province has also experimented with fixed election dates, now typically scheduled every four years unless an early dissolution is triggered by a vote of no confidence.

Over time, the franchise has expanded to include women (1917), Indigenous peoples (1960), and all citizens over the age of 18. Voter turnout has fluctuated, declining in recent decades due to political disengagement and other social factors.

The list of Ontario general elections, beyond being a record of political change, reflects broader trends in Canadian democracy, including shifts in political ideology, institutional development, and public participation.
