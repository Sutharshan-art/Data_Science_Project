**Nikolai Ivanovich Ryzhkov** (Russian: Николай Иванович Рыжков; born 28 September 1929) is a Russian politician and former Soviet statesman who served as Chairman of the Council of Ministers of the USSR (Premier) from 27 September 1985 until 14 January 1991. A leading figure in Mikhail Gorbachev’s perestroika-era government, Ryzhkov presided over attempts to reform the Soviet economy during a period of political upheaval, and later stood as a candidate in the first Russian presidential election of 1991.

## Early Life and Career

Ryzhkov was born in the Donets Basin (Donbas) town of Dronovka (now in Ukraine) to a working-class family. After training as a metallurgical engineer at the Magnitogorsk Mining and Metallurgical Institute (graduating 1953), he rose through the ranks at the Magnitogorsk Iron and Steel Works. By the late 1960s he had joined the Communist Party and entered regional party leadership in Chelyabinsk Oblast, eventually becoming First Secretary of the regional party committee (1971–1976).

In 1976 Ryzhkov was appointed First Deputy Chairman of the State Planning Committee (Gosplan) in Moscow, where he worked on industrial investment programs and five-year plans. His technocratic expertise—and reputation for delivering production targets—caught the attention of Leonid Brezhnev’s Politburo, leading to his appointment as First Deputy Chairman of the Council of Ministers (1979–1985), effectively the Soviet Union’s number-two economic official.

## Premiership under Gorbachev (1985–1991)

### Appointment and Early Mandate

In the aftermath of Konstantin Chernenko’s death, Mikhail Gorbachev became General Secretary in March 1985 and reshuffled the government. On 27 September, Gorbachev appointed Ryzhkov Premier, entrusting him with implementing perestroika (“restructuring”) in the planned economy. Ryzhkov pledged to boost growth by modernizing industry, reducing waste, and introducing limited market mechanisms.

### Economic Reforms and Challenges

Ryzhkov’s government pursued a series of reform measures: decentralizing decision-making in state enterprises; granting factories greater autonomy over production and profits; and introducing joint ventures with foreign investors. In 1986 he oversaw the Law on State Enterprise, which loosened central controls, and the Law on Cooperatives (1988), which legalized small-scale private and cooperative businesses.

However, structural rigidities—centralized pricing, soft budget constraints, and reliance on heavy industry—hindered acceleration. Shortages of consumer goods persisted, and inflationary pressures mounted as price controls were lifted unevenly. The 1986 Chernobyl disaster, occurring under Ryzhkov’s watch, further strained the economy and exposed systemic failures in governance and crisis management.

### Political Context and Glasnost

While Ryzhkov focused on economic restructuring, Gorbachev’s policy of glasnost (“openness”) encouraged public debate and media scrutiny of official performance. Parliamentary bodies such as the Congress of People’s Deputies (established 1989) began to question the pace and depth of reforms. Ryzhkov defended the cautious, phased approach to avoid social dislocation, but critics—including radical reformers led by Yegor Gaidar—called for more rapid liberalization.

### The 500 Days Program and Resignation

In mid-1990, reformist deputies proposed the “500 Days” program to transition to a market economy within 18 months. Ryzhkov resisted this rapid timetable, insisting on preserving social guarantees and a role for state planning. In January 1991, amid growing discord, Gorbachev replaced him with Valentin Pavlov, and Ryzhkov left the premiership after more than five years in office.

## 1991 Presidential Campaign and Later Roles

Following the failed August 1991 coup, Russia held its first presidential election. Ryzhkov, running as the candidate of the conservative “Communists of Russia” bloc, campaigned on preserving social stability and state-sector employment. He won 16.9% of the vote, coming third behind Boris Yeltsin (57.3%) and Vladimir Zhirinovsky (7.8%), and subsequently endorsed Yeltsin in the runoff.

From 1993 to 2003, Ryzhkov served in the Federation Council (the upper house of the Russian parliament), holding senior positions on economic and budgetary committees. He later presided over the Russian Council of Veterans of War and Labor and chaired the advisory Russian Economic Forum, speaking frequently on fiscal policy, social welfare, and pensions.

## Political Significance and Legacy

Ryzhkov’s tenure as Premier coincided with the most turbulent decade in Soviet history. As a technocrat with deep industrial experience, he navigated between hardline conservatives wary of change and radical reformers seeking to dismantle the planned economy. His incremental approach achieved modest gains in enterprise autonomy and foreign investment but was overtaken by political liberalization and the Soviet Union’s ultimate dissolution in December 1991.

In post-Soviet Russia, Ryzhkov emerged as a prominent elder statesman advocating pension reform, economic modernization, and measured fiscal policy. His career illustrates the challenges of managing systemic economic transition under both political and institutional constraints, and his leadership remains a key case study in the limits of reform within authoritarian systems.
