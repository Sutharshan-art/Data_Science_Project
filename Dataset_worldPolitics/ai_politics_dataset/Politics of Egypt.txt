**Politics of Egypt**

The **politics of Egypt** operate within the framework of a **semi-presidential republic** governed by a strong executive, a bicameral legislature, and an independent judiciary. Since gaining independence from British influence in the mid-20th century, Egypt has experienced periods of authoritarian rule, revolutionary upheaval, and limited democratic experimentation. Political power in Egypt has traditionally been centralized in the presidency, with the military playing a dominant role in national governance.

Modern Egyptian politics have been shaped by key historical events, including the **1952 revolution**, the establishment of a republic, multiple wars with Israel, the **2011 Arab Spring**, and the **2013 military coup** that ousted the country's first democratically elected president. Throughout its contemporary history, Egypt’s political trajectory has often oscillated between reform and repression.

---

### Historical Context

Following the 1952 coup led by the **Free Officers Movement**, Egypt abolished its monarchy and declared itself a republic. **Gamal Abdel Nasser**, one of the coup’s leaders, emerged as president and introduced a populist, Arab nationalist, and socialist agenda, including land reform, nationalization of the Suez Canal, and strong state control over the economy.

Nasser’s successors, **Anwar Sadat** and later **Hosni Mubarak**, maintained Egypt's strong presidential system while gradually shifting toward economic liberalization and closer ties with the West, especially after the signing of the **1979 Egypt–Israel Peace Treaty**. Political opposition was tolerated within limits, but real power remained with the president, the military, and the ruling **National Democratic Party (NDP)**.

The **2011 Egyptian revolution**, part of the broader Arab Spring movement, led to Mubarak’s resignation after nearly 30 years in power. A transitional military government assumed control before overseeing elections in which **Mohamed Morsi**, affiliated with the **Muslim Brotherhood**, became Egypt’s first civilian, democratically elected president.

However, Morsi’s presidency faced deep polarization and widespread protests. In July 2013, the military, led by **General Abdel Fattah el-Sisi**, removed Morsi from office. El-Sisi was later elected president in 2014 and re-elected in 2018 in elections criticized by international observers for lacking fair competition.

---

### Executive Branch

The **President of Egypt** is the head of state and commander-in-chief of the armed forces. The president is elected by popular vote for a six-year term and may serve a maximum of two terms following a 2019 constitutional amendment, which also extended the presidential term from four to six years and allowed el-Sisi to remain in power potentially until 2030.

The president holds broad executive powers, including the ability to appoint the prime minister, dissolve parliament, and declare states of emergency. The **Prime Minister** acts as head of government and oversees cabinet functions but is subordinate to the president.

---

### Legislative Branch

Egypt has a **bicameral legislature** consisting of the **House of Representatives** and the **Senate** (re-established in 2020). The **House of Representatives** holds legislative authority, approves the state budget, and oversees the executive. It is composed of elected members, some of whom are appointed by the president.

The **Senate** serves in an advisory role and reviews proposed constitutional amendments, treaties, and state policy matters. Its reintroduction was part of a broader constitutional revision aimed at consolidating state institutions under presidential influence.

While nominally independent, the legislature has often functioned in support of the executive, particularly under el-Sisi, with many lawmakers aligned with pro-government coalitions.

---

### Judiciary and Rule of Law

Egypt's judiciary is nominally independent, with key institutions including the **Supreme Constitutional Court**, **Court of Cassation**, and **State Council**. Judges are appointed by the president and are tasked with ensuring the constitutionality of laws and administrative acts.

Under el-Sisi’s rule, however, the judiciary has come under increasing executive influence. Courts have played a central role in suppressing opposition, including the banning of the Muslim Brotherhood and the prosecution of dissenters, journalists, and activists under broad anti-terrorism and cybercrime laws.

---

### Political Parties and Opposition

Egypt recognizes multiple political parties, but meaningful opposition has been heavily restricted since the 2013 coup. The formerly ruling **Muslim Brotherhood** has been declared a terrorist organization, and its members face severe repression.

Legal opposition parties, such as the **Al-Dustour Party**, **Tagammu**, and the **Wafd Party**, operate under constraints and often struggle to compete effectively. Pro-government coalitions, such as **Support Egypt**, dominate the political landscape. The **National Elections Authority** oversees electoral processes, which have been criticized for lack of transparency and voter suppression.

---

### Civil Liberties and Human Rights

Human rights in Egypt have faced significant setbacks in recent years. Authorities have conducted mass arrests of protesters, political opponents, and civil society actors. Independent media outlets have been shut down or restricted, and internet censorship has increased.

International organizations, including Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International, have raised concerns about **torture**, **enforced disappearances**, and **unfair trials**, especially in national security courts. The government has defended its actions as necessary for combating terrorism and preserving national stability.

---

### Foreign Relations

Egypt plays a key role in Middle Eastern politics due to its strategic location, population, and historical influence. It maintains a peace treaty with Israel, serves as a mediator in regional conflicts, and is a major recipient of U.S. military aid.

Under el-Sisi, Egypt has deepened ties with the **Gulf states**, **Russia**, and **China**, while maintaining a cautious but functional relationship with the **United States** and **European Union**. The government presents itself as a bulwark against Islamist extremism and a stable partner in a turbulent region.

---

### Conclusion

The politics of Egypt reflect a trajectory of centralized executive control, fluctuating openness, and strong military influence. While the 2011 revolution briefly opened the door to democratic change, subsequent events re-established an authoritarian model under President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi. The current political environment is characterized by limited pluralism, suppressed dissent, and ongoing debates about the future of governance, civil liberties, and reform in the Arab world’s most populous country.
