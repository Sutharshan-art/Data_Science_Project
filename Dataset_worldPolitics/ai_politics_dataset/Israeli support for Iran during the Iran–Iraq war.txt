**Israeli Support for Iran during the Iran–Iraq War**

During the Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988), Israel—despite its official hostility toward Iran following the 1979 Islamic Revolution—provided covert assistance to Iran’s military. This unlikely alignment was driven by strategic calculations aimed at preventing an Iraqi victory that could imperil Israel’s security. Israeli support took the form of arms sales, spare parts, and intelligence sharing, often routed through third parties to evade international scrutiny and U.S. arms‐embargo restrictions.

### Historical and Geopolitical Context

After the 1979 Islamic Revolution, Iran severed diplomatic ties with Israel and adopted an anti‐Zionist rhetoric. Nonetheless, the outbreak of war in September 1980 between Iran and Iraq—then led by Saddam Hussein, whose regime maintained a state of belligerence toward Israel—prompted a realignment of strategic interests. Iran possessed large stockpiles of Western aircraft, tanks, and artillery purchased under the Shah, but these rapidly deteriorated without a reliable supply of spare parts. Meanwhile, Iraq, armed with Soviet and French weaponry and boasting a larger population, threatened to overrun Iran’s southwestern oil fields and potentially use the war to undermine Israel by destabilizing the broader Middle Eastern balance of power.

### Motivations for Covert Assistance

Israeli decision‐makers viewed a strong Iraq—flush with Gulf Arab and Soviet backing—as a greater long‐term threat than post‐revolutionary Iran. By helping Iran remain locked in a costly stalemate, Israel sought to:

* **Dilute Iraq’s military capacity**, preventing Baghdad from expanding its influence or acquiring battlefield victories that might embolden further aggression.
* **Avoid Iranian victory**, which could have unleashed a wave of revolutionary Islamist fervor hostile to Israel.
* **Strengthen ties with regional actors** indirectly, by demonstrating Israel’s willingness to counter common adversaries through covert means.

### Mechanisms of Support

Israel’s assistance to Iran was conducted almost entirely through clandestine channels. The primary methods included:

1. **Arms Transfers via Third Parties**: Israel sold U.S.‐origin weapons—such as TOW anti‐tank missiles, artillery shells, and ammunition—to the Iranian government through intermediaries in Europe and Asia. Shipments were often mislabeled or transshipped to disguise their origin.
2. **Spare Parts for Western Equipment**: Iranian F-14 Tomcats, F-4 Phantoms, and Chieftain tanks required Western parts. Israeli intelligence services brokered deals that funneled critical components to keep these platforms operational.
3. **Technical Assistance and Training**: Israeli experts provided technical advice on maintenance and battlefield tactics for weapons systems that Iran could no longer service through official supply chains.

### Coordination with the United States

While the U.S. maintained an arms embargo against Iran, senior Israeli and American officials recognized their shared interest in containing Saddam Hussein. Declassified accounts indicate that Israel occasionally informed U.S. intelligence of pending shipments to Iran, tacitly coordinating to avoid diplomatic conflicts. This covert collaboration laid groundwork for the later Iran–Contra affair, in which arms sales to Iran were explicitly linked to the release of American hostages in Lebanon and to clandestine funding of anti‐communist forces in Central America.

### Scale and Impact

Quantifying the full extent of Israeli support is difficult due to its covert nature. Estimates suggest that Israel delivered thousands of guided missiles, hundreds of tons of ammunition, and critical spare parts for several dozen F-14 aircraft. While Iranian forces suffered heavy casualties, Israeli aid contributed to Iran’s ability to sustain air operations and armored counterattacks, prolonging the conflict and enhancing Iran’s strategic resilience.

### Regional and Domestic Reactions

The revelation of Israeli‐Iranian collaboration shocked many observers, given the two countries’ official enmity. In Israel, the operation remained a closely guarded secret, known to only a handful of senior officials. Among U.S. policymakers, reactions were mixed: some viewed the coordination as a pragmatic necessity, while others feared it undermined official U.S. policy and risked exposure through political scandals. In the broader Middle East, the covert alliance contributed to narratives of foreign meddling and deepened suspicions among Arab states.

### Aftermath and Legacy

By the war’s end in 1988, Iran and Iraq had exhausted themselves in a stalemate that left both economies crippled. Israeli support, alongside Soviet and Chinese aid to Iraq, underscored the extent to which external powers used the conflict to pursue their own strategic goals. The covert Israel–Iran nexus presaged more complex regional realignments: after 1990, Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait prompted Israel to side openly with the U.S.-led coalition, while Iran continued to view Israel as an existential foe. The operation remains a prominent example of “enemy-of-my-enemy” realpolitik, illustrating how ideological adversaries can become temporary collaborators when confronting shared threats.
