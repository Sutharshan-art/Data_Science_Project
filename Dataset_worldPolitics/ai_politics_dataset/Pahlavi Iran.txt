**Pahlavi Iran**

**Pahlavi Iran** refers to the period in Iranian history from 1925 to 1979 during which the country was ruled by the Pahlavi dynasty. This era began with the ascension of Reza Shah Pahlavi to the throne after the fall of the Qajar dynasty and ended with the overthrow of his son, Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi, during the Iranian Revolution of 1979. The Pahlavi period was characterized by rapid modernization, secularization, centralization of power, and increasing Western influence, alongside growing internal dissent and political repression.

### Establishment of the Pahlavi Dynasty

Following widespread dissatisfaction with the Qajar dynasty's weakness, especially in the face of foreign interference and internal disorder, Reza Khan, a military officer in the Persian Cossack Brigade, emerged as a powerful figure. With the support of British officials and internal allies, he led a coup d'état in 1921 and subsequently consolidated power. In 1925, the Iranian parliament (Majles) deposed the last Qajar monarch, Ahmad Shah Qajar, and declared Reza Khan as Reza Shah Pahlavi, establishing the Pahlavi dynasty.

### Reza Shah’s Reign (1925–1941)

Reza Shah embarked on a wide-ranging program of modernization and state-building. He focused on infrastructure development, the creation of a centralized bureaucracy, a modern army, and secular educational institutions. Railroads, highways, factories, and public buildings were constructed, while efforts were made to reduce the power of the clergy and tribal leaders.

Reza Shah implemented policies aimed at diminishing religious influence in public life, including banning traditional Islamic dress such as the veil and restricting religious schools. He also pursued cultural Persianization, emphasizing pre-Islamic Iranian history and heritage. His authoritarian style of governance suppressed political opposition, and parties and press freedoms were curtailed.

Despite efforts to maintain neutrality, his growing ties with Nazi Germany drew the concern of Allied powers during World War II. In 1941, the British and Soviet forces invaded Iran to secure oil resources and supply routes. Reza Shah was forced to abdicate in favor of his son, Mohammad Reza Pahlavi.

### Mohammad Reza Shah’s Early Reign (1941–1953)

Initially seen as a weaker monarch, Mohammad Reza Shah faced significant political instability. During the 1940s, Iran experienced a brief period of political pluralism with the emergence of various political parties, labor unions, and increased activity in the press. The most significant figure of this era was Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh, who nationalized the Iranian oil industry in 1951, previously controlled by the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company.

Mossadegh's move provoked a severe reaction from Britain and the United States. In 1953, he was removed from power through a CIA- and MI6-backed coup (Operation Ajax), restoring the Shah’s authority. The coup marked a turning point, reinforcing the Shah's dependence on Western support and beginning a period of authoritarian consolidation.

### White Revolution and Consolidation of Power

In 1963, the Shah launched the **White Revolution**, a series of reforms aimed at modernizing the country and countering communist and clerical influence. These included land reforms, expansion of women's rights (such as the right to vote), educational development, and industrial growth. While these reforms achieved some socio-economic progress, they also deepened divisions in Iranian society. The clergy, led by Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, emerged as vocal critics, particularly opposing secularization and Westernization.

The Shah expanded the powers of the secret police (SAVAK), banned opposition parties, and established a one-party system under the Rastakhiz Party. Political dissent was met with censorship, imprisonment, and exile. Meanwhile, oil revenues allowed for major state-led development projects and increased military spending, further tying Iran to Western—particularly American—interests.

### Foreign Relations

Under both Reza Shah and Mohammad Reza Shah, Iran pursued a policy of modernization and national sovereignty. Reza Shah sought to balance British and Soviet influence by engaging with Germany and other European powers. Under Mohammad Reza Shah, Iran aligned itself more closely with the United States and the West, becoming a key ally during the Cold War.

Iran joined the Baghdad Pact (later CENTO) and received significant military and economic aid. The Shah positioned Iran as a regional power, aspiring to elevate its international status through military strength and oil diplomacy. However, these foreign alignments also fed domestic resentment, especially among nationalists and religious conservatives.

### Decline and Fall of the Monarchy

By the late 1970s, widespread dissatisfaction with the Shah’s rule had grown. Economic inequality, political repression, corruption, and resentment toward Westernization fueled mass unrest. The 1978–79 revolutionary movement, led by a broad coalition including Islamic clerics, leftists, and students, culminated in nationwide protests and strikes.

In January 1979, the Shah left Iran for medical treatment, and on February 1, Ayatollah Khomeini returned from exile, receiving a rapturous welcome. The monarchy officially collapsed on February 11, 1979, and the Islamic Republic of Iran was established shortly thereafter.

### Legacy

The Pahlavi era remains a subject of significant debate in Iranian history. Supporters credit the dynasty with modernizing Iran and strengthening the state, while critics emphasize its authoritarianism, suppression of dissent, and failure to address societal demands for political participation and cultural autonomy. The legacy of Pahlavi Iran continues to influence the country’s political discourse and identity in the post-revolutionary period.
