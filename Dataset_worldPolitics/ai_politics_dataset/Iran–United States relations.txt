**Iran–United States Relations**

Iran–United States relations have been marked by dramatic shifts—from early 20th-century cooperation under the Qajar and Pahlavi dynasties to near-hostility following the 1979 Islamic Revolution. Throughout, bilateral ties have been shaped by strategic interests, ideological conflict, and domestic politics in both countries.

### Early Contacts and Pahlavi Era (1900s–1979)

Diplomatic relations began in 1883, when Persia (now Iran) established a legation in Washington, D.C. U.S. interest in Iran accelerated during both World Wars, as Iran’s oil and strategic position linked it to Allied supply routes. Following the 1941 Anglo-Soviet occupation, the United States took an active role in Iran’s postwar reconstruction.

During the Cold War, Iran under Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi evolved into a key U.S. ally. The 1953 CIA- and MI6-backed overthrow of Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh—who had nationalized the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company—confirmed U.S. readiness to intervene to protect Western oil interests and stem Soviet influence. In return, Iran received extensive American military aid, economic assistance, and diplomatic support, joining the Central Treaty Organization (CENTO) and purchasing advanced U.S. weaponry.

### Islamic Revolution and Hostage Crisis (1979–1981)

Discontent with autocratic rule and rampant social inequality culminated in the 1979 Islamic Revolution, during which the Shah fled into exile. The revolutionaries, led by Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, denounced the Shah’s U.S. patronage as “American imperialism.” On 4 November 1979, Iranian students seized the U.S. Embassy in Tehran, taking 52 American diplomats and citizens hostage for 444 days. The crisis shattered bilateral relations: the United States severed diplomatic ties, imposed economic sanctions, and froze Iranian assets abroad.

### “Tanker War” and Covert Engagements (1980s)

During the Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988), the U.S. publicly supported Iraq yet covertly facilitated limited arms transfers to Iran through third-party intermediaries—part of the Iran–Contra affair. Simultaneously, U.S. naval forces protected Kuwaiti tankers from Iranian attacks in the “Tanker War” phase of the conflict. These episodic contacts did little to normalize relations and were overshadowed by mutual suspicion.

### “Dual Containment” and Sanctions (1990s)

After the war, the United States maintained comprehensive sanctions against Iran. The 1995 Executive Order by President Bill Clinton barred most U.S. companies from dealings with Iran, while the Iran and Libya Sanctions Act of 1996 targeted foreign investment in Iran’s energy sector. U.S. policy of “dual containment” sought to curb both Iranian regional influence and Saddam Hussein’s Iraq.

### Nuclear Dispute and Diplomatic Thaw (2002–2015)

Allegations that Iran pursued a clandestine nuclear weapons program emerged in 2002, prompting U.S. efforts to secure United Nations Security Council sanctions. Diplomatic dialogue was limited until 2013, when President Barack Obama’s administration pursued a negotiated settlement. The 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) lifted many sanctions in exchange for Iran’s agreement to limit uranium enrichment and accept intrusive inspections by the International Atomic Energy Agency. The deal marked a high-water mark in cooperation, though domestic critics in both countries decried perceived concessions.

### U.S. Withdrawal and Renewed Tensions (2018–2021)

In May 2018, President Donald Trump unilaterally withdrew the United States from the JCPOA, reimposing “maximum pressure” sanctions targeting Iran’s oil exports, banking, and shipping. Iran responded by gradually breaching key nuclear limits and expelling some IAEA inspectors. Tensions escalated with incidents including attacks on oil tankers in the Gulf, the downing of a U.S. drone in June 2019, and the January 2020 U.S. drone strike that killed top Iranian general Qasem Soleimani in Baghdad. Both sides avoided a full-scale war but remained in a standoff marked by reciprocal sanctions and sporadic military incidents.

### Biden Administration and Current Status (2021–Present)

President Joe Biden’s administration signaled readiness to rejoin the JCPOA if Iran returned to compliance. Indirect negotiations in Vienna proceeded intermittently through 2022, but progress stalled over Tehran’s insistence on lifting all sanctions and U.S. insistence on strict verification. Meanwhile, Iran continued to expand its regional influence via allied militias in Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, and Yemen, drawing U.S. criticism. Human rights issues—such as the suppression of domestic protests—further complicate any rapprochement.

### Legacy and Prospects

Over more than a century, Iran–United States relations have swung from strategic partnership to enduring adversarial standoff. Key drivers include competition for regional influence, ideological divergence between theocratic and democratic systems, and domestic political constraints on both sides. While the JCPOA era demonstrated the potential for negotiated compromise, renewed sanctions and mutual distrust underscore the challenges of sustaining détente. Future U.S.–Iran relations will likely hinge on broader regional dynamics, nuclear diplomacy, and the willingness of both governments to reconcile strategic interests with domestic political realities.
