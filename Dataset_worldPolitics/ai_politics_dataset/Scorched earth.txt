**Scorched Earth**

**Overview**
Scorched earth is a military strategy in which retreating forces destroy anything that might be of use to an advancing enemy, including infrastructure, supplies, crops, and civilian property. The objective is to deny resources—food, shelter, transportation, or industrial facilities—that could sustain enemy armies or civilian populations aligned with opposing forces. Although effective in hindering advances, scorched‐earth tactics often inflict severe hardship on civilian populations and carry long‐term political and humanitarian consequences.

**Historical Origins**
The practice dates to antiquity. In 4 BCE, Alexander the Great’s troops devastated regions to discourage resistance. Roman generals frequently ordered burning of enemy territories during retaliatory campaigns. Medieval warfare saw similar tactics during the Mongol invasions of the 13th century, when Genghis Khan’s armies laid waste to cities refusing to capitulate, thereby spreading terror and disrupting supply lines.

**Early Modern Period**

* **Thirty Years’ War (1618–1648):** Imperial and Protestant armies alike razed villages and farmland in the German states to starve opposing forces and protect their own lines of communication.
* **Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815):** In 1812, as Napoleon’s Grande Armée invaded Russia, Russian forces under Generals Mikhail Barclay de Tolly and Mikhail Kutuzov adopted a scorched‐earth policy—burning Moscow’s suburbs and grain stores—to deprive the French of winter quarters and supplies. The resulting logistical collapse contributed significantly to the Grande Armée’s disastrous retreat.

**American Civil War (1861–1865)**
Union generals employed scorched earth in the Confederacy. In 1864, Major General William T. Sherman’s March to the Sea across Georgia destroyed railroads, factories, and farms to break Southern morale and capacity to wage war. Sherman famously wrote that his purpose was to make war so “terrible” that the South would cease resistance. While militarily effective, the campaign devastated civilian livelihoods and remains controversial in American memory.

**Twentieth Century Conflicts**

* **World War I:** Retreating Russian forces in 1915 conducted scorched‐earth operations in Poland and Galicia to impede the German‐Austro‐Hungarian advance.
* **World War II:**

  * **Eastern Front:** Both German and Soviet armies practiced scorched earth. As they retreated from advancing forces, Wehrmacht units destroyed infrastructure in the Soviet Union in 1941–1943. Conversely, the Red Army burned villages and rail lines in East Prussia and Pomerania in 1944–1945 to deny the German Wehrmacht staging areas.
  * **Asia:** Japanese forces in China (1937–1945) employed scorched earth during retreats, including the deliberate flooding of the Yellow River in 1938 to halt Chinese Nationalist forces—a catastrophe that caused hundreds of thousands of civilian deaths.

**Post‐War and Late 20th Century**

* **Vietnam War (1955–1975):** U.S. forces used herbicides like Agent Orange to defoliate forests and destroy crops, aiming to expose Viet Cong positions and eliminate food supplies.
* **Middle East Conflicts:** Retreating Iraqi troops set fire to over 600 Kuwaiti oil wells during the Gulf War (1991), creating an environmental disaster designed to hinder coalition landings and destroy a key economic resource.
* **Sub‐Saharan Africa:** During civil wars—such as in Sierra Leone (1991–2002) and Sudan (1983–2005)—both rebel groups and government forces destroyed villages, cattle herds, and crops to punish or displace civilian populations perceived as supporting the enemy.

**Legal and Ethical Considerations**
Under international humanitarian law, deliberate destruction of civilian property not justified by military necessity is prohibited. The 1907 Hague Convention (IV) Respecting the Laws and Customs of War on Land forbids the "destruction or seizure of the enemy’s property, unless such destruction or seizure be imperatively demanded by the necessities of war." Protocol I (1977) to the Geneva Conventions further restricts attacks on objects indispensable to civilian survival. Nonetheless, enforcement is uneven, and scorched‐earth tactics have persisted, often justified by commanders as military necessity.

**Political Consequences**
Scorched earth can yield short‐term military advantage by slowing enemy advances and imposing logistical burdens. However, it often provokes enduring resentment among affected civilian populations, undermines post‐conflict reconstruction, and can fuel cycles of violence and revenge. Political leaders employing or tolerating scorched‐earth tactics risk international condemnation, war‐crimes prosecutions, and long‐term damage to their country’s reputation.

**Modern Alternatives and Mitigations**
Contemporary military doctrine emphasizes precision targeting, protection of civilians, and minimizing collateral damage. Logistics planning and cooperation with local authorities aim to preserve civilian infrastructure while still denying strategic assets to adversaries. Technologies such as cyber‐operations can disrupt enemy supply chains without physical destruction. Nonetheless, in extreme cases—such as defense against invasion or suppression of insurgency—militaries may resort to scorched‐earth measures under the doctrine of total war.

**Conclusion**
Scorched earth has been a recurrent feature of warfare, reflecting the tension between military necessity and humanitarian considerations. While historically effective in disrupting enemy operations, the strategy inflicts severe hardships on civilian populations and carries significant moral and political costs. Evolving legal frameworks and changing norms increasingly constrain its use, underscoring the shift toward more discriminating methods of denying resources in modern conflict.
