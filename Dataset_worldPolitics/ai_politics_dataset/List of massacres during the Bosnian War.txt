**List of Massacres During the Bosnian War**

The Bosnian War (April 1992–December 1995) was marked by widespread ethnic violence and systematic “ethnic cleansing” campaigns carried out primarily by Bosnian Serb and Bosnian Croat forces against Bosniak (Muslim) and, in some cases, Croat civilian populations. Numerous mass killings of civilians—commonly termed massacres—occurred throughout Bosnia and Herzegovina. These events, later prosecuted by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY), illustrate the war’s brutal politicization of ethnicity and territory.

---

## Early Campaign of Ethnic Cleansing (Spring–Summer 1992)

* **Bijeljina (1–2 April 1992)**
  As Bosnian Serb paramilitaries seized the town shortly after independence referendum results, an estimated 48–100 civilians were killed. Public executions and forced expulsions signaled the beginning of a wider Serb “cleansing” in northeast Bosnia.

* **Prijedor (April 1992 onward)**
  Following takeover by Serb forces, non-Serbs were ordered to mark homes with white flags. In May–June 1992, hundreds of civilians were killed in town and surrounding villages. Thousands more perished in Omarska, Trnopolje, and Keraterm detention camps under appalling conditions.

* **Foča (April–May 1992)**
  Bosnian Serb forces overran this eastern town, killing at least 600 Muslim civilians in mass executions, rape camps, and deportations. The campaign aimed to remove the prewar Bosniak majority and secure strategic mountain passes.

* **Zvornik (April 1992)**
  Immediately after capture from Bosnian government forces, Serb militias executed dozens of men and boys in the town’s early days, initiating forced expulsions and mass murders in nearby villages.

---

## Mid–War Atrocities (1993)

* **Ahmići (16 April 1993)**
  In central Bosnia, the Croatian Defence Council (HVO) attacked the village of Ahmići, killing over 100 Bosniak civilians—men, women, and children—in houses and on the streets. The massacre underscored the Croat–Bosniak conflict within the broader war.

* **Kozarac (9–11 July 1992)**
  While technically in 1992, events around Kozarac saw Bosnian Serb forces surround and shell the town, execute hundreds, and deport the remainder; it exemplified the early systematic expulsions in northwestern Bosnia.

* **Grabovica (8 September 1993)**
  HVO troops executed more than 20 Bosniak civilians near Mostar. The act prompted condemnation and highlighted the internecine violence between former allies.

---

## Siege of Sarajevo and Targeted Shelling (1992–1996)

* **Markale Market Shellings**

  * **First Shelling (5 February 1994):** A mortar round fired from surrounding Serb-held hills hit the central Markale marketplace, killing 68 civilians and wounding over 140.
  * **Second Shelling (28 August 1995):** Another mortar attack killed 43 and wounded more than 80. These incidents galvanized NATO air strikes and international intervention to end the siege.

* **Sniper Killings**
  Throughout the nearly four-year siege, civilian targets—pedestrians, women fetching water, children in playgrounds—were deliberately shot by Serb snipers from wooded hills, accounting for thousands of deaths and psychological terror.

---

## Fall and Final Massacres (1995)

* **Srebrenica Genocide (11–17 July 1995)**
  After United Nations “safe area” status failed to prevent Bosnian Serb forces’ takeover, over 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were systematically separated, executed, and buried in mass graves around Srebrenica. It remains the largest single atrocity on European soil since World War II and was later ruled genocide by international courts.

* **Zvornik Region (July–August 1995)**
  In the final offensives (Operations “Storm” and “Maestral”), Serb forces killed scores of remaining Muslims in villages around Zvornik, complementing earlier 1992 massacres.

* **Branjevo and Pilica Killings (16–18 July 1995)**
  In the immediate aftermath of Srebrenica’s fall, Bosniak detainees were executed at multiple sites including Branjevo farm and Pilica village; combined deaths numbered in the hundreds.

---

## Political and Historical Context

1. **Ethnic Cleansing as Strategy:** Massacres served the political objective of creating ethnically homogeneous territories in anticipation of partition plans, notably by the Bosnian Serb leadership under Radovan Karadžić and Ratko Mladić.

2. **International Response:** Early inaction by the United Nations and weak arms embargoes allowed sieges and massacres to continue. High-casualty events, especially at Srebrenica and Markale, triggered NATO air campaigns and the Dayton Peace Accords (November 1995).

3. **Justice and Reconciliation:** The ICTY indicted dozens of political and military leaders—Bosnian Serb, Croat, and some Bosniak—for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide. Landmark convictions (e.g., of Mladić and Karadžić) affirmed accountability under international law.

4. **Legacy:** The Bosnian massacres transformed post–Cold War Western engagement, illustrating the limits of UN peacekeeping without robust enforcement and shaping doctrines on humanitarian intervention and “Responsibility to Protect.”
