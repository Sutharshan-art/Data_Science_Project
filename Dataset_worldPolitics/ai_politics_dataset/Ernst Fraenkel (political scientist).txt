**Ernst Fraenkel** (25 May 1898 – 24 August 1975) was a German political scientist renowned for his analysis of the legal and political structures of National Socialist Germany and his enduring concept of the “dual state.” His work provided foundational insights into the relationship between law, authority, and individual rights under authoritarian regimes and influenced postwar debates on constitutionalism and the rule of law.

### Early Life and Education

Born in Berlin to a liberal Jewish family, Fraenkel studied law and political science at the universities of Berlin and Göttingen, earning his doctorate in 1921 with a dissertation on municipal self-government. He subsequently worked at the Prussian Ministry of the Interior and lectured at the University of Hamburg. His early academic career coincided with the unstable parliamentary democracies of the Weimar Republic, where he developed a keen interest in the formal structures that underpin legal order and governance.

### The Rise of National Socialism and Emigration

With the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, Fraenkel—like many Jewish academics—was dismissed from his university post under anti-Jewish legislation. Forced into exile, he initially sought refuge in France and then in the United Kingdom. During his years in London (1933–1949), he remained an active commentator on German affairs, contributing analyses of Nazi legal practice for British think tanks and government bodies. His experiences in exile informed his later articulation of how authoritarian regimes deploy law both to legitimize and to arbitrarily subvert state authority.

### Academic Career in Exile and Return to Germany

While in Britain, Fraenkel lectured at the London School of Economics and worked with the Foreign Office’s research department. After World War II, he accepted a position at the University of California, Berkeley (1950–1955), where he taught comparative government. In 1955 he returned to West Germany as a professor of political science at the University of Hamburg, where he remained until his retirement in 1966. His return coincided with the consolidation of the Federal Republic’s democratic institutions, and Fraenkel became a prominent voice in debates over constitutional safeguards against authoritarian resurgence.

### The Concept of the “Dual State”

Fraenkel’s most influential work, **The Dual State: A Contribution to the Theory of Dictatorship** (1941), dissected the legal order of the Third Reich into two coexisting realms: the **“normative state”**, which preserved conventional laws and bureaucratic routines, and the **“prerogative state”**, where arbitrary police measures and personal decrees of the Führer operated beyond legal constraint. This framework illuminated how the regime could simultaneously claim continuity with German legal traditions while exercising unbridled power through extralegal institutions such as the Gestapo. The dual-state model became a key analytical tool for scholars examining authoritarian and totalitarian systems.

### Other Major Works and Theoretical Contributions

Building on the dual-state thesis, Fraenkel examined broader themes of sovereignty, legitimacy, and individual rights. In **Rechtsstaat im Wandel** (“The Changing Rechtsstaat,” 1963), he reflected on the evolution of the rule-of-law principle in modern democracies and warned against the expansion of executive prerogative in peacetime. His comparative studies contrasted the Federal Republic’s Basic Law with other constitutional systems, highlighting mechanisms—such as judicial review, federalism, and fundamental rights—that guard against the erosion of legal protections.

### Influence on Postwar German Politics

Fraenkel was an advisor to the Parliamentary Council that drafted West Germany’s Basic Law in 1948–1949, advocating for a clear separation of powers and strong safeguards for individual liberties. His insistence on embedding rights-based judicial review influenced the establishment of the Federal Constitutional Court (Bundesverfassungsgericht). Throughout the 1950s and 1960s, he participated in public debates about emergency powers, arguing that any allowance for exceptional government action must be tightly circumscribed and subject to legislative and judicial oversight.

### Legacy and Continuing Relevance

Ernst Fraenkel’s dual-state paradigm remains a touchstone in the study of authoritarianism, legal pluralism, and state power. Political scientists and legal scholars invoke his analysis to understand how modern regimes—both overtly totalitarian and more subtly illiberal—use legal frameworks to legitimize coercion. His work also informs contemporary discussions about emergency measures, executive overreach, and the resilience of democratic institutions. Fraenkel’s scholarship bridged the gap between legal theory and political reality, demonstrating how the formal structures of law can both constrain and enable the concentration of power. His contributions continue to serve as a cautionary guide for defenders of constitutional democracy.
