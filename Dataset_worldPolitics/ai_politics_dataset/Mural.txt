**Mural**

A **mural** is a work of art painted or applied directly on a wall, ceiling, or other large permanent surface. Historically associated with public and communal spaces, murals have served not only aesthetic functions but also political, religious, and social purposes. As a medium, murals possess the capacity to communicate collective values, commemorate events, and mobilize political sentiment, making them a potent form of public discourse from ancient civilizations to contemporary street art movements.

---

### Historical Overview

#### Pre–Modern and Religious Murals

Murals date back to **prehistoric cave paintings**, such as those at Lascaux (c. 17,000 BCE), which likely held ritual or communicative significance. In the **classical world**, Greco-Roman frescoes adorned civic buildings and villas, often reflecting mythological or imperial themes that reinforced social hierarchies.

During the **Middle Ages**, murals in churches and mosques (e.g., Byzantine mosaics, Renaissance frescoes by Giotto and Michelangelo) conveyed religious narratives and moral instruction, shaping communal identity and ecclesiastical authority.

---

### Political and Social Context of Modern Murals

#### Mexican Muralism (1920s–1940s)

One of the most influential modern mural movements emerged in post–Revolutionary Mexico. Under President **Álvaro Obregón**, the government commissioned artists—**Diego Rivera**, **José Clemente Orozco**, and **David Alfaro Siqueiros**—to create large-scale public murals celebrating the **Mexican Revolution** (1910–1920), indigenous heritage, and labor struggles. These works, executed on government buildings and educational institutions, blended social realism with nationalist and Marxist themes, redefining public art as a tool of **state-sponsored social transformation**.

#### Soviet and Fascist Propaganda Murals

In the Soviet Union and other totalitarian states, murals functioned as **state propaganda**. The Soviet regime employed socialist realist murals to glorify industrial achievements, collectivization, and the cult of leadership. Similarly, Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy commissioned murals depicting heroic Aryan imagery or Roman grandeur to legitimize authoritarian rule and mobilize nationalist fervor.

#### Community and Political Murals in the United States

From the **Chicano mural movement** in the 1960s–1970s to public art initiatives in urban renewal, murals in the United States have articulated civil rights, anti-war, and labor causes. The **East Los Angeles Murals** and Philadelphia’s **Mural Arts Program** transformed neglected neighborhoods into “open-air galleries” that address racial inequality, environmental justice, and local history, often in collaboration with community organizations and municipal agencies.

---

### Techniques and Materials

Murals employ a variety of techniques:

* **Buon fresco**: Pigments applied to wet plaster, resulting in durable integration of paint and wall surface (used in Renaissance Italy).
* **Fresco secco**: Paint applied to dry plaster, allowing greater color range but less longevity.
* **Acrylic and latex paints**: Modern synthetic mediums that offer fast drying, weather resistance, and suitability for exterior walls.
* **Spray paint**: Favored by street artists for speed and portability, contributing to the rise of **graffiti murals**.

The choice of technique often reflects the intended **durability**, **public accessibility**, and **budgetary constraints** of a project, as well as environmental factors such as humidity and pollution.

---

### Murals in Contemporary Politics

#### Protest and Memorial Murals

In periods of political upheaval—**the Troubles** in Northern Ireland, **the Arab Spring**, **Black Lives Matter** demonstrations—murals have emerged spontaneously on barricades, building facades, and public squares. These ephemeral artworks memorialize victims, express dissent, and foster solidarity, often circumventing censorship by existing outside institutional frameworks.

#### UNESCO and Cultural Heritage

Recognizing the cultural significance of murals, UNESCO has inscribed sites such as the **Ajanta Caves** (India), **Angkor Wat** (Cambodia), and Mexico’s **Palacio Nacional** under its World Heritage List. Preservation efforts balance the need to maintain murals’ physical integrity with respect for their ongoing social and political relevance to local communities.

---

### Criticism and Controversy

Murals can become sites of **political contention**. Government-commissioned murals may be criticized as propaganda or cultural imposition, while unsanctioned graffiti murals can lead to legal battles over vandalism and public space rights. The **“gentrification debate”** in many cities highlights tensions between mural-driven placemaking and the displacement of long-term residents.

---

### Legacy and Impact

Murals occupy a unique intersection of **art**, **politics**, and **public space**. Their scale and accessibility democratize artistic expression, enabling wide-ranging audiences to engage with narratives of power, resistance, and identity. From ancient religious frescoes to contemporary street art, murals continue to reflect and shape the political landscapes in which they appear, serving as enduring canvases for collective memory and social commentary.
