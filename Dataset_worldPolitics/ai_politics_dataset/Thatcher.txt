**Thatcher**

**Introduction**
Margaret Hilda Thatcher, Baroness Thatcher, LG OM PC FInstP (1925–2013), was a British stateswoman who served as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom from 1979 to 1990 and Leader of the Conservative Party from 1975 to 1990. She was the first woman to hold either office and remains the longest-serving British prime minister of the 20th century. Thatcher’s administration enacted profound economic and social reforms—collectively known as “Thatcherism”—characterized by market liberalization, privatization of state-owned industries, reduction of trade-union power, and a commitment to monetarist policies.

**Early Life and Education**
Margaret Hilda Roberts was born on 13 October 1925 in Grantham, Lincolnshire. Her father, Alfred Roberts, was a grocer and local alderman who instilled in her a sense of civic duty and self-reliance. She won a scholarship to Somerville College, Oxford, where she studied chemistry and graduated with a second-class honours degree in 1947. After a brief career as a research chemist at the British Xylonite Company, she turned to law, qualifying as a barrister in 1953 and specializing in taxation and pensions.

**Entry into Politics**
Thatcher was elected to the London County Council in 1950 and made two unsuccessful general-election bids before winning the Finchley constituency in 1959. In Parliament, she served on select committees and gained a reputation for careful preparation and tenacity. Under Prime Minister Edward Heath, she was appointed Secretary of State for Education and Science in 1970, where she ended free school milk for children over seven—earning her the nickname “Milk Snatcher.” In 1972, she became Secretary of State for Prices and Consumer Protection.

**Conservative Party Leadership**
Following the Conservative defeat in the February 1974 election, Heath called a leadership contest. Thatcher challenged his leadership later that year but narrowly failed. In the 1975 contest triggered by poor opinion polls, she defeated Heath and two other candidates, becoming Leader of the Opposition and the first woman to lead a major British political party.

**First Term as Prime Minister (1979–1983)**
Thatcher led the Conservatives to victory in May 1979, capitalizing on public discontent with high inflation, industrial unrest, and the “Winter of Discontent.” Her administration implemented monetarist policies to reduce inflation, resulting in tight control of the money supply, public-spending cuts, and higher interest rates. Economic growth returned by the early 1980s, but unemployment nearly doubled, peaking above three million. Thatcher introduced free-market reforms, deregulation of financial markets, and the right-to-buy scheme, which enabled council-house tenants to purchase their homes at discounted rates.

**Second Term (1983–1987)**
Re-elected with a landslide majority in 1983—buoyed by victory in the 1982 Falklands War—Thatcher accelerated privatization of state enterprises, including British Telecom (1984) and British Gas (1986). Her government passed the 1984–85 Employment Acts to curb trade-union power, culminating in the defeat of the National Union of Mineworkers during the 1984–85 miners’ strike. The 1986 “Big Bang” reforms deregulated the London Stock Exchange, transforming the City of London into a leading global financial center.

**Third Term (1987–1990)**
Thatcher won a third term in 1987 but faced growing dissent over domestic and European policies. Amid economic expansion, her administration introduced the Community Charge (“poll tax”) in Scotland in 1989 and in England and Wales in 1990, sparking widespread protests. Thatcher’s Euroskepticism—articulated in her 1988 Bruges speech—alienated pro-European members of her party. After failing to secure sufficient support in a November 1990 leadership challenge, she resigned on 22 November 1990.

**Foreign Policy**
Thatcher was a staunch Cold War ally of U.S. President Ronald Reagan, supporting the deployment of U.S. cruise missiles on British soil and demanding firm opposition to Soviet influence. She endorsed the Strategic Defense Initiative and championed NATO’s deterrent posture. Thatcher forged close ties with newly elected Polish Solidarity leader Lech Wałęsa and opposed German reunification until it could be reconciled with NATO stability.

**Later Life and Legacy**
After leaving 10 Downing Street, Thatcher was created a life peer—Baroness Thatcher of Kesteven—and took a seat in the House of Lords. She authored memoirs, delivered lectures, and remained a vocal influence on Conservative politics. She was made a Companion of Honour (1990), a Lady Companion of the Order of the Garter (1995), and received numerous international honors. Thatcher died on 8 April 2013 at age 87.

Her legacy remains contested: supporters credit her with revitalizing the British economy, reducing the power of entrenched interests, and restoring national confidence; critics argue that her policies eroded social cohesion, increased inequality, and dismantled public-sector institutions. Thatcherism reshaped British politics, prompting both Labour’s shift toward market-oriented “New Labour” and continuing debates over the role of the state in economic management.

**Political Significance**
Margaret Thatcher’s premiership marked a decisive break from post-war consensus politics, laying the foundations for the modern British Conservative Party and influencing center-right governments worldwide. Her emphasis on individual responsibility, free markets, and a robust national defense defined a political era and continues to shape policy debates in the United Kingdom and beyond.
