**Palestine–United States Relations**

**Palestine–United States relations** refer to the diplomatic, political, and economic interactions between the United States of America and the Palestinian people, primarily represented by the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO) and the Palestinian Authority (PA). These relations have evolved over decades, shaped by the broader context of the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, regional dynamics, and U.S. foreign policy priorities in the Middle East.

### Early U.S. Policy and Position

Following the end of the British Mandate in Palestine and the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948, the United States became one of the first countries to recognize Israel. During the early decades of the Arab-Israeli conflict, the U.S. largely maintained a pro-Israel stance, while its interactions with Palestinians were limited and largely indirect.

The U.S. did not recognize any Palestinian national authority, particularly as much of the Palestinian territories fell under the control of neighboring Arab states—Jordan in the West Bank and Egypt in the Gaza Strip. Palestinian political representation began to coalesce under the PLO, founded in 1964 and later led by Yasser Arafat. Initially viewed by the U.S. as a terrorist organization, the PLO was excluded from formal diplomatic engagement.

### Shift in Engagement: The 1980s and Oslo Accords

The U.S. began cautiously shifting its approach in the late 1980s. In 1988, following the Palestinian Declaration of Independence and the PLO’s acceptance of UN Security Council Resolutions 242 and 338, which implicitly recognized Israel’s right to exist, the United States opened a dialogue with the PLO. This marked a turning point in U.S.–Palestinian relations.

The 1993 **Oslo Accords**, signed by Israel and the PLO and facilitated by the United States, were a significant milestone. The Accords established the Palestinian Authority (PA) as a provisional self-government in parts of the West Bank and Gaza Strip. The U.S. played a critical role in supporting the peace process, providing financial assistance to the PA and encouraging bilateral negotiations.

President Bill Clinton hosted key moments in the Oslo process, including the signing ceremony at the White House. However, the failure to reach a final agreement during the 2000 Camp David Summit and the outbreak of the Second Intifada (2000–2005) strained the peace process and complicated U.S.–Palestinian relations.

### Post-Oslo Period and Second Intifada

During the early 2000s, U.S. policy toward the Palestinians focused on security, governance reform, and resumption of peace talks. The administration of President George W. Bush supported a two-state solution and pushed for democratic reforms within the Palestinian Authority.

In 2006, the U.S. reacted strongly to the electoral victory of Hamas, an Islamist group designated as a terrorist organization by the United States, in Palestinian legislative elections. Following Hamas’s takeover of the Gaza Strip in 2007, the U.S. cut direct aid to the Hamas-led government and reinforced support for the PA, which continued to govern parts of the West Bank under President Mahmoud Abbas.

### Obama Administration

Under President Barack Obama, the U.S. reiterated its support for a two-state solution and attempted to restart negotiations between Israel and the Palestinians. Despite these efforts, talks repeatedly stalled. In 2011, the Palestinian Authority sought full UN membership and recognition of Palestinian statehood, a move opposed by the United States, which used its veto power in the UN Security Council to block the initiative.

In 2012, the U.S. opposed the successful Palestinian bid for non-member observer state status at the United Nations General Assembly, viewing it as a unilateral action that undermined direct negotiations with Israel.

### Trump Administration

The administration of President Donald Trump marked a significant shift in U.S. policy. In 2017, the U.S. formally recognized Jerusalem as the capital of Israel and moved its embassy from Tel Aviv to Jerusalem in 2018. This decision was met with strong Palestinian condemnation, as East Jerusalem is claimed by Palestinians as the capital of a future state.

Following the embassy move, the Palestinian leadership cut official ties with the U.S., and the Trump administration responded by closing the PLO office in Washington, D.C., and cutting hundreds of millions of dollars in aid to the PA and UNRWA, the UN agency supporting Palestinian refugees.

In 2020, the Trump administration unveiled the “Peace to Prosperity” plan, known as the **Trump peace plan**, which proposed a fragmented Palestinian state with significant Israeli control over key territories. The Palestinian leadership rejected the plan outright, viewing it as heavily biased in favor of Israel.

### Biden Administration

President Joe Biden has taken steps to restore relations with the Palestinian leadership. The administration reopened diplomatic channels with the PA, resumed funding to UNRWA and Palestinian humanitarian programs, and reaffirmed U.S. commitment to a two-state solution. However, the U.S. has not reversed the embassy move to Jerusalem or reopened the PLO mission, maintaining some Trump-era policies.

The Biden administration has also condemned acts of violence on both sides and called for de-escalation during periods of conflict, such as the 2021 Gaza war. Efforts to revive peace talks have remained stalled amid ongoing tensions and political instability in both Israeli and Palestinian leadership structures.

### Current Status

As of the mid-2020s, U.S.–Palestinian relations remain cautious and complicated. While the United States officially supports the establishment of an independent Palestinian state through negotiations, its close strategic alliance with Israel, along with domestic political dynamics, has limited its ability to act as a neutral broker in the eyes of many Palestinians.

The future of U.S.–Palestinian relations continues to depend on broader regional developments, the internal political landscape of both parties, and the viability of renewed peace efforts.
