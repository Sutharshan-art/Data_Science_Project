**2002 Rome NATO–Russia Summit**

The **2002 Rome NATO–Russia Summit** was held on **May 28, 2002**, at **Pratica di Mare Air Base**, near **Rome, Italy**, and marked a historic step in post–Cold War relations between the **North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO)** and the **Russian Federation**. The summit resulted in the establishment of the **NATO–Russia Council (NRC)**, a new institutional framework intended to deepen cooperation between NATO and Russia on security matters of mutual interest.

The creation of the NRC was seen as a significant diplomatic breakthrough in Euro-Atlantic security and a symbol of Russia's evolving relationship with the West in the wake of the **September 11, 2001** terrorist attacks. By granting Russia a more equal voice in joint discussions and decision-making, the summit aimed to overcome the limitations of previous consultative mechanisms and promote a more stable and inclusive security order in Europe.

---

### Historical Context

Since the end of the **Cold War**, NATO and Russia had been working to redefine their relationship. In **1991**, the collapse of the Soviet Union opened the door to diplomatic engagement, but the process was slow and often strained by mutual mistrust. The establishment of the **Partnership for Peace (PfP)** in 1994 and the signing of the **Founding Act on Mutual Relations, Cooperation and Security** in **Paris in 1997** laid the foundation for dialogue. However, NATO’s 1999 intervention in **Kosovo**, carried out without a UN mandate and over Russia’s objections, severely damaged trust.

Relations began to improve following the 9/11 attacks, when **President Vladimir Putin** offered Russian support for the U.S.-led **War on Terror**. Russia provided intelligence and logistical cooperation and allowed the use of its airspace for operations in **Afghanistan**. This shift led to a reassessment of NATO–Russia ties and created momentum for a more institutionalized partnership.

The 2002 Rome Summit was a culmination of this renewed political will, aimed at transforming the consultative **NATO–Russia Permanent Joint Council (PJC)** into a more integrated decision-making body.

---

### Participants

The summit brought together the heads of state and government of the **19 NATO member countries** at the time and **President Vladimir Putin** of Russia. Hosted by **Italian Prime Minister Silvio Berlusconi**, the event was attended by prominent leaders including **U.S. President George W. Bush**, **U.K. Prime Minister Tony Blair**, **German Chancellor Gerhard Schröder**, and **French President Jacques Chirac**.

The choice of **Pratica di Mare Air Base**, rather than a formal diplomatic venue, was symbolic of the summit’s military and strategic emphasis, as well as its informal tone aimed at breaking with Cold War legacies.

---

### Establishment of the NATO–Russia Council

The most important outcome of the summit was the signing of the declaration titled:
**“NATO–Russia Relations: A New Quality.”**
This agreement formally established the **NATO–Russia Council (NRC)**, replacing the PJC and granting Russia the opportunity to participate in joint decision-making “at 20” alongside NATO members on specific issues of shared concern.

Unlike the PJC, where consultations took place in a “NATO+1” format (with Russia outside the NATO consensus), the NRC was intended to allow **equal footing and consensus-based cooperation** in areas such as:

* Counterterrorism
* Non-proliferation of weapons of mass destruction
* Crisis management
* Arms control
* Missile defense
* Civil emergency planning
* Search and rescue at sea
* Military-to-military cooperation

The NRC was designed not to give Russia veto power over NATO operations but to serve as a mechanism for trust-building, transparency, and joint initiatives where interests aligned.

---

### Political and Strategic Significance

The Rome Summit was widely interpreted as a **strategic realignment** in the Euro-Atlantic area. For NATO, the establishment of the NRC reflected a willingness to integrate Russia more closely into European security frameworks without compromising the alliance’s decision-making autonomy. For Russia, it represented an opportunity to influence Western security policy and shed the adversarial legacy of the Cold War.

The summit also reflected broader shifts in the international order after 9/11. The emphasis on **counterterrorism**, non-state threats, and global security challenges provided a new basis for NATO–Russia collaboration, moving the agenda beyond traditional territorial defense.

However, while the NRC symbolized rapprochement, underlying tensions remained. Russia continued to express concerns about NATO’s **eastward enlargement**, particularly with the prospect of the **Baltic states** joining the alliance. At the same time, NATO emphasized that the NRC would not affect its **open-door policy** or commitments to prospective members.

---

### Reception and Criticism

Reactions to the summit were mixed. Western officials praised the NRC as a **milestone in NATO–Russia cooperation**, offering hope for a more integrated and peaceful Europe. Many observers noted President Putin’s pragmatic pivot toward the West and his willingness to work within multilateral frameworks.

Critics, however, questioned the NRC’s potential effectiveness, citing differing strategic interests, uneven implementation, and the limited scope of cooperation. Some feared that the council would be more symbolic than substantive, serving as a diplomatic gesture rather than a platform for meaningful alignment.

---

### Legacy

The **2002 Rome NATO–Russia Summit** remains a landmark in the history of NATO–Russia relations. It institutionalized a new phase of engagement and raised expectations for a partnership based on shared interests rather than geopolitical rivalry.

In the following years, the NRC facilitated dialogue and cooperation on a range of technical and security issues. However, the relationship would again deteriorate—especially after NATO’s **2004 enlargement** and the **2008 Russia–Georgia War**—revealing the fragility of the trust built in Rome.

Nevertheless, the summit stands as a moment when NATO and Russia attempted to transcend Cold War divisions and build a common security agenda in a post-9/11 world.
