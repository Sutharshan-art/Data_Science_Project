**Treaty on the Final Settlement with Respect to Germany**

**Overview**
The Treaty on the Final Settlement with Respect to Germany, commonly known as the “Two-Plus-Four Treaty,” was signed on 12 September 1990 in Moscow by the two German states—the Federal Republic of Germany (FRG) and the German Democratic Republic (GDR)—and the four occupying powers of World War II: the United States, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, and France. Entering into force on 15 March 1991, the Treaty provided the international legal framework for German reunification, restoring full sovereignty to a united Germany and resolving all outstanding post-war territorial and military issues.

**Historical Background**
In the aftermath of World War II, Germany was divided into occupation zones administered by the Allied powers. The establishment of two sovereign states in 1949—the democratic, Western-aligned FRG and the socialist, Soviet-aligned GDR—solidified the division. Throughout the Cold War, the four powers retained residual rights over Germany’s external relations, borders, and armed forces. By the late 1980s, Mikhail Gorbachev’s policies of perestroika and glasnost in the Soviet Union, coupled with popular protest movements in East Germany, laid the groundwork for political change. The fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 symbolized the irreversible momentum toward reunification, but legal obstacles persisted due to the Allies’ retained rights under the 1945 Potsdam Agreement and subsequent occupation statutes.

**Negotiations and Signing**
Formal negotiations began in February 1990, following an agreement by the two German governments to pursue unity. The “two” parties (FRG and GDR) and the “four” powers convened in a series of talks to address sovereignty, borders, military arrangements, and the withdrawal of occupation forces. Key figures included Chancellor Helmut Kohl (FRG), Prime Minister Lothar de Maizière (GDR), U.S. Secretary of State James Baker, Soviet Foreign Minister Eduard Shevardnadze, British Foreign Secretary Douglas Hurd, and French Foreign Minister Roland Dumas. Despite initial disagreements over the pace of troop withdrawals and the extent of Germany’s future military capabilities, a consensus was reached in early September. The Treaty was formally signed in the Grand Kremlin Palace and ratified by all signatories before German reunification on 3 October 1990.

**Key Provisions**

* **Sovereignty and Borders:** The Treaty recognized the inviolability of Germany’s existing frontiers, including the Oder–Neisse line with Poland, and prohibited any future territorial claims.
* **Military Limitations:** Reunified Germany was permitted to maintain armed forces within prescribed limits (a ceiling of 370,000 troops), subject to transparency measures, and was barred from possessing nuclear, biological, or chemical weapons.
* **Allied Rights and Withdrawal:** All Allied occupation rights were relinquished, and foreign forces were to withdraw from former East German territory by the end of 1994. Existing Western forces in unified Germany were to remain under NATO command, while Soviet troops were to depart in stages.
* **External Alliances:** Germany confirmed its membership in NATO and renounced any other military alliances or the production of nuclear weapons.
* **International Cooperation:** The Treaty established mechanisms for consultation among the signatories on security matters, including a framework for stationing foreign troops and exercises on German soil.

**Political Implications**
The Two-Plus-Four Treaty marked the end of the post-war settlement and the Cold War division of Europe. By restoring full sovereignty, it enabled Germany to emerge as a unified, democratically governed state with full rights under international law. The Treaty’s reaffirmation of borders and prohibition of German militarism addressed longstanding concerns among Germany’s neighbors, particularly Poland and France. Its provisions also transformed NATO’s strategic posture by integrating a larger Germany into the alliance. For the Soviet Union, the agreement secured Western assurances against further NATO expansion—promises that would later become points of contention in East-West relations.

**Implementation and Aftermath**
Following ratification, Soviet forces withdrew from the former GDR ahead of the 1994 deadline, and the last Russian troops left in August 1994. German armed forces were unified under the Bundeswehr, now responsible for defense across the entire country. The FRG’s Basic Law (Grundgesetz) was extended to the former East German Länder (federal states) on reunification day, completing the legal integration. Controversies arose over the pace of economic and social convergence, but politically, the Treaty successfully removed all external constraints on German statehood.

**Legacy**
The Treaty on the Final Settlement with Respect to Germany remains a milestone in European history, symbolizing the peaceful resolution of Cold War divisions and the restoration of national sovereignty. Its framework has been studied as a model for conflict resolution and state reunification. In contemporary discourse, the Treaty’s assurances and the diplomatic guarantees underpinning it continue to inform debates over NATO enlargement, European security architecture, and Germany’s role as a central actor in the European Union.
