**Iran–Iraq War**

The Iran–Iraq War was a protracted armed conflict between the Islamic Republic of Iran and the Republic of Iraq that lasted from September 1980 to August 1988. Often described as a “war of the cities” and the first major conflict of the post-colonial Middle East, it combined territorial disputes, ideological rivalry, and regional power struggles, producing extensive human and economic costs without decisive victory for either side.

### Historical and Political Background

Iraq under President Ṣaddām Ḥussein had emerged after the 1979 revolution in Iran as the dominant Arab power in the Gulf. Iran’s 1979 Islamic Revolution overthrew the monarchy of Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi and established a theocratic republic under Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini. Baghdad’s secular Ba‘thist regime viewed the newly revolutionary Iran—with its rhetoric of exporting Islamic government—as a destabilizing threat to the Arab world, especially to Iraq’s Shiʿa majority. Long-standing disputes over oil-rich border regions, particularly the Shatt al-Arab waterway, and competition for leadership of the Gulf’s Arab states set the stage for conflict.

### Outbreak of War

On 22 September 1980, Iraqi forces launched a surprise invasion across the Iran–Iraq border, aiming to seize Khuzestan province and secure full control of the Shatt al-Arab. Iraq’s leadership anticipated a quick victory, expecting internal turmoil in Iran to hamper its defense. Instead, Iranian irregular militias and Revolutionary Guard units mobilized en masse; Iranian regular forces, despite their purges following the revolution, reorganized rapidly. Early Iraqi gains were gradually reversed as Iran launched counterattacks.

### Phases of the Conflict

1. **Iraqi Offensive (1980–1982):** Iraqi mechanized columns and air strikes targeted Khorramshahr, Abadan, and oil installations. Initial advances captured border towns, but stiff resistance around Khorramshahr halted progress. By mid-1982, Iran had recaptured lost territory and driven Iraqi troops back to prewar lines.
2. **Iranian Counteroffensive (1982–1984):** Bolstered by revolutionary zeal, Iran pressed into southern Iraq, capturing Basra’s outskirts and threatening Basra city itself. Lacking heavy armor and air support, Iranian forces relied on human wave assaults and improvised militia tactics. Iraqi defenses stiffened with increased Soviet, French, and Western weapons transfers.
3. **War of Attrition and “War of the Cities” (1984–1987):** Both sides adopted a defensive posture. Iraqi air raids struck Iranian towns such as Abadan and Dezful, while Iran retaliated with missile and air attacks on Baghdad and Kirkuk. The front stabilized along a static line in southern marshes and desert, with trench systems reminiscent of World War I.
4. **Final Iraqi Offensives and Ceasefire (1987–1988):** Exploiting new chemical-weapon capabilities supplied by Western and Soviet sources, Iraq broke through Iranian defenses in the Majnoon Islands and the Faw Peninsula. Under mounting military and economic pressure, and facing internal unrest, Iran accepted UN Security Council Resolution 598. A United Nations–brokered ceasefire took effect on 20 August 1988.

### International Involvement and Arms Flows

Cold War geopolitics shaped external support. Iraq received extensive military aid and credit from the Soviet Union, France, China, and Gulf Arab states seeking to curb Iranian influence. Iran, isolated diplomatically, relied on Chinese and North Korean arms along with clandestine purchases from Western intermediaries. The United States, while publicly neutral, covertly re-established ties with Iran through the Iran–Contra channel and provided Iraq with dual-use intelligence and satellite imagery to counter Iranian forces.

### Human and Economic Costs

The war inflicted tremendous casualties—estimates range from 500,000 to over one million military deaths combined—and displaced millions of civilians. Both armies suffered heavy losses in manpower, armor, and air fleets. Strategic bombing and missile strikes on civilian centers caused further casualties and infrastructure damage. Oil exports, the lifeblood of both economies, fluctuated dramatically under maritime minefields, tanker attacks, and embargoes. Reconstruction costs and war debts burdened national budgets for decades.

### Political and Regional Consequences

The war entrenched Ṣaddām Ḥussein’s domestic power in Iraq, justifying his authoritarian rule and fostering militarization. In Iran, the conflict solidified the Islamic Republic’s revolutionary legitimacy and empowered the Revolutionary Guard as a dominant security institution. Regionally, the conflagration shifted Gulf Arab policies, spurring the formation of the Gulf Cooperation Council to counter perceived Iranian aggression. U.S. naval operations, dubbed Operation Earnest Will, safeguarded oil shipping lanes and reaffirmed American strategic interests in the Gulf.

### Legacy and Aftermath

Although UN Resolution 598 called for prisoner exchanges and border demarcation, lasting resolution came only in 1990 when Iran and Iraq formally agreed to reset relations without territorial changes. The war’s devastation weakened both states, contributing to Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait in 1990 and the subsequent Gulf War. For Iran, the experience engendered a security doctrine of self-reliance and vigilance against external interference. The Iran–Iraq War remains a stark example of the perils of prolonged conventional warfare in the modern Middle East, demonstrating how ideological and territorial rivalries can escalate into catastrophic mutual attrition.
