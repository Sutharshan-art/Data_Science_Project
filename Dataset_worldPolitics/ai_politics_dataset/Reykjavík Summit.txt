**Reykjavík Summit**

**Overview**
The Reykjavík Summit was a two-day meeting between U.S. President Ronald Reagan and Soviet General Secretary Mikhail Gorbachev, held on October 11–12, 1986, in Reykjavík, Iceland. Although the talks failed to produce a formal agreement, they marked a turning point in Cold War diplomacy by demonstrating both superpowers’ willingness to consider radical reductions in their nuclear arsenals. The summit laid the groundwork for subsequent arms-control treaties, most notably the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty of 1987.

**Historical Context**
By 1986, the Cold War had endured over four decades of military rivalry and nuclear brinkmanship. U.S.–Soviet relations had thawed somewhat under earlier arms-control efforts (the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks), but stalled over verification and modernization issues. President Reagan’s 1983 announcement of the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI)—a proposed space-based missile-defense system—had reignited Soviet fears of losing the strategic balance. In the Soviet Union, Gorbachev’s twin reform programs of **perestroika** (economic restructuring) and **glasnost** (openness) signaled a more flexible Soviet approach to international engagement.

**Preparation and Delegations**
The Reykjavík Summit arose from Gorbachev’s initiative to broaden arms-control negotiations, coupled with U.S. interest in reducing the burdens of nuclear competition. Hosting took place at the Höfði House, a former French consulate, selected for its symbolic neutrality. Reagan’s delegation included Secretary of State George P. Shultz, National Security Advisor Robert McFarlane, and arms-control negotiator Paul Nitze. The Soviet side comprised Foreign Minister Eduard Shevardnadze, Defense Minister Dmitry Yazov, and chief arms-control negotiator Yuli Kvitsinsky. Both leaders arrived prepared to explore the unthinkable: the elimination of all strategic nuclear weapons.

**Key Issues and Negotiating Positions**
At the heart of the talks was the possible abolition of all intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs), and heavy bombers. Gorbachev proposed deep cuts in exchange for U.S. constraints on SDI research and deployment; Reagan insisted on preserving SDI as essential to long-term U.S. security. Secondary issues included limits on chemical weapons stockpiles and reductions in theater nuclear forces. Each side presented draft texts outlining phased elimination schedules and verification mechanisms, but could not reconcile Soviet demands for binding constraints on SDI testing with U.S. insistence on maintaining the initiative’s technological autonomy.

**Summit Outcomes**
No formal treaty was signed at Reykjavík. On October 12, the summit ended abruptly when the two sides failed to bridge the gap over SDI’s verification and scope. Nonetheless, both Reagan and Gorbachev publicly described the discussions as earnest and historic. They agreed to continue negotiations and to accelerate the drafting of an INF Treaty to eliminate land-based missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometers. Within four months, negotiators concluded the INF Treaty, banning an entire class of nuclear weapons—an achievement directly traceable to Reykjavík’s spirit of radical ambition.

**Legacy and Significance**
Although Reykjavík is often remembered as a “failed” summit, historians and participants view it as a diplomatic success. It shattered taboos by bringing the possibility of a world free of nuclear weapons onto the agenda and by testing ideas later enshrined in START and INF agreements. The summit bolstered public confidence in superpower dialogue, contributed to the gradual de-escalation of Cold War tensions, and lent momentum to the Gorbachev-Reagan partnership. Reykjavík remains a case study in high-stakes diplomacy: a demonstration that even deeply entrenched adversaries can approach consensus on existential security issues when political leadership is determined to push beyond incrementalism.
