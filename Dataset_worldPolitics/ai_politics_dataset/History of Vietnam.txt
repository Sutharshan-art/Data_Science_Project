**History of Vietnam**

The history of Vietnam spans over two millennia of state formation, dynastic rule, colonial domination, revolutionary struggle, and socialist governance. Situated on the eastern margin of the Indochinese Peninsula, Vietnam’s political trajectory has been shaped by its interactions with China, European colonial powers, and global ideological conflicts.

---

## Early Statehood and Chinese Rule (c. 1000 BCE–938 CE)

Archaeological sites such as Đông Sơn (c. 600 BCE) attest to complex Bronze Age chiefdoms centered on wet-rice agriculture and metalworking. From 111 BCE, China’s Han dynasty annexed northern Vietnam (Giao Chỉ), integrating it as a series of provinces under imperial administration. Chinese rule introduced Confucian bureaucracy, Chinese script, and Buddhism, but periodic revolts (notably the Trung Sisters’ rebellion, 40–43 CE) signaled persistent local resistance.

---

## Independent Dynasties (938–1802)

In 938 CE, Ngo Quyen’s victory at the Battle of Bach Dang River ended a millennium of Chinese rule, inaugurating a sequence of independent Vietnamese dynasties. The **Đinh** (968–980) and **Early Lê** (980–1009) dynasties consolidated central authority, while the **Lý** (1009–1225) and **Trần** (1225–1400) eras fostered Confucian statecraft, Buddhism, and military repulsion of Mongol invasions (1258, 1285, 1288).

The **Lê** dynasty (1428–1789), under Lê Lợi’s leadership, restored independence after the Ming occupation. Under the **Later Lê**, a Confucian meritocracy governed through regional lords (Trịnh in the north, Nguyễn in the south), leading to de facto partition (1592–1774). Peasant revolts, especially the **Tây Sơn** uprising (1771–1802), overthrew both Trịnh and Nguyễn lords, briefly reunifying the country before Nguyễn Ánh established the **Nguyễn** dynasty (1802–1945) with French support.

---

## French Colonization and Nationalist Awakening (1858–1945)

France began acquiring territory in southern Vietnam (Cochinchina) in 1858, completing colonial control by 1887 under French Indochina. Colonial rule imposed cash-crop economies, Western education, railway and port infrastructure, and Catholic missionary activity. Limited political reforms galvanized nationalist movements: the **Phan Bội Châu**–led Đông Du (Eastern Study, 1905–1908), the **Vietnam Revolutionary Youth League** (1925) founded by Hồ Chí Minh, and the **Indochinese Communist Party** (1930).

Japanese occupation (1940–1945) during World War II weakened French authority. The **August Revolution** (1945), led by Hồ Chí Minh’s Việt Minh, seized power in Hanoi and proclaimed the **Democratic Republic of Vietnam** on 2 September 1945.

---

## Division and the Wars for Unification (1946–1975)

French attempts to reassert control sparked the **First Indochina War** (1946–1954). The Việt Minh’s victory at Điện Biên Phủ (1954) led to the **Geneva Accords**, temporarily dividing Vietnam at the 17th parallel: communist North Vietnam (DRV) and the Republic of Vietnam in the south under Ngô Đình Diệm.

The **Second Indochina War** (Vietnam War) erupted in 1959 as the communist **National Liberation Front** (Viet Cong) in the south, backed by the DRV, waged guerrilla warfare against the U.S.–backed Saigon government. U.S. combat forces deployed in 1965 under the **Gulf of Tonkin Resolution**. Following the **Paris Peace Accords** (1973) and U.S. withdrawal, North Vietnamese forces captured Saigon on 30 April 1975, reunifying the country under communist rule.

---

## Socialist Republic and Early Reconstruction (1976–1986)

On 2 July 1976, the **Socialist Republic of Vietnam** (SRV) was proclaimed, merging North and South under the **Communist Party of Vietnam** (CPV). The new government nationalized industry, collectivized agriculture, and aligned closely with the Soviet bloc. Border conflicts—the Sino-Vietnamese War (1979) and Cambodian intervention (1978–1989) to topple the Khmer Rouge—strained resources and led to international isolation, notably an economic embargo by the United States (1975–1994).

---

## Đổi Mới Reforms and Economic Integration (1986–2000)

Facing economic stagnation and global shifts, the CPV’s 6th National Congress (1986) launched **Đổi Mới** (“renovation”), transitioning to a **socialist-oriented market economy**. Reforms included decollectivizing agriculture, legalizing private enterprise, and courting foreign investment through **economic zones** (e.g., Đà Nẵng, Vũng Tàu).

Economic liberalization spurred rapid GDP growth, poverty reduction, and trade expansion. Diplomatic normalization with the United States (1995) and ASEAN membership (1995) facilitated integration into regional and global institutions.

---

## Political Continuity and Contemporary Challenges (2000–present)

Vietnam maintains a one-party state under the CPV, with National Congresses every five years shaping leadership and policy. The economy continues to grow—averaging 6–7 percent annual GDP growth pre-COVID—driven by manufacturing, services, and exports (textiles, electronics).

Political control remains firm: the CPV oversees media, civil society, and the judiciary, curtailing dissent and religious activism. Campaigns against corruption, notably under General Secretary Nguyễn Phú Trọng (elected 2011, re-elected 2016, 2021), have targeted officials at all levels.

Key challenges include income inequality, environmental degradation, and vulnerability to climate change, especially in the Mekong Delta. Strategically, Vietnam balances relations with China—its largest trading partner but maritime rival in the South China Sea—and deepens security and economic ties with the United States, Japan, India, and ASEAN under its **“multidirectional foreign policy.”**

---

Vietnam’s history from its ancient state formations through colonial subjugation, revolutionary war, socialist consolidation, and market-oriented reform reflects persistent themes of national independence, ideological adaptation, and pragmatic governance in a complex regional and global environment.
