**Joseph Vissarionovich Stalin** (born Ioseb Besarionis dze Jughashvili; December 18 \[December 6 OS], 1878 – March 5, 1953) was a Soviet political leader who served as General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU) from 1922 until his death in 1953, and Premier of the Soviet government from 1941. Under Stalin’s rule, the USSR transformed from a predominantly agrarian society into a major industrial and military power, but his tenure was also marked by widespread political repression, forced collectivization, and famines.

### Early Career and Rise to Power

Born in Gori, Georgia, then part of the Russian Empire, Stalin joined the Bolshevik faction of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party in 1903. He organized strikes and expropriations (“exes”) to fund party activities, leading to multiple arrests and exiles in Siberia. Following the February and October Revolutions of 1917, Stalin was appointed People’s Commissar for Nationalities and later held posts in the Central Committee. In 1922, he became General Secretary of the party’s Secretariat—a position he used to build a base of loyal supporters by controlling appointments and party patronage.

After Vladimir Lenin’s death in January 1924, Stalin outmaneuvered rivals such as Leon Trotsky, Grigory Zinoviev, and Lev Kamenev. By 1928, he had consolidated power and embarked on policies to secure his leadership and reshape Soviet society.

### Domestic Policies and the Great Purge

Stalin’s first major initiative was the “First Five-Year Plan” (1928–1932), which prioritized rapid industrialization and the collectivization of agriculture. State control of industry expanded dramatically, with the USSR achieving significant increases in steel, coal, and armaments production. Forced collectivization of peasant farms aimed to extract grain to finance industrial growth, but resistance and poor harvests led to famines—most notably the Holodomor in Ukraine (1932–1933), which resulted in millions of deaths.

From 1936 to 1938, the Great Purge (or “Great Terror”) targeted perceived political opponents within the CPSU, the military, and the broader population. Show trials, mass arrests by the NKVD (secret police), and executions or gulag sentences removed an estimated 600,000–1.2 million people. The purges consolidated Stalin’s authority but disrupted military leadership and administrative expertise on the eve of World War II.

### World War II and the Eastern Front

In August 1939, the USSR signed the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact with Nazi Germany, dividing Eastern Europe into spheres of influence and enabling Soviet occupation of eastern Poland, the Baltic states, and parts of Romania. On June 22, 1941, Germany launched Operation Barbarossa, invading the Soviet Union. Stalin initially distrusted intelligence warnings of the invasion and evacuated to Kuibyshev (now Samara), but soon assumed direct command of the war effort.

Under Stalin’s leadership, the Red Army absorbed devastating losses but ultimately repelled the Wehrmacht. Key victories at Stalingrad (1942–1943) and Kursk (1943) marked turning points. By May 1945, Soviet forces had captured Berlin, leading to Nazi Germany’s surrender. The USSR emerged from the war with enormous human and material losses—over 20 million dead—but established itself as a global superpower alongside the United States.

### Postwar Policies and the Onset of the Cold War

After World War II, Stalin established pro‐Soviet regimes across Eastern Europe, creating a “buffer zone” known as the Eastern Bloc. The Yalta and Potsdam Conferences (1945) delineated spheres of influence, but disagreements with Western Allies over Germany and political freedom in Eastern Europe contributed to Cold War tensions. Stalin’s blockade of Berlin (1948–1949) and support for communist parties worldwide—through the Cominform—heightened the rivalry between East and West.

Domestically, Stalin pursued reconstruction through the “Fourth Five-Year Plan” (1946–1950), emphasizing heavy industry and defense. Widespread shortages of consumer goods persisted. The anti‐cosmopolitan and anti‐Jewish campaigns of the late 1940s targeted perceived “rootless cosmopolitans” and culminated in the “Doctors’ Plot” of 1952–1953, shortly before Stalin’s death.

### Death and Legacy

Stalin suffered a stroke on March 1, 1953, and died on March 5. His funeral in Moscow drew millions. In 1956, Nikita Khrushchev’s “Secret Speech” denounced Stalin’s cult of personality and abuses, initiating a period of “de‐Stalinization.” Historians estimate that Stalin’s policies led to the deaths of at least 6–9 million in the purges and famine, with total excess deaths under his rule possibly exceeding 20 million.

Stalin’s role in industrializing the Soviet Union and defeating Nazi Germany secured the USSR’s superpower status, but at immense human cost. His legacy remains deeply contested: in Russia and parts of the post‐Soviet space, some view him as a strong leader who modernized and defended the nation; elsewhere, he is condemned as a tyrant whose regime perpetrated atrocities on a massive scale.
