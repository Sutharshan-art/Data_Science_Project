**Three-Finger Salute (Serbian)**

The three-finger salute (Serbian: Три прста, *Tri prsta*) is a hand gesture commonly used by ethnic Serbs as a symbol of Serbian identity, unity, and Orthodox faith. It is performed by raising the thumb, index, and middle fingers (or sometimes the thumb, index, and little fingers) while folding the remaining digits into the palm. The gesture invokes the Holy Trinity—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—and has acquired nationalist overtones in modern political contexts.

**Historical Origins**
The gesture’s roots trace to medieval Serbian Orthodox tradition, wherein believers make the sign of the cross using three fingers to symbolize the Trinity and two fingers (the index and middle) to represent Christ’s dual nature. This practice became widespread in liturgical rites and popular devotion.

**World War II and Chetnik Adoption**
During World War II, Serbian nationalist guerrilla forces known as the Chetniks, led by Draža Mihailović, repurposed the three-finger cross‐making into an overt salute among fighters. Members and supporters of the Yugoslav Army in the Fatherland (Chetniks) used the salute to express anti‐communist resistance and Serbian patriotism. Although the Chetniks were officially royalist and anti-Axis, accusations of collaboration with occupying forces complicated their legacy.

**Post‐War Prohibition and Underground Use**
Under Socialist Yugoslavia (1945–1991), overt displays of Serbian nationalism were discouraged or suppressed by the communist regime, which promoted “brotherhood and unity” among Yugoslavia’s six republics and multiple ethnicities. The three-finger salute survived in private devotional practice but was largely absent from public political life.

**Reemergence in the 1990s and the Yugoslav Wars**
With the rise of nationalism following the breakup of Yugoslavia, the three-finger salute reemerged as a symbol of Serbian resistance and solidarity. During the Croatian War of Independence (1991–1995) and the Bosnian War (1992–1995), Serb paramilitary groups and Republican Army of Republika Srpska (VRS) troops used the salute in combat zones and propaganda. It became a ubiquitous marker of ethnic Serb identity in protests, rallies, and military contexts.

**Political and Cultural Significance**

* **Ethnic Identity:** The salute conveys pride in Serbian heritage and Orthodox faith. It is used by political leaders—most notably Presidents Slobodan Milošević (1990s), Boris Tadić, and Aleksandar Vučić—as a gesture of solidarity with ethnic Serbs domestically and in the diaspora.
* **Sporting Events:** Serbian athletes and fans frequently employ the salute during international competitions (e.g., football, basketball), linking national achievement to historical perseverance.
* **Diaspora Communities:** Serbian expatriate organizations in Western Europe, North America, and Australia use the salute at cultural festivals and church events to reinforce communal bonds.

**Controversies and Criticism**
The three-finger salute is controversial in regions affected by the Yugoslav wars:

* **Croatia and Bosnia and Herzegovina:** Governments and minority communities often view the salute as evocative of wartime aggression and ethnic cleansing. Croatian and Bosnian authorities have on occasion banned or fined its public display under laws against hate speech and incitement.
* **Kosovo:** In the predominantly ethnic Albanian province, which declared independence from Serbia in 2008, the salute is widely seen as a provocation. Kosovo police and municipalities have fined individuals for performing the gesture at official events.
* **International Law:** The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) judged certain uses of Serbian nationalist symbols—including the three-finger salute—within broader patterns of ethnic intimidation, particularly in the context of war crimes trials. However, the salute itself has not been formally designated as a prohibited symbol under international conventions.

**Legal and Regulatory Responses**

* **Freedom of Expression Protections:** Serbia’s constitution guarantees freedom of expression, and the salute remains legal in Serbia and Montenegro. Attempts to legislate against nationalist symbols have been met with resistance by nationalist parties.
* **Hate‐speech Legislation:** In Croatia, Bosnia, and Kosovo, hate‐speech and public‐order laws have been applied to salute displays deemed to incite ethnic hatred. Enforcement varies by municipality and judicial interpretation.

**Contemporary Usage**
In the 21st century, the salute retains prominence during Serbian national holidays (e.g., Statehood Day, Vidovdan) and political rallies. Political parties across the spectrum—ranging from the Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) to smaller nationalist groups—employ the salute to varying degrees. Critics argue that its nationalist connotations hinder reconciliation in the Western Balkans, while supporters assert its cultural and religious significance.

**Conclusion**
The three-finger salute exemplifies the complex interplay of religion, nationalism, and politics in the Western Balkans. From medieval devotional practice to Chetnik wartime symbol, and from communist-era proscription to post‐Yugoslav resurgence, the gesture encapsulates key themes of Serbian identity and regional tension. Its continued use underscores unresolved historical grievances and the challenges of fostering multi‐ethnic coexistence in Southeast Europe.
