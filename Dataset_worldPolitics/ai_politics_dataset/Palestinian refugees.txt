**Palestinian Refugees**

**Palestinian refugees** are individuals and their descendants who were displaced from their homes in Mandatory Palestine during the 1948 Arab–Israeli War and the 1967 Six-Day War. The term commonly refers to those who fled or were expelled during the 1948 conflict that accompanied the establishment of the State of Israel, and to a lesser extent, those displaced by subsequent hostilities. The refugee issue remains one of the most enduring and contentious aspects of the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, with significant political, humanitarian, and legal implications.

### Origins and Displacement (1948)

The Palestinian refugee crisis began in 1948, during the final months of the British Mandate and the outbreak of the Arab–Israeli War following the UN Partition Plan and the declaration of the State of Israel. Between 700,000 and 750,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled from their homes in cities and villages across Palestine. Causes of this exodus included military attacks, fear of violence, psychological warfare, and, in some cases, direct expulsions by Zionist forces.

The newly established State of Israel did not permit the return of the refugees, citing security concerns and the disruption of the demographic and political balance. Most refugees fled to neighboring Arab countries such as Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, and to the West Bank and Gaza Strip, which came under Jordanian and Egyptian control, respectively.

### United Nations Response

In response to the crisis, the United Nations established the **United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA)** in 1949 to provide humanitarian assistance, education, health care, and social services to Palestinian refugees. UNRWA defines a Palestinian refugee as someone whose residence was in Palestine between June 1946 and May 1948 and who lost both home and livelihood due to the 1948 conflict. The agency also extends its services to descendants of male refugees.

Unlike the UN High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), which handles most global refugee cases, Palestinian refugees fall under the exclusive mandate of UNRWA within its five areas of operation: Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, the West Bank, and Gaza Strip.

### The 1967 Displacement

A second major wave of Palestinian displacement occurred during and after the **Six-Day War** in June 1967, when Israel occupied the West Bank, Gaza Strip, East Jerusalem, and the Golan Heights. Approximately 300,000 Palestinians were displaced, many of whom were refugees for the second time. This group became known as the 1967 refugees and, while distinct from the 1948 refugees, their plight added complexity to the overall refugee issue.

### Refugee Camps and Conditions

Many Palestinian refugees initially lived in makeshift camps, which over time developed into more permanent, though often under-resourced, communities. Today, there are over 50 recognized Palestinian refugee camps, primarily in Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, the West Bank, and Gaza. Conditions vary by location, with refugees in Lebanon and Syria facing more severe restrictions on employment, mobility, and property rights compared to those in Jordan, where most Palestinian refugees have full citizenship.

Political instability, armed conflicts, and host-country policies have further complicated refugee lives. For instance, the Syrian civil war has severely impacted the well-being of Palestinian refugees in Syria, leading many to become secondary or even tertiary refugees.

### Legal and Political Status

The legal status of Palestinian refugees varies by host country. Jordan granted citizenship to most 1948 refugees, while Lebanon and Syria restrict legal and civil rights. Refugees in the West Bank and Gaza Strip live under the jurisdictions of the Palestinian Authority and, in Gaza, Hamas.

One of the core political issues is the **“right of return”**, which refers to the claim by Palestinian refugees and their descendants to return to their original homes in what is now Israel. This claim is grounded in UN General Assembly Resolution 194 (1948), which states that refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbors should be permitted to do so.

Israel rejects the full implementation of the right of return, arguing that it would threaten the Jewish character of the state and that the refugee problem should be resolved through resettlement and compensation. Conversely, Palestinian leaders and many refugees maintain the right of return as a fundamental principle and non-negotiable component of any peace settlement.

### Role in Peace Negotiations

The refugee issue has been central to various rounds of Israeli–Palestinian peace negotiations, including the 1991 Madrid Conference, the 1993 Oslo Accords, the 2000 Camp David Summit, and the 2007 Annapolis Conference. Despite international mediation, no comprehensive agreement has been reached on the fate of the refugees.

Proposed solutions have included repatriation to a future Palestinian state, integration into host countries, resettlement elsewhere, and compensation. However, political, demographic, and symbolic considerations have made the issue one of the most challenging to resolve.

### Current Status

As of the 2020s, there are over 5.9 million registered Palestinian refugees according to UNRWA. The majority continue to live in refugee camps or in marginalized communities across the Middle East. The unresolved status of Palestinian refugees remains a powerful symbol of Palestinian identity and dispossession, and a key factor in regional politics and peace efforts.

The refugee question continues to shape Palestinian national aspirations, Israeli security concerns, and international diplomatic initiatives, reflecting its enduring complexity and sensitivity.
