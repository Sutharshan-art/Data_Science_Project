**CIA Activities in Iran**

The **Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)** has been actively involved in Iran since the early years of the Cold War, engaging in operations that have significantly influenced the country's political trajectory and bilateral relations between Iran and the United States. The CIA's activities in Iran have ranged from covert regime change to intelligence gathering, surveillance, and counterintelligence efforts. These operations reflect broader U.S. strategic interests in the Middle East, including concerns over Soviet influence, oil resources, regional stability, and nuclear proliferation.

The legacy of CIA actions in Iran—particularly the 1953 coup and subsequent intelligence operations—has left a lasting imprint on Iranian political discourse and public perceptions of the United States.

---

### 1953 Coup d’État (Operation Ajax)

The most well-known CIA operation in Iran was **Operation Ajax**, the 1953 coup that overthrew Iran's democratically elected Prime Minister, **Mohammad Mossadegh**. Mossadegh had nationalized the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company (AIOC), angering British interests and raising concerns in Washington about possible communist influence in Iran during the Cold War.

In coordination with Britain’s MI6, the CIA organized and financed a campaign of propaganda, political bribery, and street protests to destabilize Mossadegh’s government. On August 19, 1953, the coup succeeded in removing Mossadegh from power and reinstating **Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi** as the dominant political figure. The Shah subsequently established a more authoritarian regime, heavily supported by U.S. economic and military aid.

The coup deeply affected Iran’s political evolution and remains a central grievance in Iran’s historical narrative. It is widely cited as an example of Cold War-era Western interference in sovereign states and is often regarded by historians as a key turning point in U.S.–Iranian relations.

---

### Post-Coup Cooperation and Intelligence Operations

Following the 1953 coup, the CIA maintained close ties with Iran’s internal security and intelligence agency, **SAVAK** (Organization of Intelligence and National Security), which was established in 1957 with U.S. and Israeli assistance. SAVAK was tasked with suppressing dissent, monitoring opposition groups, and protecting the Shah’s regime. It gained notoriety for its use of torture and harsh methods, contributing to public resentment toward the monarchy and its Western allies.

During this period, the CIA used Iran as a key base for regional operations, particularly for monitoring the Soviet Union. Iran’s strategic location and close alliance with Washington made it a valuable platform for intelligence gathering during the Cold War. Iran also participated in U.S.-backed regional security initiatives such as the **Central Treaty Organization (CENTO)**.

---

### 1979 Revolution and CIA Setbacks

The **1979 Iranian Revolution**, which led to the overthrow of the Shah and the establishment of the **Islamic Republic under Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini**, dramatically altered the CIA’s presence and capabilities in Iran. The revolutionaries viewed the United States—and particularly the CIA—as complicit in decades of authoritarian rule and repression.

Following the storming of the U.S. Embassy in Tehran in November 1979, 52 American diplomats and citizens were taken hostage in what became known as the **Iran Hostage Crisis**. Among the documents seized from the embassy were CIA files and communications, which were later published by Iranian authorities. The incident effectively ended direct CIA operations in Iran and marked a significant rupture in bilateral relations.

Since the revolution, the Islamic Republic has systematically dismantled the CIA’s network inside Iran, executed suspected informants, and maintained an adversarial posture toward U.S. intelligence activities.

---

### Post-Revolution Intelligence Focus

Despite the lack of a formal presence, the CIA has continued to monitor Iran from outside its borders. Its post-revolution focus has included:

* **Nuclear Proliferation**: Monitoring and assessing Iran’s nuclear program, particularly following suspicions of weaponization efforts in the early 2000s. Intelligence estimates and covert operations have played a central role in U.S. policy decisions, including sanctions and diplomatic engagement, such as the **Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA)** signed in 2015.

* **Covert Cyber Operations**: The CIA has reportedly collaborated with Israeli intelligence agencies in cyber operations targeting Iran’s nuclear infrastructure, most notably the **Stuxnet** cyberattack, which damaged centrifuges at the Natanz enrichment facility in 2010.

* **Support to Dissidents and Exiles**: While publicly denied, there have been persistent allegations that the CIA has provided funding and support to Iranian dissident groups, such as the **Mujahedin-e Khalq (MEK)**, which has been both a controversial and shifting element of U.S. policy circles.

* **Counterterrorism and Regional Intelligence**: The CIA continues to track Iranian support for proxy groups across the Middle East, including Hezbollah in Lebanon, Shi’a militias in Iraq, and the Houthis in Yemen. These efforts form part of broader U.S. efforts to contain Iranian influence in the region.

---

### Contemporary Context and Legacy

The history of CIA activities in Iran continues to shape Iranian political rhetoric and foreign policy. Iranian leaders regularly cite the 1953 coup and subsequent CIA involvement as evidence of Western interference and justification for their anti-American stance. The CIA’s role is invoked to explain both internal crackdowns and external tensions.

In the U.S., Iran remains a high-priority intelligence target, with debates over how best to engage, contain, or negotiate with the Islamic Republic frequently influenced by the legacy of past operations.

---

### Conclusion

CIA activities in Iran have been pivotal in shaping the modern history of both countries. From orchestrating regime change to tracking nuclear developments and regional power projection, the CIA's involvement reflects broader themes of Cold War rivalry, geopolitical strategy, and the complex legacy of foreign intervention. The fallout from these operations continues to resonate in U.S.–Iranian relations and the broader Middle East political landscape.
