**Soviet–Afghan War**

The **Soviet–Afghan War** was a nearly decade-long conflict from **December 1979 to February 1989** in which the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR)** intervened militarily in **Afghanistan** to support the country's Marxist-Leninist government against a growing insurgency. The war became a Cold War proxy conflict, drawing in the **United States**, **Pakistan**, **Saudi Arabia**, and others who supported the Afghan **mujahedeen** resistance. The conflict resulted in significant human, economic, and geopolitical costs, contributing to the weakening of the Soviet Union and shaping the future of Afghanistan and regional politics.

---

### Background

In **1978**, the **Saur Revolution** brought the pro-Soviet **People’s Democratic Party of Afghanistan (PDPA)** to power, overthrowing President **Mohammed Daoud Khan**. The PDPA, led initially by **Nur Muhammad Taraki** and later by **Hafizullah Amin**, began implementing radical socialist reforms, including land redistribution and secularization. These policies alienated large segments of Afghan society, especially conservative tribal and religious groups.

Internal divisions within the PDPA and growing opposition led to widespread unrest. The regime’s brutal repression further destabilized the country. Alarmed by the situation and Amin's erratic leadership, Soviet leaders decided to intervene militarily to prevent the collapse of the Afghan communist government and preserve their influence in the region.

---

### Soviet Invasion and Objectives

On **24 December 1979**, the Soviet Union launched a full-scale invasion of Afghanistan. Soviet special forces stormed the presidential palace in Kabul, assassinated Amin, and installed **Babrak Karmal**, leader of the rival PDPA faction **Parcham**, as the new president.

The USSR justified the intervention under the **Brezhnev Doctrine**, claiming to defend socialism and assist a fraternal state under threat. Officially, the Soviet leadership aimed to stabilize the government, suppress the insurgency, and maintain Afghanistan as a buffer state on the USSR’s southern border. However, the invasion was widely condemned internationally as an act of aggression.

Approximately **100,000 Soviet troops** were deployed at the height of the conflict. Despite their superior firepower and control of major cities, the Soviets faced a determined insurgency composed of various mujahedeen groups operating in the rugged countryside and supported by foreign powers.

---

### Mujahedeen Resistance and Foreign Involvement

The **mujahedeen**, or "holy warriors," were a loose coalition of Islamic guerrilla fighters opposing the Soviet-backed regime. They received significant **military, financial, and logistical support** from the United States (through the CIA's **Operation Cyclone**), **Pakistan** (via the ISI), **Saudi Arabia**, **China**, and other countries. The Pakistani border regions, particularly in **Peshawar**, became centers of resistance coordination.

The insurgents used hit-and-run tactics, ambushes, and their knowledge of the terrain to challenge Soviet supply lines and installations. Over time, they gained access to increasingly advanced weaponry, including **U.S.-supplied Stinger missiles**, which were instrumental in neutralizing Soviet air superiority by targeting helicopters and aircraft.

The Soviet military, despite occupying urban centers and maintaining a heavy presence, was unable to win the hearts and minds of the Afghan population or decisively defeat the insurgency.

---

### Human and Political Cost

The war had a devastating impact on Afghanistan. Estimates suggest up to **1.5 million Afghan civilians** were killed, and over **5 million** fled the country as refugees, primarily to **Pakistan** and **Iran**. Entire villages were destroyed, and the Afghan economy and infrastructure were severely damaged.

The Soviet Union also suffered heavily: over **15,000 Soviet soldiers** were killed and tens of thousands wounded. The war placed a huge financial burden on the USSR and led to growing domestic discontent and criticism of the Communist Party leadership.

Politically, the war eroded Soviet prestige, deepened internal divisions, and exposed the limits of Soviet military power. It became increasingly unpopular among the Soviet public and leadership, contributing to the reformist wave under **Mikhail Gorbachev**, who sought to end the conflict as part of his broader policy of **perestroika** and **glasnost**.

---

### Withdrawal and Aftermath

Under Gorbachev, the Soviet Union began pursuing a negotiated settlement. The **Geneva Accords**, signed in **April 1988**, outlined the terms for Soviet withdrawal. The last Soviet troops left Afghanistan on **15 February 1989**, ending nearly ten years of occupation.

Though the USSR had propped up the Afghan government under **Mohammad Najibullah**, it eventually fell in **1992** after the Soviet collapse and the end of aid. Afghanistan descended into civil war, and the eventual rise of the **Taliban** in the mid-1990s would have profound consequences for global politics, including the events of **9/11**.

---

### Legacy

The Soviet–Afghan War is often referred to as the “Soviet Union’s Vietnam” due to its high cost, unclear objectives, and long-term repercussions. It significantly weakened the Soviet Union both materially and ideologically and exposed the vulnerabilities of its political and military systems.

For Afghanistan, the war marked the beginning of decades of instability, warlordism, and foreign intervention. It also played a crucial role in the rise of **militant Islamism**, as foreign fighters and ideologues who participated in the jihad later formed global terrorist networks.

The conflict remains a defining moment in late Cold War history, symbolizing the limits of superpower intervention and the unintended consequences of proxy warfare in the modern era.
