**Woman’s Own**

**Introduction**
*Woman’s Own* is a British weekly women’s magazine, first published on 1 April 1932. Over its history, it has combined fashion, lifestyle, and celebrity content with advocacy on social and political issues affecting women. Owned by TI Media (formerly IPC Media), *Woman’s Own* has periodically mounted campaigns on topics such as equal pay, domestic violence, and women’s health, positioning itself as both a popular glossy and a forum for public debate on gender and social policy.

**Founding and Early Years (1932–1950s)**
Launched by George Newnes Ltd in the interwar period, *Woman’s Own* filled a growing market for mass-market women’s publications. Early issues offered homemaking advice, serialized fiction, and features on etiquette and fashion. During World War II, the magazine supported the war effort by publishing articles on women’s contributions to munitions factories and the Women’s Voluntary Service, reflecting government drives to mobilize female labor. Postwar, *Woman’s Own* tracked the transition of women from wartime work back to domestic roles, while also spotlighting emerging consumer culture.

**Expansion and Social Advocacy (1960s–1980s)**
In the 1960s, *Woman’s Own* was acquired by IPC Magazines, which invested in higher production values and celebrity interviews. The magazine began to engage more directly with social issues: in 1967 it ran a series on the legalization of contraception and abortion that coincided with debates leading to the 1967 Abortion Act. During the 1970s women’s liberation movement, *Woman’s Own* both reflected and shaped public opinion by publishing articles on workplace discrimination, marital rape (then not criminalized), and the introduction of the Equal Pay Act (1970). Its 1975 cover story “Equal Pay Now!” helped catalyze grassroots pressure on unions and Parliament to implement anti-discrimination provisions more rigorously.

**Format and Editorial Strategy**
As a weekly glossy, *Woman’s Own* balances celebrity and lifestyle content—fashion spreads, cosmetics reviews, and TV personality interviews—with investigative journalism and reader-driven campaigns. Its “Real Lives” features profile women who have overcome adversity—domestic violence, poverty, or chronic illness—and link personal stories to broader policy debates. Regular columns by legal and medical experts advise readers on rights and services, reinforcing the magazine’s role as both entertainer and advocate.

**Campaigns and Political Impact (1990s–Present)**
In the 1990s, under editor Eve Pollard, *Woman’s Own* launched the “Break the Silence” campaign against domestic violence, collaborating with Women’s Aid to lobby for stronger legal protections and funding for shelters. The campaign’s petitions and high-profile endorsements contributed to the Domestic Violence, Crime and Victims Act (2004). In 2008, *Woman’s Own* partnered with the Fawcett Society on a “Mind the Pay Gap” drive, which helped keep gender pay disparities on the national agenda ahead of the 2010 Equality Act.

More recently, the magazine campaigned on menopause recognition, publishing a “Menopause Manifesto” in 2019 urging employers to adopt supportive workplace policies. This advocacy preceded the 2022 Government Workplace Menopause Pledge, signaling *Woman’s Own*’s continued influence on policy discourse. Alongside political campaigns, the magazine maintains lifestyle sections on home décor, cooking, and celebrity news, sustaining broad readership.

**Circulation and Demographics**
At its peak in the 1990s, *Woman’s Own* sold over 600,000 copies weekly; circulation has declined in line with industry trends, standing at approximately 250,000 in 2023. Its core readership comprises women aged 30–55, primarily in middle-income households. While digital subscriptions and online content have grown, the print edition remains a significant platform for advocacy, with reader panels and social media engagement amplifying its campaigns.

**Editorial Leadership**
Key editors have shaped *Woman’s Own*’s political engagement. Joyce Stone (1960–1972) expanded social-issue coverage; Eve Pollard (1985–1989) professionalized investigative journalism; Constance Duncombe (2002–2010) refocused on reader-led campaigning; and Victoria Summerley (2018–present) has prioritized digital outreach and inclusivity, introducing regular columns by experts on mental health, financial rights, and political advocacy.

**Criticism and Controversy**
While praised for its campaigns, *Woman’s Own* has faced criticism for the tension between its commercial content—beauty and diet features—and its political advocacy, with some commentators dubbing such efforts “cause marketing.” It has also been scrutinized for the occasional sensationalism of personal stories. Nevertheless, its editors argue that commercial viability underwrites its capacity to sustain advocacy journalism.

**Legacy**
Over nine decades, *Woman’s Own* has mirrored and influenced changing gender norms in the UK. By combining popular culture with substantive policy debate, the magazine has contributed to legislative reforms—from equal pay to domestic-violence protections—while offering a platform for women’s voices. Its continued presence in both print and digital formats attests to its adaptability and enduring role in the intersection of lifestyle media and political discourse.
