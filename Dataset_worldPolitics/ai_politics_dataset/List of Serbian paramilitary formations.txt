**List of Serbian Paramilitary Formations**

The **list of Serbian paramilitary formations** includes armed groups operating alongside or independently of formal military or police forces, often during periods of conflict or political instability. Serbian paramilitaries have played notable roles in the Yugoslav Wars of the 1990s, including in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, and Kosovo. While some formations were formally disbanded or integrated into state structures, others operated with varying degrees of autonomy, often with alleged or proven links to political figures, intelligence services, or military institutions.

These groups have been associated with a range of activities, from territorial defense and nationalist mobilization to human rights abuses and war crimes. Their emergence and operations must be understood within the context of the violent disintegration of Yugoslavia, ethnic nationalism, and the collapse of state authority.

---

### Historical Context

Following the death of Yugoslav leader Josip Broz Tito in 1980 and the subsequent weakening of the federal government, nationalist movements gained strength across the republics of Yugoslavia. As the federation began to unravel in the early 1990s, the Socialist Republic of Serbia—under the leadership of Slobodan Milošević—sought to protect Serb populations in Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and later Kosovo.

Amid the chaos, various Serbian paramilitary groups emerged, often recruited from former military personnel, nationalist volunteers, criminals, or political loyalists. While some operated under the command of official Yugoslav or Serbian forces, others functioned more as irregular militias. These groups were implicated in numerous atrocities during the wars and were sometimes used as proxies to provide the state with plausible deniability.

---

### Notable Serbian Paramilitary Formations

#### **Serbian Volunteer Guard (SDG) – “Arkan’s Tigers”**

* **Leader:** Željko Ražnatović “Arkan”
* **Active:** 1990–1996
* **Conflict Involvement:** Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina
  One of the most infamous Serbian paramilitary units, the SDG operated under the leadership of Arkan, a career criminal and nationalist. The group was involved in ethnic cleansing campaigns and war crimes during the wars in Croatia and Bosnia. It was believed to have ties to the Serbian Interior Ministry and intelligence services. Arkan was indicted by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) but was assassinated in 2000 before facing trial.

#### **White Eagles (Beli Orlovi)**

* **Leaders:** Dragan Vasiljković (Captain Dragan), Mirko Jović
* **Active:** 1991–1995
* **Conflict Involvement:** Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina
  A nationalist and monarchist paramilitary formation, the White Eagles were associated with the Serbian Radical Party and Orthodox Christian symbolism. The group was accused of involvement in atrocities against Croat and Bosniak civilians. It operated in close proximity to official Serbian and Bosnian Serb forces.

#### **Scorpions**

* **Active:** 1990s–early 2000s
* **Conflict Involvement:** Bosnia and Herzegovina, Kosovo
  The Scorpions were originally a special forces unit but later operated as a paramilitary group aligned with Serbian security services. They were involved in the Srebrenica massacre, and several members were prosecuted for executing Bosniak prisoners. The unit was also active during the Kosovo War and implicated in war crimes there.

#### **Yugoslav People's Army (JNA) Paramilitary Units**

* As the JNA fractured along ethnic lines, various units formed localized Serbian paramilitary detachments. While not officially designated as paramilitaries, some JNA-aligned Serb units carried out irregular operations and worked in tandem with nationalist militias.

#### **Red Berets (Unit for Special Operations – JSO)**

* **Active:** 1996–2003
* **Leader:** Milorad Ulemek “Legija”
  Though formally part of the Serbian State Security Service, the Red Berets operated with considerable autonomy and conducted covert operations in Kosovo, Bosnia, and Macedonia. They were later linked to organized crime and political violence in Serbia, including the assassination of Prime Minister Zoran Đinđić in 2003.

---

### Other Lesser-Known or Localized Formations

* **Chetnik Detachments**: Revived during the Yugoslav Wars, these units drew on World War II-era Chetnik symbolism and ideology, particularly in Bosnia.
* **Luka’s Wolves**: A small group operating in eastern Bosnia, involved in crimes against non-Serb civilians.
* **Kninjas**: A paramilitary unit active in the self-declared Republic of Serbian Krajina, led by Dragan Vasiljković.

---

### Legal and Political Implications

Many Serbian paramilitary formations have been implicated in serious human rights violations, including ethnic cleansing, mass killings, forced displacement, and sexual violence. Numerous leaders and members have been indicted or convicted by the ICTY and domestic courts. The relationship between these groups and the Serbian state remains controversial, with evidence suggesting varying degrees of state support, coordination, or complicity.

The use of paramilitaries allowed political leaders to wage war while maintaining deniability and avoiding full international accountability. In the post-war years, efforts have been made to prosecute war crimes and disband such units, though some remnants of paramilitary networks have persisted in organized crime and political violence.

---

### Legacy and Reconciliation

The legacy of Serbian paramilitary formations continues to affect regional politics, ethnic relations, and transitional justice in the Western Balkans. Memorialization and denial of war crimes remain contentious issues, and some former paramilitaries have been portrayed as heroes in nationalist narratives. At the same time, civil society groups and international institutions continue to advocate for accountability, historical truth, and reconciliation.

Understanding the structure, role, and impact of Serbian paramilitaries is critical to comprehending the dynamics of the Yugoslav Wars and the broader challenges of post-conflict peacebuilding in the region.
