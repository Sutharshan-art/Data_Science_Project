**Second Inauguration of Bill Clinton**

**Overview**
The second inauguration of William Jefferson Clinton as the 42nd President of the United States took place on Monday, January 20, 1997, at the West Front of the United States Capitol in Washington, D.C. It marked the commencement of Clinton’s second and final four‐year term, alongside Vice President Albert Arnold “Al” Gore Jr. Sworn in by Chief Justice William H. Rehnquist, Clinton became the first Democratic president since Franklin D. Roosevelt to win two consecutive terms.

**Historical and Political Context**
Clinton’s first term (1993–1997) witnessed significant policy achievements—passage of the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), welfare reform via the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996, and budget surpluses after decades of deficit spending. The economy experienced sustained growth, low unemployment, and technological expansion. Politically, the 1994 midterm elections had delivered Republicans a congressional majority for the first time in 40 years, propelling Newt Gingrich’s “Contract with America.” Clinton responded with a centrist “New Democrat” agenda, blending fiscal discipline with investment in education and technology, which contributed to his re‐election victory over Republican nominee Senator Bob Dole in November 1996.

**Planning and Organization**
The Presidential Inaugural Committee for 1997, chaired by Prudence Bushnell and Senator Bob Kerrey, coordinated events under the theme “Building a Bridge to the Twenty‐First Century.” The committee raised over \$30 million through private donations, corporate sponsorships, and ticket sales for inaugural balls. Security planning involved the United States Secret Service, the Federal Bureau of Investigation, and local law enforcement, reflecting heightened concerns after the 1993 World Trade Center bombing and 1995 Oklahoma City bombing.

**Ceremony Details**

* **Oath of Office**: At 12:05 p.m. EST, following the conclusion of outgoing President Clinton’s participation in the swearing‐in of Vice President Gore, Chief Justice William Rehnquist administered the presidential oath: “I do solemnly swear that I will faithfully execute the Office of President of the United States, and will to the best of my ability, preserve, protect, and defend the Constitution of the United States.” Clinton’s hand rested on a Bible once owned by Civil War General and President Ulysses S. Grant.
* **Inaugural Address**: Clinton’s 20‐minute speech—his sixth major address as president—emphasized themes of economic opportunity, technological innovation, and national unity. He called for continued investment in education (“the schools of the twenty-first century”), the Information Superhighway, and a “third way” beyond partisanship. He reaffirmed America’s global leadership responsibilities and social safety‐net commitments, declaring: “Our progress began not in Washington or Wall Street or Silicon Valley, but in our homes and our schools, in our workplaces and our communities.”
* **National Anthem and Invocation**: Country music star Trisha Yearwood performed “The Star-Spangled Banner.” The invocation was delivered by Bishop Frank J. Dewane, reflecting Clinton’s appeal to a broad religious electorate.

**Parade and Festivities**
Following the swearing-in, a 1.5-mile inaugural parade proceeded down Pennsylvania Avenue, featuring 12,000 participants: military units, marching bands, equestrian detachments, cultural delegations, and floats commemorating American diversity. Notable marchers included members of the Tuskegee Airmen, high-school bands from every U.S. state, and representatives of Native American nations. The parade culminated at the Ellipse near the White House, where President and First Lady Hillary Rodham Clinton greeted the public.

**Inaugural Balls and Cultural Events**
That evening, 11 official inaugural balls were held in the Washington Convention Center and at satellite locations nationwide. Headliners included Smokey Robinson, Mary J. Blige, and the Washington National Opera’s Metropolitan Opera singers. Ticket prices ranged from \$150 to \$350, with proceeds benefiting the Presidential Inaugural Committee’s scholarship and cultural programs. Clinton and Gore attended a “Millennium Ball” featuring multimedia projections on the theme of global interconnection.

**Attendance and Public Reaction**
An estimated 300,000 spectators lined the National Mall and Pennsylvania Avenue, a decrease from Clinton’s first inauguration in 1993 amid colder weather. Television coverage on major networks drew over 25 million viewers, reflecting sustained public interest despite predictable political divisions. Editorial commentary praised Clinton’s optimistic tone but noted ongoing challenges: partisan gridlock, healthcare reform setbacks, and foreign policy crises in Bosnia and the Middle East.

**Legacy and Significance**
Clinton’s second inaugural underscored the evolving nature of American centrism in the 1990s, balancing market‐driven growth with social investment. His emphasis on information technology foreshadowed the Internet’s explosive expansion and the dot-com era. The ceremony also highlighted the tensions between private sponsorship and public pageantry, as inaugural funding costs surged. In retrospective analyses, historians view the 1997 inauguration as the high‐water mark of post–Cold War domestic confidence, preceding later controversies such as the Monica Lewinsky scandal and the contentious 2000 election.
