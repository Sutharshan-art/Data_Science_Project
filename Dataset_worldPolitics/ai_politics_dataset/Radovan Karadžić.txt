**Radovan Karadžić**

**Early Life and Education**
Radovan Karadžić was born on June 19, 1945, in Petnjica, in what was then the People’s Republic of Montenegro, Federal People’s Republic of Yugoslavia. He was the youngest of four children in a Bosnian Serb family. His father, Nikola, was a schoolteacher and his mother, Olga, a homemaker. After the death of his mother in 1952, Karadžić moved with his family to Sarajevo, where he completed primary and secondary schooling. He studied psychiatry at the University of Sarajevo’s Faculty of Medicine, graduating in 1970. Early in his career, Karadžić worked at the University Clinical Center in Sarajevo and gained a reputation as a writer of poetry and folk-inspired prose.

**Political Emergence**
Karadžić entered politics in the late 1980s amid rising ethnic tensions in Yugoslavia. In 1989, he co-founded the Serbian Democratic Party (SDS) in Bosnia and Herzegovina, positioning it as the principal political voice for Bosnian Serbs. As president of the SDS, Karadžić championed the principle of “one man, one vote” to counter perceived political disadvantages for Serbs under a system he viewed as dominated by Bosniak and Croat interests. Through speeches and party organization, he built a support base in rural and semi-urban Serb communities, emphasizing Serbian national identity and autonomy within Bosnia.

**Role in the Bosnian War**
Following Bosnia and Herzegovina’s declaration of independence in March 1992, Karadžić assumed leadership of the self-proclaimed Republika Srpska (RS), serving as its first president. Under his direction, the Bosnian Serb Army (VRS) sought to establish contiguous Serb territories and secure strategic towns and routes. Karadžić’s administration coordinated military and political strategies alongside General Ratko Mladić, with whom he shared command authority. During the war, ethnic Serb forces controlled large swaths of northeastern Bosnia, besieged Sarajevo for nearly four years, and conducted campaigns aimed at separating Bosniak and Croat populations from Serb areas.

**War Crimes and Indictment**
Throughout the conflict (1992–1995), forces under Karadžić’s authority committed widespread atrocities, including the siege of Sarajevo and the massacre at Srebrenica, where more than 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were killed. These events were characterized by systematic shelling of civilian areas, ethnic cleansing through forcible displacement, detention camps, and extrajudicial killings. In July 1995, the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) indicted Karadžić on charges of genocide, crimes against humanity, and violations of the laws or customs of war, marking him as one of the principal architects of the RS’s wartime policies.

**Fugitive Years and Arrest**
Karadžić evaded arrest for over a decade, adopting the alias “Dragan Dabić” and living in Belgrade, Serbia. During this period he engaged in alternative medicine, zodiacal research, and poetry readings, occasionally giving public interviews without revealing his identity. International pressure and cooperation among law enforcement agencies intensified, leading to his capture on July 21, 2008, at a Belgrade bus station. His arrest was hailed as a major milestone in the enforcement of international justice and brought long-awaited accountability for wartime leaders.

**Trial and Conviction**
Karadžić’s trial at The Hague began in October 2009 and was one of the longest in ICTY history. Prosecutors presented evidence of his direct involvement in policymaking, military strategy, and public incitement of violence. Defense arguments focused on claims of self-defense, denial of genocidal intent, and challenges to the tribunal’s jurisdiction. In March 2016, the ICTY found Karadžić guilty on multiple counts, including genocide in Srebrenica and crimes against humanity, sentencing him to 40 years’ imprisonment. On appeal in 2019, the sentence was increased to life imprisonment, reflecting the gravity of his actions and the scale of civilian suffering.

**Legacy and Historical Assessment**
Radovan Karadžić remains a polarizing figure. For many Bosniaks and Croats, he symbolizes the brutality and ethnic divisions of the Bosnian War, embodying a campaign of systematic persecution. Among some Serb nationalists, he is regarded as a defender of Serb interests, though his conviction has tempered overt praise. His trial is often cited as a benchmark in international criminal law, demonstrating the ability of global institutions to hold heads of state and political leaders personally responsible for mass atrocities. Karadžić’s life and career continue to inform debates on nationalism, secessionist movements, and the international community’s role in preventing and prosecuting war crimes.
