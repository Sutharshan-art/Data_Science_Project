**Soviet Invasion of Poland**

The **Soviet invasion of Poland**, conducted on **17 September 1939**, was a coordinated military operation by the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR)** in eastern Poland during the opening weeks of **World War II**. The invasion followed the signing of the **Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact**, a non-aggression treaty between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, which included a secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe into spheres of influence. The Soviet invasion came 16 days after Nazi Germany’s attack on Poland from the west and contributed to the collapse of the **Second Polish Republic**.

The Soviet government justified the invasion on the grounds that the Polish state had ceased to exist and that it was acting to protect ethnic Ukrainians and Belarusians in the region. In reality, the move was a direct implementation of territorial ambitions agreed upon with Germany. The invasion led to the occupation and annexation of eastern Polish territories into the Soviet Union and was followed by political repression, mass arrests, and deportations.

---

### Background and Political Agreements

On **23 August 1939**, Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union signed the **Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact**, named after foreign ministers **Vyacheslav Molotov** and **Joachim von Ribbentrop**. Though publicly presented as a non-aggression treaty, a **secret protocol** delineated spheres of influence in Eastern Europe. Under this agreement, eastern Poland was assigned to the Soviet sphere.

On **1 September 1939**, Germany invaded Poland from the west, triggering war with Britain and France. Although the Soviet Union remained officially neutral, it began mobilizing its forces along the Polish border. As the Polish military focused on resisting the German advance, its eastern front remained poorly defended.

Soviet authorities delayed their intervention until it became clear that Polish resistance was weakening and that Germany was advancing quickly. On **17 September**, the USSR crossed into Poland from the east, citing the need to protect minorities and restore order in the absence of a functioning Polish state.

---

### Military Operation and Occupation

The Red Army invaded with approximately **600,000 troops**, facing only sporadic and disorganized resistance from Polish border units. The Polish government, recognizing it could not fight a war on two fronts, issued orders to avoid armed conflict with Soviet forces if possible. However, in some cases, Polish units engaged Soviet troops, particularly in cities like **Lwów (Lviv)** and **Wilno (Vilnius)**.

Within a few weeks, Soviet forces had occupied a territory of over **200,000 square kilometers**, home to approximately **13 million people**, including Poles, Ukrainians, Belarusians, and Jews. The Red Army met little organized resistance and was sometimes welcomed by segments of the local non-Polish population.

On **28 September 1939**, the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany signed the **German–Soviet Boundary and Friendship Treaty**, which redrew the demarcation line and formalized the partition of Poland. The Soviet Union took control of eastern Poland, while Germany retained the west.

---

### Political Repression and Annexation

The Soviet-occupied territories were soon incorporated into the Ukrainian and Belarusian Soviet Socialist Republics. In October 1939, staged elections were held in which Soviet authorities claimed overwhelming popular support for annexation. The territories were formally annexed in November.

The Soviet regime quickly implemented policies of **Sovietization**, including the nationalization of land and industry, suppression of political and religious institutions, and the arrest of Polish military personnel, landowners, intellectuals, and officials.

Between **1939 and 1941**, it is estimated that **over 1 million people** were deported from the annexed territories to Siberia and other parts of the USSR. Many perished due to harsh conditions. Among the most notorious episodes was the **Katyn Massacre** in 1940, in which approximately **22,000 Polish officers and intelligentsia** were executed by the NKVD, the Soviet secret police.

---

### International Reactions

The Soviet invasion of Poland was widely condemned in the West, though the focus remained primarily on Germany’s aggression. Britain and France, allied with Poland, declared war on Germany but not on the Soviet Union, in part due to political pragmatism and the desire not to open a wider conflict.

The Polish government-in-exile, established first in France and later in London, never recognized the legality of the Soviet occupation. Diplomatic relations between Poland and the USSR remained strained until the German invasion of the Soviet Union in **June 1941**, when both sides signed the **Sikorski–Mayski Agreement** to cooperate against the Axis.

---

### Legacy and Historical Interpretation

The Soviet invasion of Poland has remained a highly controversial subject in Polish–Russian relations. While Soviet historiography long portrayed the action as a liberation of oppressed peoples, post-Soviet scholarship and contemporary Polish historiography emphasize the invasion as an act of aggression and a clear example of collusion with Nazi Germany.

In 1989, the Soviet Union officially acknowledged the secret protocol of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact. In 1993, Russian President **Boris Yeltsin** expressed regret over Soviet actions, including the Katyn massacre, but disputes over historical memory have continued into the 21st century.

The Soviet invasion of Poland marked the beginning of a brutal occupation, the dismemberment of the Polish state, and the expansion of Soviet influence westward. It contributed to the wider collapse of the interwar European order and set the stage for the complex realignments and atrocities of World War II.
