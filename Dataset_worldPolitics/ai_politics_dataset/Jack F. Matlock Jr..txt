**Jack F. Matlock Jr.** (born October 1, 1929) is an American diplomat, political scientist, and historian noted for his role in shaping United States policy toward the Soviet Union during the final years of the Cold War. Serving in senior positions in the U.S. Foreign Service from 1956 to 1991—including U.S. Ambassador to the Soviet Union (1987–1991)—Matlock played a central part in the diplomatic initiatives that led to the end of superpower confrontation and the peaceful dissolution of the USSR.

### Early Life and Education

Born in Greensboro, North Carolina, Matlock grew up in a family with a strong interest in international affairs. He studied German and Russian at Duke University, earning a B.A. in 1951, followed by an M.A. in international economics and Russian studies from Columbia University in 1953. His master’s thesis examined Soviet economic planning, presaging his lifelong focus on U.S.–Soviet relations.

### Early Career and Soviet Expertise

Matlock joined the U.S. Foreign Service in 1956. Early postings included Berlin (1958–1962), where he witnessed Berlin Wall construction, and Warsaw (1963–1966). In 1967 he began five years at the State Department’s Bureau of Intelligence and Research as a Soviet analyst, advising on Soviet political and economic developments. His expertise in Russian language and culture led to assignments at U.S. embassies in Moscow (1974–1976) and Geneva (as political counselor to the U.S. mission to the Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe, 1976–1978).

### Senior Posts and Cold War Diplomacy

From 1978 to 1981, Matlock served as Deputy Director of the State Department’s policy planning staff, contributing to the Carter administration’s human‐rights‐focused approach to the Soviet Union. In 1981 he returned to Moscow as Political Counselor, deepening his ties with reform‐minded Soviet interlocutors. His analysis of Soviet leadership under Leonid Brezhnev, Yuri Andropov, and Konstantin Chernenko informed U.S. strategic thinking during the Reagan era’s initial confrontational phase.

### Ambassador to the Soviet Union

In August 1987, President Ronald Reagan appointed Matlock U.S. Ambassador to the Soviet Union, a position he held through President George H.W. Bush’s administration until February 1991. Matlock’s tenure coincided with Mikhail Gorbachev’s policies of *perestroika* (restructuring) and *glasnost* (openness). He cultivated close working relationships with Soviet reformers, including Foreign Minister Eduard Shevardnadze, and facilitated high‐level summits that produced landmark arms control agreements: the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty (1987) and the Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (START I) negotiations.

Matlock’s embassy became a hub for diplomatic engagement: he organized people‐to‐people exchanges, cultural events, and media dialogues that humanized Americans and Soviets to one another. He also provided Washington with nuanced assessments of Soviet political dynamics, warning policymakers of the risks of rapid upheaval while supporting the U.S. goal of a peaceful end to the Cold War.

### Role in Soviet Dissolution

As Soviet republics moved toward independence in late 1990 and early 1991, Matlock advised against U.S. recognition of Baltic independence until the Gorbachev government’s legal authority had agreed, favoring stability. Following the failed August 1991 coup by hardliners in Moscow, Matlock supported U.S. statements backstopping Gorbachev while recognizing emerging republics. He left Moscow in February 1991, just months before the Soviet Union formally dissolved in December.

### Post‐Diplomatic Career and Scholarship

After retirement, Matlock served as President of the Rockefeller Brothers Fund (1991–1995) and taught diplomacy and Russian studies at Williams College. He authored several books on U.S.‐Soviet relations, notably *Autopsy on an Empire: The American Ambassador’s Account of the Collapse of the Soviet Union* (1995) and *Reagan and Gorbachev: How the Cold War Ended* (2004). His works emphasize the importance of sustained dialogue, cultural understanding, and patiently managed reform.

### Legacy and Impact

Matlock is widely regarded as a model of principled diplomacy and expert regional knowledge. His advocacy for deep engagement with the Soviet leadership underpinned pivotal arms control and confidence‐building measures. He has continued to comment on U.S. foreign policy, cautioning against renewed adversarial postures toward Russia and advocating for diplomatic engagement grounded in mutual understanding.

Jack F. Matlock Jr.’s career illustrates the central role that skilled diplomacy and cross‐cultural expertise played in peacefully ending the Cold War. His efforts alongside reformist Soviets and supportive U.S. policymakers helped transform an era defined by nuclear standoff into one of superpower cooperation and set precedents for conflict resolution based on dialogue and incremental trust‐building.
