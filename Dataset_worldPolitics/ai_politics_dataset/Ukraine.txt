**Ukraine**

**Overview**
Ukraine, officially the Republic of Ukraine, is a sovereign state in Eastern Europe. Bordered by Russia to the east and northeast, Belarus to the north, Poland, Slovakia and Hungary to the west, Romania and Moldova to the southwest, and the Black Sea to the south, Ukraine is the second-largest country in Europe by area. With a population of approximately 44 million, its capital and largest city is Kyiv. Since declaring independence from the Soviet Union in 1991, Ukraine has undergone profound political transformations, navigating democratic reforms, economic challenges, and security crises, most notably the 2014 annexation of Crimea by Russia and the full-scale Russian invasion beginning in 2022.

**Historical Background**
The territory of modern Ukraine was the cradle of Kievan Rusʹ (9th–13th centuries), a federation of East Slavic tribes that adopted Orthodox Christianity in 988 under Prince Vladimir the Great. Following the Mongol invasions, western regions came under the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and later the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, while the eastern principalities gradually fell under the control of the Grand Duchy of Moscow. From the late 18th century, Ukraine was divided between the Russian Empire (east and center) and the Habsburg Empire (western Galicia). Ukrainian national consciousness emerged in the 19th century, spurred by cultural revival and political movements advocating autonomy.

**Twentieth-Century Transformations**
Amid the Russian Revolutions of 1917–1918, the Ukrainian People’s Republic briefly declared independence, only to be subsumed into the Soviet Union as the Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic (UkrSSR) by 1922. The interwar years saw Soviet-imposed collectivization and severe repression, culminating in the Holodomor famine (1932–1933), in which millions perished. Western Ukrainian territories were annexed after World War II. Under Soviet rule, Ukraine underwent rapid industrialization but remained subject to Moscow’s centralized governance.

**Independence and Early Political Development**
On 24 August 1991, following the failed coup in Moscow, the Verkhovna Rada (parliament) of the UkrSSR declared independence. A December 1991 referendum confirmed overwhelming support for sovereignty. Leonid Kravchuk became Ukraine’s first president, and the country embarked on post-Soviet economic reforms. Political life in the 1990s was characterized by the struggle to balance privatization, market liberalization, and social stability. Constitutional reforms in 1996 established a semi-presidential system, delineating powers between the president, parliament, and government.

**Orange Revolution (2004–2005)**
The 2004 presidential election, contested between pro-Western Viktor Yushchenko and pro-Russian Viktor Yanukovych, was marred by fraud and intimidation. Mass protests—dubbed the Orange Revolution—forced a repeat vote, which Yushchenko won. The movement highlighted deep political and regional divides: western and central regions tended to favor European integration, while eastern and southern areas leaned towards closer ties with Russia. Yushchenko’s presidency achieved limited reforms amid ongoing factionalism.

**Euromaidan and 2014 Crisis**
In November 2013, President Viktor Yanukovych halted preparations for an association agreement with the European Union, triggering the Euromaidan protests in Kyiv’s Maidan Nezalezhnosti (Independence Square). Demonstrations calling for government accountability and European integration grew into a broader uprising. In February 2014, security forces’ violent crackdown led to Yanukovych’s flight to Russia and his removal by the parliament. Russia responded by annexing Crimea in March 2014 and supporting separatist movements in the Donetsk and Luhansk regions, igniting a conflict that persisted in the Donbas.

**Post-2014 Political Landscape**
Petro Poroshenko, elected president in May 2014, pursued constitutional decentralization, anti-corruption measures, and NATO partnership. However, reform implementation was uneven. The 2019 election brought Volodymyr Zelenskyy—a political outsider and former comedian—to the presidency on a platform of anti-corruption and peace negotiations. Zelenskyy’s Servant of the People party secured a parliamentary majority, enabling ambitious legislative agendas aimed at judicial reform, asset recovery, and economic modernization.

**2022 Russian Invasion**
On 24 February 2022, Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine, escalating the conflict to a national emergency. Ukrainian forces, bolstered by widespread civilian mobilization and international military aid, mounted staunch resistance. The invasion prompted unprecedented Western sanctions on Russia, historic financial and military support for Ukraine, and reinforced Kyiv’s Euro-Atlantic aspirations. As of 2025, the war remains unresolved, with frontline stalemates, ongoing negotiations, and significant humanitarian and economic impacts.

**Government and Politics**
Ukraine is a unitary state with a semi-presidential system. Executive power is shared between the president—head of state with authority over foreign policy and defense—and the prime minister, who heads the government and domestic policy. Legislative power resides in the unicameral Verkhovna Rada, comprising 450 members elected for five-year terms through a mixed electoral system. The judiciary, headed by the Supreme Court and Constitutional Court, is constitutionally independent, though historically influenced by political pressures. Major political forces include pro-European reformists and parties favoring rapprochement with Russia, reflecting enduring east–west societal divides.

**Administrative Divisions**
Ukraine is divided into 24 oblasts (provinces), one autonomous republic (Crimea, currently under Russian occupation and internationally recognized as Ukrainian territory), and two cities with special status (Kyiv and Sevastopol). The 2014 Donbas conflict led to de facto loss of Ukrainian control over parts of Donetsk and Luhansk oblasts, complicating governance and humanitarian access.

**Foreign Relations**
Ukraine’s foreign policy is anchored in Euro-Atlantic integration. The country signed an Association Agreement and Deep and Comprehensive Free Trade Area with the EU in 2014 and has pursued NATO partnership through the Membership Action Plan framework. Relations with Russia remain adversarial due to the 2014 annexation of Crimea and ongoing Donbas conflict. Ukraine is an active member of the United Nations, Council of Europe, and Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe.

**Economy and Reforms**
Ukraine’s economy transitioned from a centrally planned system to a market-oriented model, emphasizing agriculture, heavy industry, and information technology. Reforms since 2014 have targeted corruption, energy sector liberalization, and legal transparency. Despite robust GDP growth rates in the mid-2010s, the economy suffered setbacks due to war-related disruptions, infrastructure damage, and population displacement. International financial institutions, including the IMF and World Bank, have provided loans conditioned on structural reforms.

**Current Challenges**
Ukraine’s future depends on successful implementation of anti-corruption measures, judicial independence, and war resolution. The conflict has galvanized national identity and reform consensus but poses enduring security, economic, and humanitarian challenges. As Ukraine continues to navigate its path between East and West, its governance model and resilience under crisis remain focal points of regional and global attention.
