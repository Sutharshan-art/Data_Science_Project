**Iraqi Armed Forces**

The Iraqi Armed Forces (IAF) are the unified military services of the Republic of Iraq, encompassing the Iraqi Army, Iraqi Navy, and Iraqi Air Force, along with ancillary forces such as the Popular Mobilization Forces. Established during the British Mandate period, the IAF has undergone multiple transformations—under monarchy, military rule, Ba‘th Party dictatorship, and post-2003 democratic governance—reflecting Iraq’s shifting political landscape and regional security challenges.

---

### Origins and Monarchical Period (1921–1958)

In the aftermath of World War I and the collapse of the Ottoman Empire, Britain established a mandate over modern-day Iraq. In 1921, the Kingdom of Iraq was created under King Faisal I, and the Iraqi Army was formed by integrating former Ottoman soldiers with British-trained recruits. Equipped and officered initially by British advisers, the army suppressed internal revolts and maintained order under monarchical rule. The Royal Iraqi Air Force and fledgling naval units on the Tigris and Euphrates rivers were added in the 1930s, but budget constraints limited their size.

---

### Military Coups and Republican Era (1958–1968)

The Free Officers’ coup of 1958 overthrew the monarchy and proclaimed a republic. Successive regimes—led by generals such as Abd al-Karim Qasim and Abdel Salam Aref—nationalized the armed forces. Political purges and competing factional loyalties fragmented cohesion. The Iraqi Navy was expanded modestly with patrol boats to secure waterways, while the Air Force acquired Soviet MiG-15s and later MiG-21s, marking Iraq’s Cold War alignment shift toward the Soviet bloc.

---

### Ba‘thist Consolidation and Expansion (1968–1980)

After the Ba‘th Party seized power in 1968, Saddam Hussein rose through the party ranks to become president in 1979. Under Ba‘thist rule, the IAF underwent dramatic expansion and modernization, fueled by oil revenues. The army grew to over half a million troops organized into armored, mechanized, and motorized infantry divisions. The Air Force acquired Western Mirage F1s and Soviet Sukhoi and MiG aircraft, while the Navy ordered fast attack craft and missile boats to secure the Persian Gulf. Political reliability was ensured through Ba‘th Party cells embedded at all command levels.

---

### Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988)

In September 1980, Iraq invaded Iran, triggering an eight-year war. The Iraqi military initially achieved rapid advances but soon became mired in protracted frontline attrition. The army and air force conducted combined arms offensives and air strikes against Iranian targets. Chemical weapons deployment by Iraq and extensive Soviet and French arms imports sustained its war effort. Despite heavy casualties, Iraq emerged with its pre-war borders intact, but the conflict exposed weaknesses in training, logistics, and command under sustained combat conditions.

---

### Gulf War and Sanctions (1990–2003)

Iraq’s 1990 invasion of Kuwait provoked a U.S.-led coalition intervention. Operation Desert Storm (January–February 1991) decimated Iraqi armored and air units, leaving infrastructure and command centers in ruins. Post-war United Nations sanctions, no-fly zones, and weapons embargoes prevented reconstitution of advanced capabilities. The army was reduced in size, the air force grounded most combat aircraft, and the navy’s maritime role was curtailed. Throughout the 1990s, periodic uprisings by Kurdish and Shia groups were suppressed by ground forces and helicopter gunships.

---

### Disbandment and Reformation (2003–2006)

Following the U.S.-led invasion in March 2003 and the fall of Saddam Hussein’s regime, Coalition Provisional Authority Order 2 formally disbanded the IAF, dissolving command structures and retiring personnel. Reconstruction began amid widespread insurgency. In 2004 the Iraqi Interim Government reestablished new security institutions under Ministry of Defense and Ministry of Interior oversight. Recruitment, training, and vetting programs—often overseen by U.S. and UK advisers—produced the modern Iraqi Army and Air Force. A nascent navy began patrolling coastal waters and the southern ports.

---

### Modern Structure and Coalition Partnership (2006–2014)

By 2006, the Iraqi Army comprised several corps aligned with geographic regions, integrating national, regional, and tribal militias into official brigades. The Iraqi Air Force operated a small fleet of helicopters and light attack aircraft, gradually expanding to include L-159 Alca jets and C-130 transport planes. The Navy, based at Umm Qasr and Basra, focused on maritime interdiction and coastal patrol. The Popular Mobilization Forces (PMF), sanctioned in 2014 to counter the Islamic State (IS), were formally integrated under the Prime Minister’s command.

---

### Counter-IS Operations and Security Transition (2014–2020)

The 2014 seizure of large swathes of northern and western Iraq by IS prompted a national mobilization. Iraqi Army units, supported by PMF, Peshmerga, and international coalition air strikes, recaptured key cities including Mosul (2017) and Fallujah (2016). The counter-IS campaign tested interoperability, logistics, and command resilience. By December 2017, Iraq declared victory over IS, and responsibility for internal security shifted back to Iraqi forces. In 2020, the last U.S. combat troops departed, leaving advisory elements.

---

### Present-Day Roles and Challenges

Today, the Iraqi Armed Forces number approximately 200,000 active-duty personnel across its army, air force, navy, and PMF contingents. Their mandates include border security, counter-terrorism, internal stability, and defense of sovereignty. Key challenges include modernizing equipment from multiple sources (U.S., Russia, Iran), addressing corruption and political interference, improving training and readiness, and forging cohesion among ethnically and sectarian-diverse units. Ongoing reforms aim to strengthen professional military education, enhance logistics, and integrate PMF units into the formal chain of command.

---

### Political Significance

The Iraqi Armed Forces have been central to the country’s political evolution—from instruments of authoritarian control under the monarchy and Ba‘thist dictatorship to participants in democratization and national defense. Their performance in major conflicts has shaped regional power balances, domestic legitimacy, and Iraq’s role as a pivotal state in the Middle East. As Iraq continues to rebuild and confront emerging threats, the evolution of its armed forces remains closely tied to broader processes of state-building and political reconciliation.
