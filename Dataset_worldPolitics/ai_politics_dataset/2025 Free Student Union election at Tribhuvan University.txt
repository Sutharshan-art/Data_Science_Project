**2025 Free Student Union Election at Tribhuvan University**

The **2025 Free Student Union (FSU) election** at **Tribhuvan University**, Nepal’s oldest and largest higher education institution, was held on **18 March 2025**, with votes extending into late April in some regions. The election, delayed for several years, occurred across **approximately 462 campuses** (62 constituent and 400 affiliated community colleges) following an institutional hiatus and rampant factional conflict. The FSU serves as the democratic representative body for university students, often reflecting broader national political alignments.

---

### Historical and Political Context

Established in 1969, TU’s FSU elections were intended to occur biennially. However, municipal unrest, national political instability, and factional disputes halted elections from 2009 until 2025. During this period, student organizations—typically aligned with major national political parties—played key roles in democratic movements and campus governance.

A mixed electoral system was introduced in 2017. Presidential, secretary, treasurer, and select executive members are elected using a **first‑past‑the‑post (FPTP)** format, while vice president, joint secretary, and additional members are chosen through **proportional representation (PR)**. Candidate age is capped at 28. In early 2025, the **Student Welfare Council**, under the university rector’s leadership, officially scheduled the election, moving the date due to internal disputes and scheduling conflicts with exams.

### Major Student Organizations

Key political student groups contesting included:

* **Nepal Student Union (NSU)** – Affiliated with the Nepali Congress.
* **All Nepal National Free Students Union (ANNFSU)** – Linked to the Communist Party of Nepal (Unified Marxist–Leninist).
* **All Nepal National Independent Students Union (Revolutionary) \[ANNISU(R)]** – Tied to the Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist Centre).
* Other affiliates: ANFSU (Unified Socialist), Socialist Students Union, National Democratic Student Organisation, and various independents.

These organizations often formed coalitions—such as joint panels between ANNFSU and ANNISU(R)—to consolidate votes and counter rivals.

### Election Process and Turnout

The election unfolded in several stages over March and April. Of 62 constituent campuses, 47 completed voting by early April, while the remaining went ahead following university directives or court orders. Some campuses experienced delays due to court interventions or disruptions spurred by internal factional disputes and protests.

Voter turnout varied widely. At major Kathmandu Valley campuses, participation ranged from **15% to 45%**, with a university-wide turnout estimated between **45% and 68%**. Approximately **65%** of registered students on 300 affiliated campuses were reported to have voted. The disparity highlighted local discord and widespread apathy.

### Notable Campus Outcomes

A mix of consensus and contested results emerged:

* **Competition at Central Campus, TU**: NSU's Deepak Raj Joshi narrowly won the presidency with 789 votes, defeating ANNFSU and ANNISU(R) candidates by slim margins. The contest underlined intense rivalry among major groups.
* **Prithvi Narayan Campus (Pokhara)**: ANNFSU reclaimed leadership with candidate Bishnu Pokhrel, winning by a notable margin, signaling support for UML-aligned activism in historically conservative districts.
* **Independent victories**: Some regional colleges—such as Biratnagar’s engineering campus—elected independent candidates supported informally by factions, reflecting local dynamics beyond national affiliations.
* **Election postponements**: On 15 constituent campuses—including significant ones like Nepal Law Campus—Elections were delayed by padlocking and disputes over voter's lists and candidate eligibility. Outcomes were finalized days to weeks later following high court orders.

At **82 campuses**, including vote-free consensus campuses, candidates were elected unopposed, with ANNFSU, NSU, and ANNISU(R) claiming leadership across different regions through negotiation and power-sharing.

### Political and Institutional Significance

The 2025 FSU elections marked a pivotal return of student democracy at Tribhuvan University. The reintroduction of elections after a 16-year hiatus underscored the resilience of student political structures and mirrored ongoing political contests in Nepal. Nationally, senior leaders, including former prime ministers, noted the election results as indicative of youth sentiment on issues like corruption, ideological purity, and party unity.

The mixed FPTP-PR system produced diverse leadership outcomes, ensuring representation of both mainstream and smaller student groups. While traditional party-based unions continued to dominate, independent and cross-party coalitions posed viable alternatives at select campuses.

### Challenges and Controversies

The election saw several challenges:

1. **Low turnout and apathy**: Apathy among students, particularly at urban campuses, raised concern over waning political engagement.
2. **Delays and disputes**: Campaign violence, padlocked campuses, and internal party rivalries disrupted polling schedules and required judicial intervention.
3. **Election irregularities**: Allegations of fake IDs and voter list manipulation led to controversies, protests, and re-polling in certain regions.
4. **Administrative constraints**: Balancing normal academic schedules with election logistics proved difficult, prompting university-level calls for process reforms.

### Aftermath and Legacy

Following the elections, FSU executives assumed office to serve until 2027, tasked with representing student interests in university governance, academic policy, and extracurricular activities. The election set priority issues for subsequent student councils: infrastructure improvements, quality of education, campus facilities, and academic administration.

Politically, the election results were seen as a barometer of youth opinion and a proving ground for national parties. Calls for election reforms—longer campaigning periods, enhanced security, and voter education—emerged from both students and alumni, aiming to strengthen future student democratic processes.

The 2025 FSU election at Tribhuvan University thus represented a critical revival of student voice and organizational activity in Nepal’s higher education system, highlighting the enduring intersection of campus politics and national ideological trends.
