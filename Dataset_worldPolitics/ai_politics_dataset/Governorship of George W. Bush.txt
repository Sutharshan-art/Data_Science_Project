**Governorship of George W. Bush**

**George W. Bush** served as the 46th Governor of Texas from January 17, 1995, to December 21, 2000, before resigning to assume the U.S. presidency. His tenure was marked by efforts to reform public education, reduce taxes, and implement tort‐reform measures, set against the backdrop of Texas’s shift toward Republican dominance and the broader national “Republican Revolution” of the mid‐1990s.

**Election and Political Environment**
In 1994, Bush—then a political newcomer and first‐term Republican lieutenant governor candidate—capitalized on statewide dissatisfaction with long‐entrenched Democratic control and concerns over property taxes and education quality. He won the gubernatorial election with 53.5 percent of the vote, defeating Democratic Attorney General Ann Richards. His victory coincided with a national Republican wave that unseated many congressional Democrats, reflecting voter appetite for conservative fiscal policies and governmental accountability.

**First Term (1995–1999)**
Bush’s inaugural term focused on three principal reforms:

1. **Property Tax Reduction**

   * Tasked with addressing high rates driven by school district funding, Bush championed a \$2.5 billion “tax swap” in 1997. The measure cut local school property‐tax rates by one cent per dollar of assessed value, offset by increases in state sales and business taxes. The legislation passed narrowly in a special legislative session and survived a legal challenge, lowering reliance on local property taxes and shifting the tax burden toward consumption.

2. **Education Reform**

   * Building on the **1995 “Robin Hood”** school‐finance plan—which redistributed state aid from wealthier to poorer districts—Bush sought to improve accountability and standards. He signed the **Texas Assessment of Academic Skills** (TAAS) into law in 1994, requiring students to pass standardized tests to advance grades. In 1997, he secured legislation creating the **Texas Reading Initiative**, funding early‐grade literacy programs. Bush emphasized parental choice through charter schools and home‐schooling support, positioning Texas as a leader in decentralized education models.

3. **Tort Reform**

   * Confronted with rising medical‐malpractice insurance premiums and business liability concerns, Bush backed the **Tort Reform Act of 1995**, which capped non‐economic damages in medical‐malpractice cases and curtailed joint‐and‐several liability. The law aimed to lower health‐care costs and encourage physician retention in rural areas. While business groups applauded the changes, consumer advocates argued that damage caps limited access to justice for injured parties.

**Second Term (1999–2000)**
Re‐elected in November 1998 with 68 percent of the vote against Democratic State Senator Garry Mauro, Bush’s second term refined earlier initiatives and addressed new challenges:

1. **Continued Education Emphasis**

   * Bush signed the **Complete Communities Initiative** (1999), expanding charter‐school autonomy and accelerating the development of high‐performing campuses. He also increased funding for technology in schools and raised teacher salaries, aiming to attract qualified educators to underserved districts.

2. **Criminal Justice and Public Safety**

   * Responding to concerns over violent crime, Bush supported legislation strengthening sentencing guidelines and expanding the Texas Department of Criminal Justice’s capacity. He signed laws creating “three‐strikes” mandates for repeat violent offenders and allocating funds for prison construction. Critics warned of prison overcrowding and questioned the long‐term efficacy of tougher sentencing alone.

3. **Economic Development and Energy Policy**

   * Leveraging Texas’s booming economy, Bush promoted free‐market principles to attract business investment. He endorsed deregulation efforts in the telecommunications and electricity sectors, aiming to lower consumer prices through competition. The **Texas Workforce Commission** received expanded authority to oversee job training and unemployment benefits, reflecting Bush’s belief in private‐sector–driven growth.

**Political Style and Leadership**
Bush cultivated a centrist‐conservative persona branded as “compassionate conservatism.” He emphasized service through volunteerism—launching statewide **Points of Light** initiatives—and maintained close ties with religious and civic organizations. His administrative style favored bipartisan outreach: he worked with Democratic legislators on education and infrastructure projects, though he often relied on his veto power to check bills he deemed fiscally irresponsible.

**Resignation and Legacy**
On December 21, 2000, Bush resigned as governor to prepare for his inauguration as President of the United States. His tenure left a mixed legacy:

* **Successes**: Property‐tax relief, education accountability, and tort‐reform measures were widely cited as positive reforms that contributed to Texas’s economic growth and improved standardized‐test performance.
* **Criticisms**: Opponents argued that the tax swap shifted burdens onto low‐income consumers, that education reforms led to “teaching to the test,” and that criminal‐justice policies inflated the state’s prison population without addressing underlying social issues.

Bush’s governorship influenced national policy debates, serving as a credential for his presidential campaign in 2000. His “compassionate conservatism” approach prefigured the policy platforms he would advance as president, while Texas’s economic expansion under his administration became a central theme of his presidential stump speeches.
