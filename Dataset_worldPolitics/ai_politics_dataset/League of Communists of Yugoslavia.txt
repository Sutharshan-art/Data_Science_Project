**League of Communists of Yugoslavia** (Serbo-Croatian: Savez komunista Jugoslavije, SKJ; Slovene: Zveza komunistov Jugoslavije, ZKJ) was the ruling and only legal party of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia from 1945 until its dissolution in 1990. Evolving from the pre-war Communist Party of Yugoslavia (CPY), it presided over Yugoslavia’s transition from royal dictatorship to socialist federation, guided post-war reconstruction, and managed the delicate balance between centralized authority and republican autonomy that ultimately unraveled at the end of the Cold War.

**Origins and Early History**
Founded in 1919 as the Communist Party of Yugoslavia, the organization was banned in 1921 under the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes’ Obznana decree. Forced underground, the CPY maintained clandestine cells and mobilized workers’ and peasant movements throughout the interwar period. During World War II, under the leadership of Josip Broz Tito, the party organized the Yugoslav Partisans—Europe’s most effective resistance movement—earning popular legitimacy and Soviet recognition at the Tehran Conference (1943).

**Establishment of Socialist Yugoslavia**
In the aftermath of the war, the CPY consolidated power through a series of coalitions and popular front governments, culminating in the 1945 proclamation of the Federal People’s Republic of Yugoslavia. Purges of non-communist collaborators and rival claimants secured the party’s monopoly on political life. Reconstruction policy, collectivization drives, and heavy industrialization were implemented along Stalinist lines until 1948, when doctrinal and personal clashes with Joseph Stalin led to the Yugoslav–Soviet split.

**Transformation into the League of Communists**
At the Sixth Congress in 1952, the CPY renamed itself the League of Communists of Yugoslavia (LCY/SKJ) to signal a move away from rigid Stalinism toward a more pluralistic party structure. The congress abolished the Russian-style Politburo in favor of a broader Presidium and renamed “party cells” as “basic organizations,” reflecting a commitment to workers’ self-management.

**Organizational Structure and Federal–Republican Balance**
The SKJ was organized on democratic-centralist principles but operated through six republican branches—League of Communists of Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro, and Macedonia—plus two autonomous provincial committees in Serbia (Vojvodina and Kosovo). Each branch elected its own leadership and delegates to the federal SKJ Congress, creating a delicate power-sharing arrangement that balanced republican interests against federal authority in Belgrade.

**Tito–Stalin Split and Non-Alignment**
Expelled from the Cominform in 1948, Yugoslavia under Tito charted an independent socialist path. The SKJ pioneered the doctrine of workers’ self-management, granting firms and enterprises decision-making autonomy. In foreign affairs, it co-founded the Non-Aligned Movement in 1961, positioning Yugoslavia as a leader of Third World and neutralist states during the Cold War.

**Ideological Evolution and Self-Management**
Throughout the 1950s and 1960s, the SKJ promoted “socialist self-management” (samoupravljanje), decentralizing economic control to workers’ councils and devolving certain functions to the republics. Political debates at successive congresses addressed market mechanisms, agricultural reform, and the relationship between party oversight and enterprise autonomy. The 1965 economic reforms introduced limited market pricing and greater mobility of capital between republics.

**1974 Constitution and Decentralization**
Enshrined at the Ninth SKJ Congress and promulgated as the Yugoslav Constitution of 1974, extensive decentralization granted each republic and autonomous province near-sovereign status over economic and cultural affairs. The collective Presidency replaced the single-party General Secretary with a rotating chairmanship among representatives of the six republics and two provinces. While intended to manage ethnic diversity and prevent power monopolies, this structure weakened federal cohesion and limited the SKJ’s ability to enforce uniform policy.

**Decline and Dissolution**
Economic crises of the late 1970s—marked by rising foreign debt, inflation, and unemployment—fueled popular dissatisfaction. Calls for stronger republican control mounted, especially in Slovenia and Croatia. After Tito’s death in 1980, factional struggles intensified over austerity versus reform and the status of Kosovo. At its 14th and final Congress in January 1990, the SKJ failed to agree on a common platform, and delegates from Slovenia and Croatia walked out. Without party unity, multicandidate elections ensued, and the SKJ formally dissolved itself in October 1990.

**Legacy**
The League of Communists of Yugoslavia’s experiment with self-management and non-alignment offered a distinct model of socialism independent of Soviet hegemony. Its eventual unraveling presaged the violent dissolution of Yugoslavia and remains a subject of study for scholars of federalism, ethnic politics, and alternative socialist governance. While praised for its resistance legacy and early social and economic gains, critics fault the SKJ for its later inability to reconcile centralized authority with republican aspirations, setting the stage for the conflicts of the 1990s.
