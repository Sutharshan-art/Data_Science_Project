**Soviet of the Union**

The **Soviet of the Union** (Russian: *Совет Союза*, *Sovet Soyuza*) was one of the two chambers of the **Supreme Soviet of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR)**, serving as the lower house of the country’s highest legislative body from **1938 until the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991**. It was established as part of the Soviet constitutional reforms that created a more formalized and structured legislature, with the stated goal of representing the Soviet people on a population basis, distinct from the territorial representation of the **Soviet of Nationalities**, the upper chamber.

Although constitutionally designed to play a significant legislative role, in practice the Soviet of the Union—like the broader Supreme Soviet—functioned largely as a rubber-stamp institution for decisions made by the **Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU)**. Nevertheless, it was a key element of the Soviet Union’s official government structure and a platform for political appointments, propaganda, and legislative formalities.

---

### Origins and Constitutional Role

The Soviet of the Union was formally established under the **1936 Soviet Constitution**, also known as the **Stalin Constitution**, which replaced the Congress of Soviets with a new bicameral legislature: the **Supreme Soviet of the USSR**. This body consisted of:

* **The Soviet of the Union**, elected on the basis of equal population representation.
* **The Soviet of Nationalities**, representing the various ethnic and territorial divisions of the USSR (e.g., union republics, autonomous republics, autonomous oblasts).

The Soviet of the Union was designed to ensure that all Soviet citizens were equally represented regardless of their nationality or region. Members were elected from equally populated electoral districts, unlike the Soviet of Nationalities, which allocated representation based on administrative divisions.

---

### Composition and Election

Deputies to the Soviet of the Union were elected by direct universal suffrage in elections that were held every four to five years. In theory, the deputies were chosen by the people in competitive elections, but in practice, candidates were pre-selected and endorsed by the Communist Party or affiliated mass organizations. Prior to 1989, the **Communist Party** maintained a constitutional monopoly on political power, and all deputies were expected to follow the party line.

The number of deputies varied over time in accordance with population changes. At its height, the Soviet of the Union had around **750 to 1,500 members**, with the exact number determined by law before each election.

---

### Powers and Functions

According to the 1936 and 1977 Soviet Constitutions, the Soviet of the Union had equal legislative powers to the Soviet of Nationalities. Both chambers had to pass legislation jointly, and either could initiate or propose laws. The chamber’s key powers included:

* Approving legislation proposed by the **Presidium** or by deputies.
* Confirming or amending the state budget and national economic plans.
* Discussing and voting on foreign policy, defense, and internal matters.
* Approving constitutional amendments (jointly with the Soviet of Nationalities).

The Soviet of the Union also elected a chairman, standing commissions, and participated in the election of members of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet and other high offices.

In reality, most legislation was drafted and finalized by the Communist Party’s **Politburo** and the **Council of Ministers** before being submitted to the Supreme Soviet for approval. Debates were rare, and laws were typically passed unanimously or with minimal opposition.

---

### Decline and Reforms

Under **Mikhail Gorbachev**, significant reforms were introduced to the Soviet political system during the **glasnost** and **perestroika** era. The 1988 constitutional amendments created the **Congress of People's Deputies**, a larger body that indirectly elected a restructured Supreme Soviet with a more professional and permanent character.

The Soviet of the Union continued to function as part of the reformed Supreme Soviet, but its composition was now drawn from the elected Congress rather than direct elections. Political pluralism was allowed for the first time, and debates within the chamber became more open and at times contentious.

The legislative landscape shifted dramatically after the failed **August 1991 coup** and the weakening of the Communist Party’s control. The Soviet of the Union, along with other central institutions of the USSR, lost authority as republics declared independence and moved to establish their own national parliaments.

---

### Dissolution

The Soviet of the Union held its final sessions in late 1991 as the USSR disintegrated. On **26 December 1991**, the **Supreme Soviet of the USSR**, including both the Soviet of the Union and the Soviet of Nationalities, formally voted to dissolve itself following the creation of the **Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS)** and the resignation of **Mikhail Gorbachev** as President of the USSR.

The chamber’s dissolution marked the end of the Soviet Union’s formal legislative system and the transfer of sovereignty to the constituent republics, including the newly independent **Russian Federation**, which inherited the USSR’s international legal identity.

---

### Legacy

The Soviet of the Union symbolized the centralizing and hierarchical nature of Soviet governance, where formal institutions often existed to legitimize decisions already made by the Communist Party leadership. While largely ceremonial for most of its existence, it reflected the USSR’s attempt to portray itself as a constitutional and democratic state, particularly through the rhetoric of equal representation and legislative procedures.

In post-Soviet historical discourse, the Soviet of the Union is often viewed as a part of the broader façade of democratic governance in the Soviet state—an institution with significant theoretical powers but limited practical autonomy. Nonetheless, its brief period of openness in the late 1980s provided a forum for early reformist voices and political debate during the USSR’s final years.
