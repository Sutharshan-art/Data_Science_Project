**History of Soviet Russia and the Soviet Union (1917–1927)**

The period from 1917 to 1927 encompasses the revolutionary overthrow of the Russian Provisional Government, the establishment of Soviet Russia, the Russian Civil War, the creation of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR), and the consolidation of Bolshevik rule under the Communist Party. Marked by ideological experimentation, economic crisis, and intense political struggle, these years laid the institutional and doctrinal foundations of the world’s first socialist state.

---

## February and October Revolutions (1917)

In February 1917, mass protests in Petrograd (Saint Petersburg)—fueled by food shortages and war fatigue—forced Tsar Nicholas II’s abdication. A dual power structure emerged between the liberal–socialist Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet of Workers’ and Soldiers’ Deputies. Bolshevik leader Vladimir Lenin’s April Theses, calling for “all power to the Soviets” and an immediate end to World War I, gained traction amid continued hardship. On 25–26 October (Julian calendar), Bolshevik-led forces seized key points in Petrograd, toppling the Provisional Government and inaugurating Soviet rule.

---

## Establishment of Soviet Russia and Early Policies

Following the October insurrection, the All-Russian Congress of Soviets recognized the Council of People’s Commissars (Sovnarkom) as the new government. Decrees nationalized land, transferred factories to workers, and withdrew Russia from the war via the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918), ceding vast territories. The Bolsheviks (Communists) faced immediate opposition from monarchists, liberals, and socialists disaffected by one-party rule, setting the stage for civil conflict.

---

## Civil War and War Communism (1918–1921)

A multi-front Civil War pitted the Red Army against White Army forces, foreign interventionists (British, French, American, Japanese), and regional nationalists. To sustain the war effort, the Bolsheviks implemented **War Communism**, requisitioning grain, nationalizing industry, and enforcing labor discipline. Severe shortages and famine (1919–21) provoked peasant rebellions—most notably the Tambov uprising—and discontent in industrial centers. Nevertheless, centralized command and the Cheka secret police enabled the Red Army to defeat fragmented opponents by 1921.

---

## Creation of the Soviet Union (1922)

In December 1922, the Treaty on the Creation of the USSR united the Russian, Ukrainian, Byelorussian, and Transcaucasian Soviet republics into a federal union. The new constitution established a bicameral legislature (Supreme Soviet of the USSR) and enshrined the Communist Party’s monopoly on power. Lenin, as chairman of the Council of People’s Commissars of the RSFSR, also presided over the USSR’s first government, emphasizing unity amid ethnic diversity and pledging support for revolutionary movements abroad.

---

## New Economic Policy (NEP) and Economic Stabilization

Recognizing the unsustainability of War Communism, the 10th Party Congress (1921) introduced the **New Economic Policy** (NEP), relegalizing small-scale private trade and leasing to stimulate agricultural and industrial production. While the state retained control over “commanding heights” (heavy industry, banking, foreign trade), NEP’s market-oriented measures reversed economic collapse: grain procurement stabilized, urban food supplies improved, and industrial output partially recovered by mid-1920s. NEP fostered a “mixed economy” under party oversight, reflecting tactical retreat from immediate socialist transformation.

---

## Political Consolidation and Internal Party Struggles

Between 1922 and 1927, Lenin’s health declined, and power struggles emerged among Bolshevik leaders. After Lenin’s death in January 1924, a collective leadership formed among Joseph Stalin (General Secretary), Leon Trotsky (People’s Commissar for Military and Naval Affairs), and Nikolai Bukharin, among others. Stalin leveraged his bureaucratic position to build a loyal base, promoting “socialism in one country” against Trotsky’s push for permanent international revolution. By the Fourteenth Party Congress (December 1925), Stalin’s faction prevailed, expelling Trotsky and Left Oppositionists and marginalizing NEP critics.

---

## Cultural and Social Developments

The early Soviet state embarked on ambitious campaigns of literacy, secularization, and gender equality. Mass education and the Romanization of minority scripts accompanied efforts to create a proletarian culture, supported by state theaters, schools, and youth organizations (Young Pioneers). Simultaneously, Soviet nationality policy granted limited cultural autonomy to non-Russian republics, while promoting Russian as the lingua franca of the union. These measures sought to remake society in accordance with Marxist-Leninist principles, though tensions between centralization and regional identities persisted.

---

## Transition to Centralized Planning

By 1927, Stalin’s consolidation signaled an end to NEP’s market elements. The Fifteenth Party Congress (December 1927) condemned the remaining Right Opposition, endorsing accelerated industrialization and collectivization. Although large-scale five-year plans and forced agricultural collectivization would commence after 1928, the ideological and institutional groundwork was laid during the previous decade of turbulence and policy experimentation.

---

In the decade following 1917, Soviet Russia and the early USSR navigated revolutionary upheaval, civil war, economic collapse, and political factionalism. The establishment of the Soviet state, the pragmatic NEP interlude, and Stalin’s rise to dominance set the trajectory for the USSR’s subsequent evolution into a centrally planned superpower.
