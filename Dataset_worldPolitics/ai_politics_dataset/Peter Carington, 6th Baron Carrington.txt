**Peter Carington, 6th Baron Carrington**

**Peter Alexander Rupert Carington, 6th Baron Carrington** (6 June 1919 – 9 July 2018), known as Lord Carrington, was a British Conservative politician, diplomat, and hereditary peer who held several prominent roles in British and international politics during the 20th century. Notably, he served as **Foreign Secretary**, **Defence Secretary**, **Leader of the House of Lords**, and **Secretary General of NATO**, earning a reputation for diplomatic skill, integrity, and public service.

A seasoned statesman, Carrington played a key role in major international negotiations, including the **Lancaster House Conference** that led to Zimbabwean independence and the **Falklands crisis**, which precipitated his resignation as Foreign Secretary in 1982. His long career spanned military service, domestic politics, and international diplomacy.

---

### Early Life and Military Service

Peter Carington was born into the British aristocracy on 6 June 1919. He succeeded to the barony at age 19 upon the death of his father, Rupert Carington, 5th Baron Carrington. Educated at **Eton College** and the **Royal Military College, Sandhurst**, he was commissioned into the **Grenadier Guards** and served during the Second World War, participating in campaigns across Europe, including in the Netherlands, where he was decorated for bravery.

---

### Entry into Politics

Carrington entered politics following World War II, taking his seat in the **House of Lords** in 1945 as a hereditary peer. He became involved in the Conservative Party and was appointed **Parliamentary Secretary to the Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries** under Prime Minister Winston Churchill in 1951.

He held several junior ministerial positions during the 1950s, including **Parliamentary Secretary to the Ministry of Defence**, and became **High Commissioner to Australia** from 1956 to 1959, gaining valuable diplomatic experience.

---

### Senior Cabinet Roles

Carrington returned to Britain in 1959 and was soon appointed **First Lord of the Admiralty** under Prime Minister Harold Macmillan. During this period, he oversaw parts of the modernization of the Royal Navy. He later served as **Leader of the House of Lords** and **Chairman of the Conservative Party** under Edward Heath, helping to navigate party politics during a period of economic difficulty and trade union unrest.

From 1970 to 1974, Carrington served as **Secretary of State for Defence**, during which time he dealt with rising tensions in Northern Ireland and ongoing issues of military reform and spending.

---

### Foreign Secretary (1979–1982)

Carrington’s most prominent domestic role came under Prime Minister **Margaret Thatcher**, who appointed him **Secretary of State for Foreign and Commonwealth Affairs** in 1979. As Foreign Secretary, Carrington demonstrated strong diplomatic acumen and was widely respected for his calm demeanor and pragmatic approach.

One of his major achievements was chairing the **Lancaster House Conference** in 1979, which brought an end to the protracted conflict in **Rhodesia** (now Zimbabwe) and paved the way for internationally recognized independence under a new government led by **Robert Mugabe**.

However, Carrington’s tenure ended in 1982 following the surprise invasion of the **Falkland Islands** by Argentina. Accepting responsibility for intelligence and diplomatic failings that may have contributed to the crisis, Carrington resigned—an act widely praised for its honor and accountability, although many argued he had been unfairly scapegoated.

---

### Secretary General of NATO

Following his resignation, Carrington remained influential in global affairs. In 1984, he was appointed **Secretary General of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO)**, a position he held until 1988. During this time, he worked to maintain alliance unity during the latter stages of the Cold War, especially amid debates over nuclear weapons deployment in Europe and relations with the Soviet Union.

His tenure coincided with the presidency of Ronald Reagan and the early reforms of Mikhail Gorbachev, during which Carrington emphasized the importance of deterrence and dialogue.

---

### Later Life and Legacy

After retiring from NATO, Carrington remained active in diplomacy and conflict resolution. He chaired the **Bosnian peace negotiations** on behalf of the European Community in the early 1990s, though initial efforts were ultimately unsuccessful due to the outbreak of full-scale war in the Balkans.

Carrington was regarded as a statesman of the old school—pragmatic, principled, and understated. He was **Chancellor of the Order of St Michael and St George** and received numerous honors, including appointments to the **Order of the Garter** and **Order of Merit**.

He passed away on 9 July 2018 at the age of 99, as the last surviving member of Churchill’s post-war government and the last former British Foreign Secretary to have held the post in the House of Lords.

---

### Political Significance

Lord Carrington’s career exemplified mid-20th-century British diplomacy and conservatism. He was a bridge between the imperial past and postwar multilateralism, balancing loyalty to British interests with a strong belief in international cooperation. His resignation after the Falklands invasion became a model of political accountability, and his work on Rhodesia and at NATO left a lasting impact on international relations.
