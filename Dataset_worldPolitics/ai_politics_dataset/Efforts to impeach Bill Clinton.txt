**Efforts to Impeach Bill Clinton**

Efforts to impeach William Jefferson “Bill” Clinton, the 42nd President of the United States (1993–2001), culminated in 1998 when the House of Representatives adopted two articles of impeachment against him. The proceedings marked only the second presidential impeachment in U.S. history, following Andrew Johnson in 1868. The movement to remove Clinton from office was driven by allegations of perjury, obstruction of justice, and abuse of power arising from both personal misconduct and broader investigations into the Clinton administration.

## Political and Legal Background

The impetus for impeachment efforts began in 1994 with Republican gains in the midterm elections, which gave the GOP control of both chambers of Congress for the first time in four decades. Under the leadership of House Speaker Newt Gingrich and House Judiciary Committee Chairman Henry Hyde, Republicans pursued oversight of the Clinton administration, focusing initially on alleged ethical lapses and campaign finance irregularities.

### Whitewater and Independent Counsel Investigations

In 1994, the Department of Justice appointed Independent Counsel Robert B. Fiske, later succeeded by Kenneth W. Starr, to investigate the Whitewater real estate investments of the Clintons and their associates. Over several years, Starr’s probe expanded to cover the suicide of deputy White House counsel Vince Foster, the firing of White House travel office employees, alleged misuse of FBI files (Travelgate and Filegate), and, critically, President Clinton’s relationship with White House intern Monica Lewinsky.

## Monica Lewinsky Scandal and Starr Report

In January 1998, news of a sexual relationship between President Clinton and Lewinsky became public. Clinton initially denied the affair under oath in a deposition related to Paula Jones’s sexual harassment lawsuit. In September 1998, Independent Counsel Starr submitted a 445-page **Starr Report** to Congress, detailing evidence that Clinton had lied under oath and attempted to influence Lewinsky and others to conceal their relationship. The report concluded that Clinton’s conduct constituted grounds for impeachment under constitutional provisions for “high crimes and misdemeanors.”

## Congressional Impeachment Proceedings

### House Judiciary Committee Inquiry

On October 8, 1998, the House Judiciary Committee voted 21–16 along party lines to launch a formal impeachment inquiry. The committee conducted public hearings from October to December 1998, examining testimony from Starr, Lewinsky, and other witnesses. Debate centered on whether Clinton’s false statements under oath and alleged attempts to obstruct justice met the constitutional threshold for impeachment.

### Approval of Articles of Impeachment

On December 11, 1998, the Judiciary Committee approved four articles of impeachment:

1. **Perjury before a grand jury** (undermining the integrity of judicial proceedings).
2. **Perjury in the Paula Jones civil deposition**.
3. **Obstruction of justice** (including efforts to conceal evidence and influence testimony).
4. **Abuse of power** (misuse of presidential office to impede the judicial process).

On December 19, 1998, the full House voted to impeach Clinton on two of these articles—perjury before the grand jury (228–206) and obstruction of justice (221–212)—while rejecting the other two.

## Senate Trial and Acquittal

The articles of impeachment were transmitted to the Senate, where Chief Justice William Rehnquist presided over the trial beginning January 7, 1999. Both parties presented opening statements: House managers argued that Clinton’s conduct violated constitutional duties, while White House counsel contended that the president’s misconduct, though improper, did not rise to an impeachable offense. Witness testimony was limited; Senate Republicans, led by Majority Leader Trent Lott, and Senate Democrats negotiated rules restricting additional evidence.

On February 12, 1999, the Senate voted on the two articles: Article I (perjury) failed 45–55 (short of the two-thirds majority required), and Article II (obstruction) failed 50–50. With neither article achieving the necessary 67 votes, Clinton was acquitted and remained in office for the remainder of his second term.

## Political and Public Response

### Public Opinion

Public reaction to the impeachment was deeply divided along partisan lines. Gallup polls in late 1998 showed that a majority of Americans disapproved of Clinton’s conduct but opposed removing him from office. Approval ratings for both Clinton and Congress fluctuated, with Clinton’s favorability dipping during the height of the scandal but recovering after acquittal.

### Impact on 2000 Election

The impeachment had mixed effects on subsequent politics. Democrats argued that the proceedings were part of a partisan “witch hunt,” galvanizing their base in the 2000 presidential election. Republicans claimed moral high ground but faced voter fatigue over prolonged investigations. In the 2000 election, George W. Bush defeated Vice President Al Gore by a narrow margin; the impeachment’s legacy remained a contentious topic in that campaign.

## Historical Significance

The efforts to impeach Bill Clinton underscored tensions between legal accountability and political partisanship in U.S. governance. Scholars and constitutional experts continue to debate whether Clinton’s actions constituted “high crimes and misdemeanors.” The 1998–1999 impeachment also influenced how Congress approaches presidential oversight, contributing to later debates over executive privilege, independent investigations, and the scope of impeachable conduct.

No subsequent impeachment efforts against Clinton materialized, but the framework and precedents set during his proceedings informed the impeachments of Presidents Donald J. Trump in 2019–2020 and 2021, reflecting ongoing struggles over the constitutional balance of powers in American political life.
