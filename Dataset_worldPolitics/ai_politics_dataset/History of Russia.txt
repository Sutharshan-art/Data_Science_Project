**History of Russia**

The history of Russia spans over a millennium, encompassing the rise and fall of principalities, tsardoms, empires, a revolutionary superstate, and the contemporary Russian Federation. Political developments on Russian territory have shaped Eurasian geopolitics, influenced global ideologies, and left enduring legacies in law, culture, and international relations.

---

## Kievan Rus and the Rise of Moscow (9th–15th Centuries)

**Kievan Rus’** (c. 882–1240), founded by East Slavic and Norse rulers around Kyiv, was the first polity to unite the region’s principalities. Its adoption of Orthodox Christianity in 988 under Prince Vladimir I forged religious and cultural ties with Byzantium. The Mongol invasion (1237–1240) devastated Kievan Rus’ and imposed the **Mongol yoke**, under which successor principalities paid tribute to the Golden Horde.

Among these, the **Grand Duchy of Moscow** gradually asserted dominance. Moscow’s princes, leveraging strategic alliances and the favor of Mongol khans, expanded their territories and secured the title of Grand Prince. In 1480, Ivan III (“the Great”) ended tribute payments, effectively asserting Muscovite sovereignty and laying the foundations for a centralized Russian state.

---

## Tsardom and Imperial Expansion (16th–19th Centuries)

Ivan IV (“the Terrible,” r. 1547–1584) assumed the title **Tsar of All Russia**, formalizing the autocratic monarchy. Under the Romanov dynasty (1613–1917), Russia expanded eastward across Siberia to the Pacific and southward into the Caucasus and Central Asia.

Peter the Great (r. 1682–1725) implemented sweeping **Westernizing reforms**: modernizing the army and navy, reorganizing government into collegia (ministries), and founding **St. Petersburg** as a “window to Europe.” His successors continued imperial consolidation, notably Catherine II (“the Great,” r. 1762–96), who extended Russian control over Crimea, Poland, and the Black Sea region.

By the mid-19th century, however, serfdom and autocracy strained Russia’s capacity to compete with industrializing European powers. The **Emancipation Reform of 1861** freed millions of serfs but left many landless and impoverished, fueling social unrest.

---

## Revolutionary Upheaval and the Soviet Era (1905–1991)

Defeat in the **Russo-Japanese War** (1904–05) and the **1905 Revolution** forced limited constitutional reforms, including the creation of the **State Duma** (parliament). Continued autocratic resistance, wartime crises in **World War I**, and economic collapse precipitated the **February Revolution** of 1917, which overthrew the Romanov monarchy and established a provisional government.

The **October Revolution**, led by Vladimir Lenin and the Bolshevik Party, replaced the provisional government with a socialist regime. A **civil war** (1918–21) ensued between Bolshevik (Red) and anti-Bolshevik (White) forces, ending in Bolshevik victory and the creation of the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics** (USSR) in 1922.

Under **Joseph Stalin** (r. 1924–53), the USSR underwent forced industrialization, collectivization of agriculture, and severe political repression, including the **Great Purge**. The USSR’s role in **World War II**—the **Great Patriotic War**—culminated in victory over Nazi Germany but at the cost of over 20 million Soviet lives. Postwar, the USSR emerged as a superpower engaged in a global **Cold War** with the United States, marked by nuclear arms races, proxy conflicts, and ideological competition.

Reforms under **Nikita Khrushchev** (de-Stalinization, 1956) and later **Mikhail Gorbachev** (*perestroika* and *glasnost*, mid-1980s) aimed to revitalize the Soviet system but unleashed forces that contributed to the USSR’s dissolution in December 1991.

---

## Post-Soviet Russia (1991–Present)

The Russian Federation, formally established in December 1991, confronted political and economic turmoil under President **Boris Yeltsin** (1991–1999). Rapid **market reforms** and privatization led to severe economic contraction, the emergence of powerful oligarchs, and regional conflicts, notably the **First Chechen War** (1994–96).

Vladimir Putin, appointed Prime Minister in 1999 and President in 2000, centralized authority, strengthened the presidency, and oversaw economic stabilization fueled by rising oil prices. His tenure has been marked by the reassertion of state control over key industries, curbs on political opposition, and assertive foreign policy actions—including the **Second Chechen War** (1999–2009), the **2008 Russo-Georgian War**, the annexation of **Crimea** in 2014, and military involvement in **Syria**.

Under Putin’s leadership, Russia has promoted a vision of multipolarity and national sovereignty, often in tension with Western institutions. Domestic political reforms in 2020 extended presidential term limits, enabling potential rule through 2036.

---

## Legacy and Contemporary Significance

Russia’s history of autocratic centralization, revolutionary change, and imperial expansion continues to shape its political culture and international posture. As the world’s largest country by land area and a major nuclear power, Russia plays a pivotal role in global security, energy markets, and regional dynamics in Eurasia. Its political evolution—from medieval principality to modern federation—reflects enduring tensions between authoritarian governance, reformist impulses, and the challenges of integrating diverse regions and populations under a unified state.
