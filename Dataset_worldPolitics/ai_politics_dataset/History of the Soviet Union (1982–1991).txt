**History of the Soviet Union (1982–1991)**

The period from 1982 to 1991 in the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) was defined by a succession of brief presidencies, mounting economic stagnation, ambitious political reforms under Mikhail Gorbachev, and ultimately the dissolution of the Soviet state. Key developments included leadership transitions, the introduction of **perestroika** (economic restructuring) and **glasnost** (openness), the liberalization of Eastern Europe, and internal struggles over sovereignty and central authority.

---

## Leadership Transitions and Gerontocracy

On 10 November 1982, Leonid Brezhnev—General Secretary since 1964—died after overseeing an era of economic slowdown and political ossification. He was succeeded by Yuri Andropov (General Secretary, November 1982–February 1984), former head of the KGB, who initiated limited anti-corruption campaigns but died after fifteen months in office. Konstantin Chernenko (General Secretary, February 1984–March 1985) continued a conservative line, prolonging the gerontocratic leadership that critics termed an “era of stagnation.” Both Andropov and Chernenko struggled with health issues, leaving significant policy initiatives incomplete.

---

## Gorbachev’s Rise and Reform Agenda

On 11 March 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev, aged 54, became General Secretary of the Communist Party. Marking a generational shift, he launched a program to revitalize the Soviet system. In April 1985, Gorbachev announced **perestroika**, aiming to decentralize economic decision-making, introduce limited market mechanisms, and reduce the politician-bureaucrat nexus. In tandem, **glasnost** policies from 1986 permitted public discussion of economic and social problems, encouraged investigative journalism, and led to the rehabilitation of many figures repressed under Stalin.

---

## Economic Reforms and Challenges

Initial reforms included the **Law on State Enterprises** (1987), granting managers greater autonomy over production and profits, and the **Cooperative Law** (1988), legalizing small-scale private businesses in retail, services, and manufacturing. However, structural issues—central planning rigidities, shortages, and declining oil revenues—hampered recovery. By 1990, inflation and supply disruptions grew acute, provoking public unrest and strikes. Gorbachev’s later reforms, including **500 Days** economic transformation proposals, were stymied by conservative Party elements and a lack of consensus on the pace of change.

---

## Political Liberalization and New Institutions

Glasnost unleashed debates on national identity, historical injustices, and institutional reform. Elective Congresses of People’s Deputies (first held in March 1989) introduced competitive voting for local and republic legislatures. In June 1990, the Congress created the **presidency**, and Gorbachev was elected the USSR’s first (and only) president, consolidating executive authority. Nevertheless, republic-level **sovereignty declarations** in Ukraine, the Baltic states, and elsewhere challenged central control, as many republics began asserting legal primacy of their constitutions over Union law.

---

## Foreign Policy and the End of the Cold War

Under Gorbachev, the USSR pursued the **New Thinking**, emphasizing common security and mutual cooperation. Key milestones included the **Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty** with the United States in December 1987, and Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan (completed February 1989). Gorbachev refrained from military intervention during the 1989 revolutions in Eastern Europe, leading to the collapse of communist regimes in Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria, and Romania. The fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989 symbolized the end of Soviet control over its satellite states.

---

## National Movements and Republican Crises

As central authority weakened, nationalist movements gained strength. The Baltic republics—Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia—declared independence by August 1991. In the Caucasus, conflicts in Georgia and Nagorno-Karabakh heightened tensions. In Russia, Boris Yeltsin’s election as president of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic (RSFSR) in June 1991 signaled a shift away from the Union Party line. The divergent political trajectories of republics created an impasse over the future structure of the USSR.

---

## August 1991 Coup Attempt

On 19 August 1991, conservative hard-liners opposed to Gorbachev’s reforms formed the **State Committee on the State of Emergency (GKChP)**, attempting a coup to restore centralized control. They detained Gorbachev at his Crimean dacha and declared a state of emergency. Massive public protests in Moscow, led by Yeltsin, and the defection of military units undermined the coup within three days. Although Gorbachev was restored, his authority was irretrievably weakened, and republic leaders accelerated moves toward sovereignty or independence.

---

## Dissolution of the Soviet Union

In early December 1991, leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus met at the **Belovezha Accords**, declaring the Soviet Union dissolved and establishing the **Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS)**. On 25 December 1991, Gorbachev resigned as president, and the Soviet flag was lowered over the Kremlin. The formal dissolution was ratified by the Soviet of the Republics on 26 December, ending the existence of the USSR.

---

Between 1982 and 1991, the Soviet Union underwent profound political transformation. What began as an attempt to rejuvenate a stagnant superpower culminated in a rapid disintegration of the Union’s political structures, reshaping the global order and marking the definitive close of the Cold War era.
