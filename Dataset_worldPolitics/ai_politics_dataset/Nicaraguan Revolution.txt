**Nicaraguan Revolution**

The **Nicaraguan Revolution** (1978–1979) was a popular uprising that culminated in the overthrow of the forty-four-year Somoza dynasty and the establishment of a revolutionary government led by the Sandinista National Liberation Front (Frente Sandinista de Liberación Nacional, FSLN). Rooted in decades of authoritarian rule, foreign intervention, and social inequality, the revolution reshaped Nicaragua’s political landscape and became a focal point of Cold War confrontation in Central America.

## Historical Background

Following independence from Spain (1821) and the collapse of the Central American Federation (1838), Nicaragua endured chronic political instability marked by rivalry between the Liberal Party (centered in León) and the Conservative Party (based in Granada). In 1936, Anastasio Somoza García—commander of the U.S.–trained National Guard—seized power by coup and established a family dictatorship. His assassination in 1956 cleared the way for his sons, Luis Somoza Debayle and later Anastasio Somoza Debayle, to consolidate a personalist regime that controlled the military, media, judiciary, and economy through patronage, repression, and close ties to the United States.

By the 1960s and 1970s, social disparities deepened: a small elite controlled agricultural land and export industries, while peasant and urban workers faced poverty, limited educational access, and political exclusion. U.S. support provided military aid and diplomatic cover, even as Somoza’s National Guard committed human-rights abuses against dissenters.

## Formation of the Sandinista Movement

In 1961, the FSLN was founded by Carlos Fonseca, Tomás Borge, and other leftist intellectuals and veterans of earlier anti-U.S. resistance. Drawing inspiration from Augusto César Sandino’s 1920s guerrilla campaign against U.S. occupation, the FSLN combined Marxist-Leninist ideology with Nicaraguan nationalist rhetoric. During the 1960s, Sandinistas conducted small-scale guerrilla actions—robberies, sabotage, and ambushes—primarily in rural regions, seeking to build popular support among peasants and urban workers.

The FSLN split into three tactical “tendencies” by the late 1960s: the “Prolonged Popular War” faction, which emphasized rural insurgency; the “Proletarian Tendency,” focused on urban worker mobilization; and the “September 1961” group, advocating rapid urban insurrection. By 1974, these factions reunited under unified leadership to coordinate broader mass and armed struggle.

## Escalation of Conflict

Somoza Debayle’s reelection in 1974, widely denounced as fraudulent, intensified opposition. The FSLN adopted a three-stage “Strategic Phase” strategy:

1. **Insurrectional Passive** – building popular front alliances and nonviolent protest
2. **Insurrectional Active** – expanding guerrilla warfare and high-profile operations
3. **Definitive War** – coordinating nationwide uprising

High-profile guerrilla actions in 1977–78—such as the kidnapping of U.S. ambassador Anthony Juliano and the 1974 assault on the National Palace—brought international attention to the Sandinista cause. In response, the Somoza regime intensified repression: censorship, arbitrary arrests, and summary executions by the National Guard.

## Mass Mobilization and the Fall of the Regime

The Somoza government’s heavy-handed tactics backfired. On 23 July 1978—Somoza’s 43rd anniversary—María Ramírez “Tachita” Chamorro, a popular local newspaper editor, was assassinated after criticizing the regime. Her murder sparked nationwide outrage, triggering general strikes, student demonstrations, and worker shutdowns in August and September. The FSLN, operating above ground for the first time, organized large rallies and coordinated urban protests that paralyzed Managua and other cities.

By late 1978, segments of the officer corps and business elites defected or withdrew support. The Carter administration in the United States—concerned over human-rights abuses—encouraged Somoza to negotiate. He refused, and on 17 July 1979, Sandinista and allied guerrilla columns entered Managua virtually unopposed. Somoza fled into exile on 17 July, ending the dynasty.

## Revolutionary Government and Reforms

On 19 July 1979, the FSLN established the Junta of National Reconstruction, headed by figures including Daniel Ortega, Sergio Ramírez, and Moisés Hassan. The new government embarked on ambitious programs:

* **Land Reform:** Expropriation and redistribution of large estates to peasants.
* **Literacy Campaign:** Mobilizing volunteers to teach reading and writing, reducing illiteracy from over 50% to under 13% by 1980.
* **Health and Housing:** Establishing rural clinics, vaccination programs, and subsidized housing.
* **Economic Nationalization:** Nationalizing key industries—banking, fishing, and large agricultural enterprises—while allowing limited private enterprise.

Meanwhile, the Sandinistas drafted a provisional constitution and, in 1984, held elections in which the FSLN won legislative and presidential majorities amid opposition boycotts that raised questions about electoral fairness.

## Contra War and Cold War Dynamics

The revolutionary government’s leftward orientation—ties to Cuba, the Soviet Union, and support for Salvadoran guerrillas—alarmed the Reagan administration. In 1981, the U.S. began covertly funding and training the **Contras**, a counter-revolutionary guerrilla force composed of former National Guard members and rural opponents. U.S. funding continued despite congressional prohibitions (the “Boland Amendments”), culminating in the Iran–Contra scandal (1985–1987). The decade-long conflict devastated Nicaragua: approximately 30,000–50,000 combatant and civilian deaths, economic contraction, and mass displacement.

## Peace Process and Legacy

Under international pressure, both sides agreed to ceasefires and UN-supervised elections in 1990. In February 1990, Violeta Chamorro of the National Opposition Union (UNO) defeated Ortega, marking the end of Sandinista rule. The revolution’s social programs left lasting gains in literacy and healthcare, yet the Contra war and U.S. embargo inflicted deep economic and social scars.

The Nicaraguan Revolution remains a defining moment in Cold War Latin America. It exemplified the contest between revolutionary nationalism and U.S. anti-communism, showcased the impact of grassroots mobilization, and spawned enduring debates over sovereignty, development, and international intervention. Its leaders—most notably Daniel Ortega—returned to power in 2006, shaping contemporary Nicaragua and ensuring that the revolution’s legacy continues to influence national politics.
