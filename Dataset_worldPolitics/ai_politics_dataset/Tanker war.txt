**Introduction**
The Tanker War was a maritime conflict during the latter stages of the Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988), in which both Iran and Iraq targeted each other’s oil tankers and merchant shipping in the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman. Beginning in 1984 and intensifying in 1986–1988, the campaign sought to undermine the opponent’s economy by disrupting oil exports, provoking international intervention and reshaping regional security dynamics.

**Historical Background**
The Iran–Iraq War began in September 1980 when Iraq invaded Iran, aiming to seize border territories and preempt the spread of Iran’s post-revolutionary ideology. By 1984, the protracted land war had stalemated. Facing dwindling revenues, both belligerents turned to maritime interdiction—attacking seaborne oil exports—as a means of economic warfare. Iraq initiated the campaign in mid-1984, but Iran’s counter-attacks in 1986 brought the conflict into full maritime dimensions.

**Escalation to the Tanker War**
Iraq’s initial naval strategy focused on mining Iranian ports and attacking tankers bound for Iran, particularly in the Strait of Hormuz. Iran responded by targeting tankers carrying Iraqi oil, including vessels flying neutral flags, under the rationale that preventing Iraq’s revenue would hasten its collapse. Both sides employed speedboats, naval patrol craft, coastal artillery, and aircraft to strike merchant ships, laying mines and launching missile and bomb attacks.

**Major Incidents and Tactics**

* **Mining Operations (1984–1985):** Iraq laid extensive minefields at the entrances to Kharg Island and Abu Dhabi, causing damage to Iranian tankers and vessels from neutral states. Iran in turn mined approaches to Basra and Khor al-Amaya.
* **Air and Missile Attacks (1986–1987):** Both navies used Exocet and other anti-ship missiles to strike tankers. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard Corps deployed helicopters and fast attack craft to ambush convoys, while Iraq relied on Mirage F1 fighters and Silkworm missiles.
* **Collateral Damage:** Neutral shipping—including vessels from Japan, Greece, and other oil-importing nations—suffered attacks or mistaken hits, raising international alarm over freedom of navigation and the safety of civilian mariners.

**International Reactions**
The disruption of oil flows and the threat to maritime commerce prompted growing concern among oil‐importing nations. Japan and Western European states called for the protection of neutral shipping, while the United States and the Soviet Union—each seeking to limit Soviet or American influence respectively—monitored developments warily. The Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) condemned attacks on its member Iraq’s exports but also recognized Iran’s grievances over Iraq’s invasion.

**United States Involvement**
In response to repeated Iranian attacks, especially on vessels chartered to Kuwait, the United States launched Operation Earnest Will in July 1987, reflagging and escorting Kuwaiti tankers with U.S. Navy warships through the Gulf. In October 1987, following the mining of a reflagged tanker, the U.S. conducted Operation Nimble Archer, a retaliatory strike on Iranian oil platforms used for directing attacks. In April 1988, after Iranian Silkworm missiles damaged the frigate USS Samuel B. Roberts, the U.S. executed Operation Praying Mantis—the largest American naval action since World War II—destroying two Iranian frigates and several platforms.

**Political and Strategic Implications**
The Tanker War transformed the Persian Gulf into a contested international battleground, challenging the principle of freedom of navigation. U.S. intervention underscored America’s strategic interest in Gulf oil and deterred further large-scale Iranian attacks, although low-level harassment continued until the war’s end. The conflict demonstrated the vulnerability of global energy routes to regional hostilities and prompted many commercial shippers to reroute around the Cape of Good Hope or install onboard defensive systems.

**Economic Impact**
Oil markets reacted to the heightened risk with price fluctuations, insurance surcharges, and reduced tanker availability. Gulf producers increased output ahead of potential blockades, while consumer nations diversified suppliers. The cost of marine insurance in the “High-Risk Area” skyrocketed, adding millions to the cost of each voyage and influencing shipping practices well beyond the immediate war years.

**Conclusion and Legacy**
The Tanker War concluded with the ceasefire of August 1988, part of U.N. Security Council Resolution 598 that ended the Iran–Iraq War. Its legacy endures in international maritime law and naval doctrine, highlighting the importance of deterrence, convoy protection, and legal frameworks for safeguarding neutral shipping. The campaign also presaged later Gulf conflicts—such as the 1991 Persian Gulf War and 2000s “Tanker War” flare-ups—underscoring the enduring linkage between regional politics, global energy security, and naval power projection.
