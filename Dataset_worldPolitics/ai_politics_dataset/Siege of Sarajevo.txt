**Siege of Sarajevo**

The **Siege of Sarajevo** was a prolonged military blockade and assault carried out by Bosnian Serb forces against the capital of Bosnia and Herzegovina, Sarajevo, during the Bosnian War. Lasting from **April 5, 1992, to February 29, 1996**, it is the longest siege of a capital city in modern warfare. The siege resulted in severe humanitarian suffering, large-scale destruction, and widespread civilian casualties, becoming one of the defining episodes of the Yugoslav Wars and a key symbol of the war’s brutality.

### Historical and Political Background

The Siege of Sarajevo took place in the context of the **dissolution of Yugoslavia**, which began in the early 1990s. As the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia disintegrated, Bosnia and Herzegovina—a republic with a mixed population of Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims), Serbs, and Croats—declared independence following a referendum in March 1992. The referendum was boycotted by most Bosnian Serbs, who rejected independence and sought to remain part of a Serb-dominated Yugoslavia.

The **Bosnian Serb leadership**, backed by the Yugoslav People’s Army (JNA) and later supported by the newly formed Army of Republika Srpska (VRS), opposed the independence of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Their goal was to create ethnically Serb territories and unite them with Serbia. Sarajevo, as the capital and a symbol of multiethnic coexistence, became a strategic and symbolic target.

### The Siege Begins

The siege began in earnest on **April 5, 1992**, when Bosnian Serb forces surrounded Sarajevo and took control of the high ground in the hills and mountains around the city. From these positions, they launched artillery, sniper, and mortar attacks against civilian and military targets. The city was effectively encircled, and all major supply routes were cut off.

The besieging forces, numbering between 10,000 and 15,000 troops, deployed heavy weapons including tanks, multiple rocket launchers, and artillery. The defenders of the city, primarily the **Army of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina (ARBiH)**, were poorly equipped at the onset, relying on limited weapons and local volunteers.

### Conditions in the City

Sarajevo’s civilian population of over 300,000 was subjected to almost daily bombardment and sniper attacks for nearly four years. Basic infrastructure collapsed, with chronic shortages of food, water, electricity, and medical supplies. Hospitals, schools, markets, and cultural institutions were frequently targeted or caught in crossfire.

Snipers positioned in buildings around the city made movement dangerous, particularly along routes such as “**Sniper Alley**,” a main thoroughfare exposed to enemy fire. International media coverage documented the suffering of civilians and highlighted the inadequacy of the international community’s response.

The **death toll** from the siege is estimated at around **13,000 people**, including over 5,000 civilians. Tens of thousands more were wounded, and many suffered long-term physical and psychological trauma. Cultural landmarks, including the National Library and numerous historic buildings, were destroyed or severely damaged.

### International Response

The siege of Sarajevo prompted widespread international condemnation but was met with limited initial intervention. The **United Nations** deployed peacekeepers under the **UN Protection Force (UNPROFOR)** and declared Sarajevo a **UN “safe area”** in 1993, but the mandate and rules of engagement limited the force’s ability to protect civilians or stop the shelling.

Humanitarian aid was delivered via **airlifts** organized by the UN and NATO, and later through the **Sarajevo Tunnel**, a clandestine passage built under the airport in 1993 that linked the besieged city to Bosnian government-held territory. However, aid efforts were often hampered by shelling, bureaucracy, and access restrictions.

NATO’s involvement escalated in 1994 and 1995, with airstrikes against Bosnian Serb positions after repeated violations of UN resolutions. The turning point came after the **second Markale marketplace massacre** in August 1995, which killed 43 people. This attack prompted **Operation Deliberate Force**, a sustained NATO bombing campaign targeting Bosnian Serb military infrastructure.

### End of the Siege

The siege officially ended on **February 29, 1996**, following the signing of the **Dayton Peace Accords** in December 1995, which brought an end to the Bosnian War. The accords established a power-sharing arrangement and recognized the internal division of Bosnia and Herzegovina into two entities: the **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina** and **Republika Srpska**.

With the implementation of the peace agreement and the arrival of the NATO-led **Implementation Force (IFOR)**, Bosnian Serb forces withdrew from Sarajevo’s surroundings, and the city was gradually reintegrated under the authority of the Bosnian central government.

### Aftermath and Legacy

The Siege of Sarajevo left deep physical and psychological scars on the city and its population. Thousands of buildings were damaged or destroyed, and the cultural fabric of the multiethnic city was disrupted. The siege became emblematic of the international community's failure to prevent atrocities during the Yugoslav conflicts.

Several **war crimes trials** were held in connection to the siege. **Stanislav Galić**, commander of the Sarajevo-Romanija Corps of the VRS, was sentenced to life imprisonment by the **International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY)** for his role in the siege. Other senior Bosnian Serb figures, including **Ratko Mladić** and **Radovan Karadžić**, were also convicted for crimes related to the siege and broader war.

Today, the Siege of Sarajevo is remembered as one of the longest and most brutal urban sieges in modern history, symbolizing both the horrors of the Bosnian War and the resilience of its citizens under extreme conditions. Numerous memorials, museums, and commemorations exist in Sarajevo to honor the victims and preserve the memory of the conflict.
