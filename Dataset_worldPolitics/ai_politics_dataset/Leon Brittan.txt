**Leon Brittan, Baron Brittan of Spennithorne** (20 September 1939 – 21 January 2015) was a British Conservative politician and barrister who served in senior ministerial roles under Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher and later as a European Commissioner. His career spanned crucial developments in UK domestic policy, the fight against international corruption and competition policy in the European Community, and the evolving relationship between Britain and Europe.

**Early Life and Education**
Leon Brittan was born in London to Latvian Jewish refugees fleeing political turmoil in Eastern Europe. He was educated at Latymer Upper School and read history at Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, where he was active in the University Conservative Association. Called to the Bar at Middle Temple in 1963, he built a successful practice in commercial and public law before entering politics.

**Entry into Parliament and Early Ministerial Roles**
Brittan was elected Member of Parliament for the marginal constituency of Cleveland and Whitby in February 1974, losing the seat in October of that year, and returned to the Commons in 1976 in a by-election for the safe Conservative seat of Richmond (Yorks). He quickly gained attention for his legal expertise and was appointed Parliamentary Under-Secretary at the Department of Trade in 1979 upon the election of Margaret Thatcher. In 1981 he was promoted to Chief Secretary to the Treasury, responsible for public spending and economic coordination at the height of the Conservative government’s monetarist policy drive.

**Home Secretary (1983–1985)**
In January 1983, Brittan succeeded William Whitelaw as Home Secretary. His tenure coincided with a surge in Northern Ireland violence, the miner’s strike of 1984–85 and growing concerns over policing, immigration, and public order. He oversaw legislation expanding police powers of stop-and-search and tightening immigration controls, while also establishing the Police Complaints Authority to oversee misconduct investigations. His firm handling of the miners’ picket lines—including emergency public order regulations—made him a polarizing figure: lauded by Thatcherites for firmness, criticized by civil liberties advocates for eroding protections against arbitrary detention.

**Cabinet Office Minister and Deputy Chairman of the Conservative Party (1985–1986)**
After the 1985 cabinet reshuffle, Brittan moved to the Cabinet Office, acting as the Prime Minister’s principal policy coordinator and crisis manager. In this capacity he chaired a cross-departmental “doomsday” committee preparing contingency plans for domestic unrest and international crises. His organisational talents were also deployed as Deputy Chairman of the Conservative Party, modernising campaign structures ahead of the 1987 general election.

**European Commissioner for Competition (1989–1993)**
Having left the Commons in 1988, Brittan was appointed United Kingdom European Commissioner in March 1989, taking the Competition portfolio. He became known for vigorous antitrust enforcement, notably blocking proposed mergers between major aerospace and brewing companies, and imposing record fines for cartels. His Commission investigated state aid to national champions, promoting single-market liberalisation across the Community. These actions positioned competition policy as central to European integration and economic growth.

**European Commissioner for Trade (1993–1999)**
Brittan’s portfolio shifted in 1993 to Trade, where he negotiated key agreements under the Uruguay Round of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), laying the foundation for the World Trade Organization. He led the Community’s responses to emerging challenges from Asia and Latin America, championed trade liberalisation combined with social and environmental standards, and navigated the EU’s complex internal decision-making to secure unanimous backing for Common Commercial Policy initiatives.

**Post-Commission Career and Peerage**
On leaving the Commission in 1999, Brittan took a life peerage as Baron Brittan of Spennithorne. He served as Chairman of UBS Investment Bank’s European arm, and held non-executive directorships in the chemicals, utilities, and media sectors. He continued to advise Conservative governments on EU policy and chaired numerous business and public-policy consultative bodies, advocating for Britain’s active engagement in Europe even as domestic Euroscepticism grew.

**Controversies**
Brittan’s reputation came under scrutiny in the late 2000s during investigations into historical allegations of child sexual abuse at a Kincora Boys’ Home in Northern Ireland and alleged failures by Home Office officials to act on intelligence in the early 1980s. Two independent inquiries in 2014 cleared him of personal wrongdoing and criticized the quality of evidence behind the claims, but the controversy highlighted the politicized environment of “troubles-era” Northern Ireland and the challenges of ministerial accountability.

**Legacy**
Leon Brittan’s career bridged domestic governance, European integration, and global trade in an era of rapid political and economic change. As Home Secretary, his policies helped define Thatcherite conservatism’s law-and-order emphasis. As European Commissioner, he shaped modern competition and trade regimes that underpin today’s EU single market and global trading system. His life peerage allowed him to continue influencing British and European policy debates into the early twenty-first century, embodying the complex interplay between national sovereignty and supranational cooperation that remains a central fault line in UK politics.
