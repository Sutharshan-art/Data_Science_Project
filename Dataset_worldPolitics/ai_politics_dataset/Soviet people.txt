**Soviet People**

The term **Soviet people** (Russian: *советский народ*, *sovetskiy narod*) was an officially promoted concept in the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR)** referring to a unified, supra-national identity encompassing all citizens of the Soviet state. It emerged as part of the Soviet leadership’s efforts to consolidate the diverse ethnic, linguistic, and cultural groups within the USSR under a common socialist and ideological framework. The concept held both political and cultural significance and was most actively promoted from the 1960s through the 1980s.

The notion of the “Soviet people” was rooted in the Marxist-Leninist ideology of internationalism, class solidarity, and the ultimate goal of transcending national divisions. It was intended to create a collective identity that would bind together the many nationalities of the Soviet Union and reinforce loyalty to the Communist Party and the socialist system.

---

### Historical Context

The USSR was founded in 1922 as a multiethnic federation consisting of numerous republics and autonomous regions, each with its own titular nationality. At its height, the Soviet Union was home to more than **100 officially recognized ethnic groups**. Initially, under **Lenin**, the Soviet leadership pursued a policy known as **korenizatsiya** ("indigenization"), which encouraged the development of local languages, cultures, and administrative elites within a socialist framework.

However, by the late 1930s, under **Joseph Stalin**, this approach shifted toward centralization and **Russification**, as Russian language and culture increasingly became dominant. After Stalin’s death in 1953, **Nikita Khrushchev** and later **Leonid Brezhnev** resumed promoting Soviet unity, but with a renewed emphasis on building a single Soviet identity, culminating in the articulation of the “Soviet people” as a distinct socio-political community.

---

### Ideological Foundations

The concept of the Soviet people was formally introduced into the CPSU’s ideological lexicon during the **Twenty-Second Party Congress in 1961**, under Khrushchev’s leadership. It was presented as a new historical community that transcended national distinctions while preserving national cultures within a unified socialist state.

The Soviet people were defined not by ethnicity or nationality but by a shared commitment to **socialist values**, **internationalism**, and **proletarian solidarity**. The idea emphasized:

* Unity across ethnic and cultural lines
* The central role of the **Russian language** as the "language of interethnic communication"
* Collective labor, industrial development, and scientific progress
* Loyalty to the **Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU)** and the goals of **Marxism-Leninism**

The state promoted this identity through education, propaganda, the military, and mass organizations such as the **Komsomol** (Young Communist League), as well as through public ceremonies and holidays that highlighted unity and socialist achievements.

---

### Social and Political Implications

The promotion of the Soviet people had significant effects on domestic policy and social life. It influenced population movements (including forced migrations and internal resettlement), language education policies, and the structure of the Soviet labor force. Ethnic Russians, as the largest group, played a leading role in this process, and critics have argued that the concept implicitly privileged Russian culture.

In census data, Soviet citizens were still required to list their **nationality (ethnos)**, and passports included a “nationality” line (e.g., Ukrainian, Georgian, Uzbek). Thus, the ideological aspiration toward a unified Soviet people often clashed with the reality of ethnic and regional distinctions. While some individuals embraced a Soviet identity, many maintained strong national allegiances and cultural ties.

By the 1970s and 1980s, the concept of the Soviet people became increasingly symbolic and ceremonial, reflecting the stagnation of Soviet ideology during the **Brezhnev era**. The term was frequently used in official rhetoric, literature, and media, but it failed to supplant deeper national identities, especially in the non-Russian republics.

---

### Decline and Disintegration

The concept of the Soviet people began to erode in the late 1980s with the advent of **glasnost (openness)** and **perestroika (restructuring)** under **Mikhail Gorbachev**. Political liberalization, economic hardship, and the weakening of centralized control led to a resurgence of national movements and ethnic consciousness across the USSR.

By the late 1980s and early 1990s, ethnic republics increasingly demanded sovereignty or independence, undermining the foundations of the Soviet identity project. The **Baltic republics**, **Georgia**, **Armenia**, and **Ukraine** led the way in asserting national distinctiveness and rejecting the narrative of a unified Soviet people.

Following the **collapse of the Soviet Union in December 1991**, the term “Soviet people” quickly disappeared from official discourse, and successor states reoriented themselves toward national identity and independence. The legacy of the Soviet people remains mixed—viewed by some as a sincere effort at unity and by others as a vehicle for cultural homogenization and political control.

---

### Legacy

Today, the idea of the Soviet people is largely historical, though it retains significance in post-Soviet nostalgia and in the memory politics of former Soviet republics. In **Russia**, which considers itself the legal successor of the USSR, the concept has at times resurfaced in discussions of national unity and regional integration, particularly in the context of the **Eurasian Economic Union** and other post-Soviet initiatives.

While the Soviet people never became a fully realized national identity, the concept remains an important lens through which to understand the Soviet Union’s attempts to manage diversity, promote ideological cohesion, and forge a modern socialist society across vast and varied populations.
