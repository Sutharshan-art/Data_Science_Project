**Iraq War (disambiguation)**

The term **Iraq War** is used ambiguously to refer to several distinct armed conflicts involving the territory or government of modern-day Iraq. Over more than a century, strategic location, imperial rivalries, regional ambitions, and internal political struggles have produced multiple campaigns each sometimes called the “Iraq War.” The following list outlines the principal conflicts commonly so designated, with their historical and political contexts.

---

**Mesopotamian Campaign (1914–1918)**
Often referred to in early twentieth-century sources as the “Iraq Campaign,” this series of operations took place during the First World War. Following the Ottoman entry into the war on the side of the Central Powers, British forces from India advanced up the Tigris and Euphrates river valleys to secure oil fields at Basra and protect Persian Gulf routes. Initial Ottoman counter-attacks culminated in the siege of Kut (1915–1916), where British forces surrendered. The campaign ended with the British capture of Baghdad in March 1917 and the defeat of Ottoman forces by 1918. The subsequent League of Nations mandate established the modern state of Iraq under British administration, setting the stage for recurring external interventions and nationalist uprisings throughout the twentieth century.

---

**Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988)**
A protracted and devastating interstate conflict, the Iran–Iraq War began when President Ṣaddām Ḥussein ordered an invasion of Iran’s oil-rich Khuzestan province and disputed Shatt al-Arab waterway on 22 September 1980. Motivated by territorial claims, fears of Islamic revolutionary export from Tehran, and ambitions of regional hegemony, Iraq initially sought a swift victory. Iranian forces, galvanized by revolutionary zeal and mass mobilization, mounted counter-offensives that reclaimed lost territory by 1982. The front then stabilized in trench warfare until Iraq, bolstered by Soviet, French, and Gulf Arab support, employed chemical weapons and renewed offensives in 1987–1988. Exhaustion and international mediation led to UN Security Council Resolution 598 and a ceasefire on 20 August 1988, leaving nearly a million casualties and unresolved grievances.

---

**Gulf War (1990–1991)**
Sometimes called the **First Iraq War**, this conflict followed Iraq’s August 1990 invasion and annexation of Kuwait. A U.S.-led coalition under UN auspices amassed forces in neighboring Saudi Arabia (Operation Desert Shield) before launching Operation Desert Storm on 17 January 1991. Massive air strikes and a ground offensive swiftly expelled Iraqi troops from Kuwait by late February. The political objectives—restoration of Kuwaiti sovereignty and containment of Iraqi ambitions—were achieved with minimal coalition casualties. However, post-war sanctions, no-fly zones, and periodic uprisings by Kurds and Shi’a were suppressed by the Iraqi regime, setting the stage for future intervention.

---

**Iraq War (2003–2011)**
Referred to as the **Second Iraq War**, this conflict began with the U.S.-led invasion on 20 March 2003, justified by assertions that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction and had ties to terrorist networks. The rapid toppling of Ṣaddām Ḥussein’s regime—culminating in the fall of Baghdad in April—gave way to a prolonged insurgency. Multinational forces faced guerrilla attacks, sectarian violence, and the rise of al-Qaeda in Iraq. Political efforts produced a new constitution in 2005 and the transfer of full sovereignty to an Iraqi government in June 2004. The 2007 “surge” of U.S. troops helped reduce violence, and American forces completed their withdrawal in December 2011. The war’s legacy includes deep sectarian divisions, humanitarian crises, and debates over interventionist policy.

---

**Iraqi Insurgency and Civil War (2011–2017)**
After the 2011 withdrawal of U.S. forces, Iraq descended into renewed conflict. Islamist militants, most notably the Islamic State (IS), exploited security vacuums in western and northern provinces. IS declared a caliphate in June 2014 after capturing Mosul and other major cities, prompting a U.S.-led anti-IS coalition and an expanded role for Iraqi and Kurdish forces. The ensuing battles—marked by urban sieges in Ramadi, Fallujah, and Mosul—constituted a civil war phase that formally ended with IS’s territorial defeat in 2017. Reconstruction, reconciliation, and the reintegration of displaced populations remain pressing challenges, even as sporadic insurgent attacks continue.
