**Nicholas Ridley, Baron Ridley of Liddesdale**

**Nicholas Ridley, Baron Ridley of Liddesdale** (17 February 1929 – 23 February 1993) was a British Conservative politician who held several senior cabinet posts under Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher. A leading free-market advocate and Eurosceptic, Ridley was instrumental in the privatisation programmes of the 1980s and was a prominent figure in debates over Britain’s relationship with the European Community.

## Early Life and Education

Born in Kensington, London, Ridley was the eldest son of Matthew White Ridley, 3rd Viscount Ridley. He was educated at Eton College and New College, Oxford, where he read Philosophy, Politics and Economics and served as President of the Oxford Union in 1952. After graduating, he undertook national service with the Royal Northumberland Fusiliers and subsequently qualified as a chartered accountant.

## Parliamentary Career

Ridley was elected Member of Parliament for Cirencester and Tewkesbury in 1959, a seat he held until narrowly losing in the 1992 general election. A backbencher in his early years, he became known for advocacy of tax cuts, deregulation, and a hard line against trade union power.

## Ministerial Offices

* **Under-Secretary of State for Trade** (1970–1972): Under Edward Heath’s government, Ridley served at the Board of Trade, where he promoted export incentives and early deregulation measures.
* **Minister of State for Northern Ireland** (1972–1974): During the height of the Troubles, he supported security-focused policies and was a proponent of direct rule from Westminster.
* **Secretary of State for Transport** (1979–1983): Appointed to Margaret Thatcher’s first cabinet, Ridley oversaw further deregulation of the bus industry (building on the Transport Act 1980) and laid groundwork for rail sector reform.
* **Secretary of State for the Environment** (1983–1986): He pursued the “Right to Buy” council houses initiative, strengthened compulsory competitive tendering for local government services, and championed deregulation of planning controls.
* **Secretary of State for Trade and Industry** (1986–1987): In the aftermath of the Westland helicopter controversy, Ridley took over the department, expediting the privatisation of British Telecom and British Airways and negotiating industry liberalisation agreements within the European Community.

## Political Philosophy and Contributions

Ridley was a leading proponent of Thatcherism’s free-market agenda. He argued that state ownership and heavy regulation stifled enterprise and that competition would improve public services and drive economic growth. A key architect of the mid-1980s privatisation wave, he helped conceive the flotation of major state‐owned enterprises, asserting that share ownership would “democratise capitalism.”

An early Eurosceptic, Ridley warned against what he termed the “democratic deficit” of the European Community. In a 1987 television interview, he asserted that membership requirements threatened British sovereignty. The ensuing controversy—Ridley’s remark that “we have to fight and stop all this nonsense about a European superstate”—led to his resignation from the cabinet later that year.

## Euroscepticism and Later Years

After leaving the cabinet, Ridley became chairman of the Conservative backbench European Research Group, lobbying against further political integration. He spearheaded efforts to roll back EC budget contributions and resisted moves toward a single currency. Although he did not live to see it, his arguments laid intellectual groundwork for the Eurosceptic wing of the party that ultimately culminated in the 2016 Brexit referendum.

Defeated in his constituency in 1992, Ridley was elevated to the peerage as Baron Ridley of Liddesdale. In the House of Lords, he continued to attack proposed Treaty on European Union provisions and to campaign for lower taxes and reduced welfare dependency. He published *The Sovereign State and Its Competitors* (1991), tracing the historical evolution of the modern nation‐state and warning of supranational encroachments.

## Legacy

Ridley died of a heart attack on 23 February 1993. He is remembered as one of the most articulate and uncompromising advocates of market liberalism in late 20th‐century Britain. His role in advancing privatisation and deregulation cemented key elements of the Thatcher government’s economic legacy. Equally, his early and vocal Euroscepticism marked him as a progenitor of the Conservative Party’s later division over Europe. A commanding orator and persuasive writer, Ridley’s ideas continue to influence debates over the balance between national sovereignty and international cooperation.
