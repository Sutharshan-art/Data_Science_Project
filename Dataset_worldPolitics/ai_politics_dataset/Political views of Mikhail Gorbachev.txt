**Political Views of Mikhail Gorbachev**

**Mikhail Gorbachev** (1931–2022) was a Soviet statesman and reformer who served as the **General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU)** from 1985 to 1991 and as the **President of the Soviet Union** from 1990 until its dissolution in 1991. His political views evolved significantly over time, reflecting both a commitment to socialism and a growing embrace of liberalization, transparency, and peaceful international engagement.

Gorbachev’s leadership marked a turning point in both Soviet domestic policy and global Cold War dynamics. His reformist agenda, centered on the twin policies of **perestroika** (restructuring) and **glasnost** (openness), aimed to revitalize the Soviet system but ultimately contributed to its transformation and collapse. Gorbachev's political philosophy combined Marxist ideals with elements of democratic governance and human rights, earning him international recognition while dividing opinion within the USSR.

---

### Commitment to Socialism and Reform

Gorbachev considered himself a committed **socialist**, but one who recognized the structural failures of the Soviet command economy and the authoritarian excesses of earlier leadership. He believed the goals of socialism—equality, justice, and public welfare—could only be achieved through **modernization**, **openness**, and a more responsive political system.

Upon assuming leadership in 1985, Gorbachev acknowledged the inefficiency, stagnation, and corruption plaguing the Soviet Union. His **perestroika** policy sought to decentralize economic planning, introduce limited market mechanisms, and improve productivity through enterprise autonomy. While retaining the idea of state ownership, Gorbachev promoted partial liberalization, including the Law on State Enterprises (1987) and limited private enterprise in the form of cooperatives.

Politically, Gorbachev believed in **reforming rather than abandoning** the one-party system. He introduced multi-candidate elections for local and national legislative bodies, starting with the 1989 Congress of People's Deputies. These reforms opened space for political pluralism and criticism of the CPSU, but Gorbachev resisted full democratization that would threaten the party’s central role.

---

### Glasnost and Democratic Principles

Gorbachev's policy of **glasnost** reflected a fundamental shift toward transparency in governance and greater freedom of expression. He believed that exposing the public to previously censored information—such as Stalinist crimes, economic failings, and environmental disasters—would foster civic engagement and pressure the bureaucracy to reform.

Glasnost transformed Soviet public life by relaxing censorship, enabling open debate, and encouraging media independence. Gorbachev supported the rise of new civil society organizations and the cultural revival of previously suppressed national and religious identities. Although these changes contributed to destabilization, they aligned with Gorbachev's belief in **socialist democracy**—a political system rooted in participation, pluralism, and accountability.

---

### Foreign Policy and "New Thinking"

Gorbachev's foreign policy, often described as **“new thinking,”** was grounded in the conviction that global security depended not on confrontation but **cooperation**, **disarmament**, and **mutual respect** among states. Rejecting Cold War zero-sum assumptions, he promoted the idea of a **common European home**, emphasizing shared human values over ideological competition.

His views led to groundbreaking arms control agreements, including the **Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty** in 1987 and the **Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (START I)** in 1991. Gorbachev also initiated the withdrawal of Soviet troops from **Afghanistan** and declined to use force to suppress revolutions in **Eastern Europe**, allowing Warsaw Pact countries to transition away from communism.

His willingness to end the Brezhnev Doctrine and accept German reunification under NATO was based on his belief that sovereignty and peace were more important than preserving Soviet hegemony.

---

### Nationalities Policy and Decentralization

Gorbachev's views on the **multiethnic character of the Soviet Union** were shaped by a desire to balance central authority with greater autonomy for the republics. He supported cultural revival and linguistic rights but resisted calls for full sovereignty or secession.

His attempts to restructure the federal system—through the proposed **Union Treaty**—were meant to preserve a reformed union based on voluntary association. However, his incremental approach alienated both conservatives defending central control and republics demanding independence. The political instability that followed culminated in the **August 1991 coup attempt** and the eventual dissolution of the USSR.

---

### Post-Soviet Advocacy and Legacy

After resigning in 1991, Gorbachev continued to advocate for **democratic socialism**, **global governance**, and **human rights**. He was a vocal critic of authoritarian tendencies in post-Soviet Russia, particularly under **Vladimir Putin**, and expressed concern over the rollback of democratic reforms and media freedom.

Gorbachev founded the **Gorbachev Foundation** and the **Green Cross International**, promoting environmental sustainability, peace, and dialogue between nations. He remained a supporter of European integration and arms control efforts, arguing that humanity faced shared challenges requiring global cooperation.

---

### Conclusion

Mikhail Gorbachev’s political views represented a unique blend of socialist ideals and democratic reformism. His belief in openness, decentralization, and peaceful engagement redefined Soviet politics and helped end the Cold War. While his reforms unintentionally contributed to the collapse of the USSR, they also opened the path to democratization and pluralism in Eastern Europe. Gorbachev remains a complex and contested figure, praised internationally for his vision and reviled by some in Russia for presiding over the end of Soviet power.
