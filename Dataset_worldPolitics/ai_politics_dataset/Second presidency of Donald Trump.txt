**Second Presidency of Donald Trump**

**Overview**
The second presidency of Donald J. Trump began on January 20, 2025, when he was inaugurated as the 47th President of the United States, having defeated incumbent President Joe Biden in the 2024 election. Trump’s return to the White House marked the first time in U.S. history that a former president served non‐consecutive terms since Grover Cleveland in the 19th century. His second term has been defined by efforts to revive his “America First” agenda amid heightened domestic polarization and shifting global dynamics.

**Election and Inauguration**
In a rematch of the 2020 contest, Trump secured the Republican nomination and campaigned on themes of border security, economic growth through deregulation, and a muscular foreign policy. He won key battleground states—Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin—by narrow margins, capturing 278 electoral votes to Biden’s 260. His inauguration on the West Front of the U.S. Capitol featured the traditional oath of office administered by Chief Justice John Roberts, followed by an inaugural address emphasizing national sovereignty, judicial reform, and “restoring common‐sense government.”

**Administration and Cabinet**
Trump’s second‐term Cabinet reflected a blend of returning veterans and new appointees:

* **Vice President**: Mike Pence returned to the role, reinforcing continuity with the first term.
* **Secretary of State**: Elaine Chao, previously Transportation Secretary, was nominated to lead diplomatic efforts.
* **Secretary of the Treasury**: Stephen Mnuchin, Trump’s former Treasury Secretary, was tapped to oversee fiscal policy.
* **Attorney General**: Tom Fitton, a conservative litigator, succeeded upon confirmation amid debates over Department of Justice independence.
  Key White House staff included Chief of Staff Mark Meadows and National Security Advisor John Bolton, signaling a hardline approach to both domestic enforcement and foreign affairs.

**Domestic Policy**
Trump’s domestic agenda focused on four pillars:

1. **Border and Immigration Enforcement**: The administration reinstated accelerated removal processes and expanded construction of physical barriers along the U.S.–Mexico border. A revised “Remain in Country” policy required asylum seekers to await adjudication abroad.
2. **Economic Deregulation and Taxation**: Building on the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, Trump signed an “Economic Expansion Act” that lowered the corporate tax rate from 21% to 18% and rolled back financial‐sector regulations enacted after the 2008 crisis. Supporters credited these measures with a short‐term GDP uptick, while critics warned of widening deficits.
3. **Judicial and Administrative Reform**: Through executive orders and legislative proposals, the president sought to curtail “bureaucratic overreach,” requiring congressional approval for major agency rule‐making and mandating a two‐for‐one rule for new regulations. The Senate confirmed three additional conservative justices to the Supreme Court, solidifying a 6–3 conservative majority.
4. **Healthcare and Social Policy**: Trump advanced a plan to cross‐state purchase of health insurance and expanded Health Savings Accounts, while preserving preexisting‐condition protections. His administration also sought to expand school choice through federal vouchers.

**Foreign Policy**
Trump’s second term featured continued skepticism of multilateral institutions and emphasis on bilateral negotiations:

* **China**: He maintained tariffs on Chinese goods, pushing for “phase two” trade talks to address alleged intellectual property theft and state subsidies.
* **NATO and Allies**: Trump reaffirmed that member states must meet defense‐spending targets but withheld U.S. commitments pending burden‐sharing negotiations.
* **Middle East**: Continuing the Abraham Accords framework, his administration brokered normalization agreements between Israel and several Gulf states, while sustaining sanctions on Iran and retaining U.S. forces in Iraq to counter militant groups.
* **Russia**: Trump declared willingness to explore nuclear arms‐control talks but controversially delayed extensions of New START, prompting concerns in European capitals. He withdrew additional sanctions tied to Nord Stream 2 unfinished pipeline.

**Political Context and Challenges**
Trump’s return intensified cultural and institutional divides. His critics decried what they characterized as erosion of democratic norms, pointing to aggressive use of executive orders and public attacks on independent agencies. Supporters lauded his direct approach, economic gains, and challenge to “deep‐state” bureaucracy. Congressional control remained narrowly divided, with Republicans holding the Senate and Democrats retaining the House after the 2024 midterms, resulting in legislative gridlock on major initiatives.

**Impeachment Inquiry and Oversight**
Early in his term, the House of Representatives initiated an impeachment inquiry related to alleged efforts to pressure the Department of Justice to pursue politically motivated prosecutions. The inquiry centered on White House communications obtained by the Judiciary Committee. Although impeachment articles were introduced, the Senate—controlled by a slim Republican majority—declined to convict, framing the investigation as partisan.

**Legacy and Ongoing Assessment**
As of mid-2025, Trump’s second presidency remains a focal point of scholarly debate on executive power, democratic resilience, and the evolving nature of American conservatism. His administration’s policy outcomes—particularly on the economy and judiciary—are evaluated against long‐term fiscal projections and shifts in the ideological composition of the courts. Internationally, allies and adversaries alike gauge U.S. reliability under a president who prizes transactional diplomacy. The ultimate impact of Trump’s second term will depend on the 2026 midterm elections and the 2028 presidential race, as well as the durability of institutional checks.
