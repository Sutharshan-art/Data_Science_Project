**1960 in the Vietnam War**

The year **1960** was a pivotal period in the early escalation of the **Vietnam War**, as the conflict deepened both militarily and politically. While large-scale U.S. involvement had not yet materialized, 1960 witnessed a significant increase in **guerrilla activity in South Vietnam**, the formal creation of the **National Liberation Front (NLF)**, and growing concern within the **United States government** over the stability of the South Vietnamese regime under **President Ngô Đình Diệm**.

These developments further solidified the division between **North Vietnam**, governed by the communist **Democratic Republic of Vietnam (DRV)** under **Ho Chi Minh**, and **South Vietnam**, led by Diệm and supported by the **United States**. The political and ideological divergence between the two Vietnamese states, combined with Cold War tensions, transformed the conflict from a localized insurgency into a broader geopolitical confrontation.

---

### Background

Following the **1954 Geneva Accords**, Vietnam was temporarily divided at the **17th parallel**, pending national elections scheduled for 1956, which never occurred. By 1960, the division had hardened into a de facto political and military boundary between the communist North and the anti-communist South.

Throughout the late 1950s, discontent with Diệm’s authoritarian rule, especially in rural areas, contributed to increasing unrest. This discontent was exploited by former **Viet Minh** cadres and communist sympathizers who had remained in the South after the Geneva partition. By 1959, North Vietnam had formally shifted to a strategy of **armed struggle** to unify the country, leading to a steady increase in attacks on South Vietnamese military and administrative targets.

---

### Founding of the National Liberation Front

One of the most consequential developments of 1960 was the **formation of the National Liberation Front (NLF)** on **December 20, 1960**, in **Tay Ninh Province**. The NLF was a coalition of communist and non-communist forces opposed to the Diệm regime. While officially presented as a grassroots movement representing southern political and social opposition, it was widely understood to be backed, guided, and supplied by the **DRV in Hanoi**.

The NLF’s platform called for the overthrow of Diệm, the removal of U.S. influence, land reform, and eventual reunification with the North. It sought to unite various anti-Diệm factions, including disaffected peasants, former Viet Minh, religious sects, and intellectuals. The front would later be labeled by U.S. and South Vietnamese officials as the **Viet Cong**, though this term was not used by the organization itself.

The founding of the NLF marked a strategic consolidation of the insurgency in the South and formalized the ideological and political contest between North and South Vietnam.

---

### Growing Instability in South Vietnam

President **Ngô Đình Diệm**, who had declared the **Republic of Vietnam** in 1955, continued to exercise authoritarian control in 1960. His regime relied heavily on family networks, secret police, and the military to suppress dissent. Diệm’s policies, including land inequality, religious favoritism (particularly toward Catholics), and repression of political opposition, fueled resentment among rural populations and urban elites alike.

In **November 1960**, a failed **coup attempt** by disgruntled military officers highlighted growing dissatisfaction within the South Vietnamese army (ARVN). Although the coup was crushed within two days, it exposed weaknesses in Diệm’s control and raised concerns in Washington about the long-term viability of his government.

At the same time, the **Strategic Hamlet Program**, still in its early stages, began to take shape. Intended to isolate rural populations from insurgent influence by relocating villagers into fortified communities, the program would become a cornerstone of Diệm’s counterinsurgency efforts in the years ahead—though its coercive implementation would ultimately backfire.

---

### Communist Strategy and Support from the North

In the North, the Vietnamese Workers’ Party (VWP) continued to support the growing insurgency in the South. Although Hanoi remained cautious about overt military involvement, it provided the NLF with **political guidance, cadres, weapons, and logistical support** via the expanding **Ho Chi Minh Trail**, which traversed Laos and Cambodia.

North Vietnam framed its support for the NLF as part of a broader anti-colonial and national liberation struggle. While publicly denying direct military involvement, the North’s leadership increasingly viewed the insurgency as the primary mechanism to undermine and eventually topple the Diệm regime.

---

### U.S. Policy and Strategic Concerns

The United States remained committed to supporting South Vietnam in 1960, both as part of its broader **Cold War containment strategy** and in response to the perceived threat of communist expansion in Southeast Asia. The **domino theory**, which held that the fall of one nation to communism would lead to the collapse of its neighbors, continued to shape U.S. thinking.

Throughout the year, the **Military Assistance Advisory Group (MAAG)** expanded its efforts to train and equip the ARVN. U.S. military personnel in Vietnam remained in advisory roles, although some were already involved in planning counterinsurgency operations. American policymakers expressed growing concern about the Diệm regime’s ability to govern effectively and counter the insurgency, but no immediate changes to the level of U.S. involvement were made.

The transition from the Eisenhower to the Kennedy administration in November 1960 did not immediately shift Vietnam policy, but the incoming team viewed the situation with fresh urgency, setting the stage for increased U.S. engagement in 1961.

---

### Conclusion

The year **1960** represented a significant escalation in the political and military dimensions of the Vietnam War. With the establishment of the **National Liberation Front**, increased guerrilla attacks in the South, and persistent instability within the Diệm regime, the conflict entered a new phase of organized insurgency and counterinsurgency.

Though full-scale war between the U.S.-backed South and the communist-led North had not yet begun, the foundational structures of both resistance and repression were in place. The developments of 1960 set the stage for the intensification of American involvement and a prolonged and complex war in the years that followed.
