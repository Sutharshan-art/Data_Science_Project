**Green Cross International** (GCI) is a non-governmental, non-profit organization dedicated to addressing the interconnected global challenges of environmental degradation, resource scarcity, and security threats resulting from unsustainable development. Founded in 1993 by Mikhail Gorbachev in the wake of the 1992 United Nations Conference on Environment and Development (“Earth Summit”) in Rio de Janeiro, GCI leverages political engagement, technical expertise, and grassroots partnerships to promote sustainable management of water, energy, and ecosystems, while advancing conflict prevention and post-crisis recovery.

**Historical and Political Context**
The end of the Cold War in the early 1990s reshaped global governance, spotlighting transnational risks such as climate change, water stress, and hazardous legacy sites. Against this backdrop, Gorbachev—formerly General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union—advocated for “green cross” principles linking environmental stewardship to peace and security. In 1992, he and UN Secretary-General Boutros Boutros-Ghali issued the Moscow Declaration calling for an organization that would address “the environmental consequences of armed conflict, eliminate nuclear and other weapons, and secure a sustainable future.” Green Cross International was formally established the following year to advance this vision.

**Mission and Objectives**
GCI’s core mission is to ensure “a secure and sustainable future for succeeding generations” by:

1. **Promoting Environmental Security**: Preventing conflicts over natural resources and assisting communities in post-conflict environmental recovery.
2. **Facilitating Sustainable Development**: Supporting water-management, renewable-energy, and climate-adaptation projects, particularly in water-scarce and vulnerable regions.
3. **Advancing Nuclear and Chemical Disarmament**: Remediating contaminated sites and educating policymakers on the security risks of radioactive and toxic pollution.
4. **Strengthening Global Governance**: Engaging with the United Nations, regional bodies, and national governments to integrate environmental considerations into peacebuilding and development frameworks.

**Organizational Structure**
Green Cross International operates as a federation of national and regional organizations, each governed by a local board under the umbrella of the GCI Council headquartered in Geneva, Switzerland. Key bodies include:

* **Council of Founders**: Advises on strategic direction, chaired by the President of GCI (a position first held by Gorbachev).
* **Board of Directors**: Oversees global policies, budgets, and organizational integrity.
* **Secretariat**: Led by a Secretary-General, manages day-to-day operations, program development, and partnership coordination.

As of 2024, GCI comprises over 30 national affiliates—such as Green Cross Russia, Green Cross Switzerland, and Green Cross Cambodia—implementing locally tailored initiatives while contributing to global policy dialogues.

**Major Programs and Initiatives**

* **Water for Peace**: Launched in the mid-1990s, this program supports transboundary water cooperation in basins such as the Jordan River, the Ili-Balkhash basin (Central Asia), and the Nile, mitigating water-related tensions through joint management and infrastructure development.
* **Nuclear and Environmental Remediation**: In partnership with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and national authorities, GCI has led projects to decontaminate former Soviet military sites, treat radioactive groundwater, and train local engineers in safe waste disposal.
* **Climate Resilience and Renewable Energy**: Working with UN agencies and local governments, GCI implements solar-power installations in off-grid communities in Africa and Central Asia, coupled with climate-resilient agriculture and watershed restoration.
* **Youth and Leadership**: Through the “Green Cross Youth Corps,” GCI engages young leaders in environmental diplomacy skills, replicating models used in post-conflict Bosnia and Herzegovina to foster reconciliation and sustainable livelihoods.

**Partnerships and Policy Influence**
GCI maintains consultative status with the United Nations Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC) and collaborates with UNESCO, UNEP, and the World Bank. It has contributed to major international frameworks including the UN Watercourses Convention (1997), the Aarhus Convention on Environmental Democracy (1998), and the Paris Agreement on climate change (2015), advocating for integrated security-environment policies.

**Criticism and Challenges**
Critics argue that GCI’s linkage of environmental issues to security agendas risks securitizing development challenges, potentially prioritizing strategic interests over community-driven solutions. Funding constraints and reliance on government and corporate partners sometimes constrain its independence. Moreover, measuring long-term peacebuilding outcomes remains complex, leading some scholars to call for more rigorous impact evaluations.

**Legacy and Continuing Relevance**
Over three decades, Green Cross International has pioneered the concept of “environmental peacemaking,” demonstrating that cooperation over shared resources can reduce conflict risks. Its early work on nuclear legacy sites anticipated contemporary debates on the security dimensions of environmental disasters. As climate change intensifies geostrategic competition over water and arable land, GCI’s model—combining technical rehabilitation with diplomatic facilitation—remains influential in shaping holistic approaches to global security and sustainability.
