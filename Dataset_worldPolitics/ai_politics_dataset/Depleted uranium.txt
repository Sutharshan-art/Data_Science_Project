**Depleted Uranium**

**Depleted uranium** (DU) is uranium in which the proportion of the fissile isotope uranium-235 has been reduced through the process of isotope separation. While natural uranium contains approximately 0.72 percent U-235 by mass, depleted uranium typically contains about 0.2–0.3 percent U-235, with the balance being U-238. DU retains the high density (about 19.1 g/cm³) characteristic of uranium and finds applications in both civilian and military contexts.

## Production and Properties

Depleted uranium is generated as a by-product of the uranium enrichment process used to produce reactor fuel and nuclear weapons material. During enrichment, the concentration of U-235 is increased for use in nuclear reactors or weapons, leaving a surplus stream of uranium depleted in U-235. DU is both pyrophoric—capable of spontaneously igniting when finely divided—and weakly radioactive, emitting primarily low-energy alpha particles that are mitigated by external shielding but pose an inhalation or ingestion hazard if particles become airborne.

## Military Applications

### Armor-Penetrating Munitions

One of the principal military uses of depleted uranium is in armor-penetrating kinetic energy penetrators. The high density and self-sharpening behavior of DU rods upon impact enable them to defeat heavy armored vehicles more effectively than tungsten or steel alternatives. DU munitions entered widespread use by the United States and United Kingdom during the Gulf War (1991) and have since been employed in conflicts including the Balkans (1995–1999), the Iraq War (2003–2011), and the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine.

### Armored Vehicle Armor

Some tanks and armored vehicles incorporate depleted uranium alloy plates in their composite armor, leveraging DU’s density and hardness to improve protection against incoming projectiles. Examples include the U.S. M1 Abrams and the British Challenger 2 main battle tanks.

## Political and Ethical Debate

### International Response

The use of DU in conflict zones has prompted international debate over its health and environmental impacts. In 2007, the United Nations General Assembly adopted a nonbinding resolution urging member states to consider restricting DU weapons and to support research on their consequences. Several countries, including Belgium, Costa Rica, and Hungary, have called for international treaties to ban or limit DU munitions, while NATO and the United States maintain that DU weapons are compliant with international humanitarian law.

### Health and Environmental Concerns

Critics argue that DU dust generated by impact and subsequent fires can contaminate soil and water, potentially exposing military personnel and civilian populations to chemical toxicity and radiological hazards. Studies commissioned by the World Health Organization and the International Atomic Energy Agency have concluded that under typical exposure scenarios, the radiological risk from DU is low, but uncertainties remain regarding long-term effects and internal exposure pathways. Some veteran and civilian groups report elevated rates of cancers, birth defects, and kidney problems, though attributing causality to DU exposure has proven scientifically challenging.

### National Policies

* **United States**: Continues to produce and stockpile DU for military use. The U.S. Department of Defense conducts environmental remediation of training ranges and accident sites.
* **United Kingdom**: Retains DU munitions in its arsenal. The UK government has funded research and clearance operations in former conflict zones, including Kosovo and Iraq.
* **France and Russia**: Employ DU rounds in select armored units but have limited public discussion of environmental remediation.
* **International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)**: Provides guidance on storage, handling, and remediation of DU compounds, emphasizing “best practice” environmental controls.

## Post‐Conflict Remediation

Several countries and non‐governmental organizations have undertaken clearing and monitoring projects in regions where DU munitions were used. In Kosovo, a NATO-led survey identified hundreds of DU impact sites; clean-up operations and long‐term environmental monitoring are ongoing. In Iraq, UN Environment Programme (UNEP) assessments have mapped contamination but noted logistical and security challenges to large‐scale remediation.

## Legal and Regulatory Framework

No specific international treaty exclusively bans DU weapons, but their use is subject to general arms-control and environmental law principles:

* **Geneva Conventions**: Prohibit weapons that cause unnecessary suffering or long‐term environmental damage.
* **Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW)**: Contains protocols on explosive remnants of war; discussions have considered DU under emerging environmental annexes.
* **National Regulations**: Export controls, hazardous waste statutes, and radiological safety standards govern DU production, storage, and disposal within individual states.

## Legacy and Ongoing Challenges

Depleted uranium remains a subject of political contention at the intersection of military necessity, public health, and environmental protection. Proponents highlight DU’s battlefield effectiveness and compliance with humanitarian law, while opponents underscore unresolved questions about long‐term toxicity and environmental persistence. Ongoing research by international organizations aims to clarify exposure risks and to develop guidelines for the safe handling of DU legacy stockpiles and contaminated sites. As global attitudes toward arms control and environmental stewardship evolve, the debate over depleted uranium is likely to continue shaping defense policies and international law.
