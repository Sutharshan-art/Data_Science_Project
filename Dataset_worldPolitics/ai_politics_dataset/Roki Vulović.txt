**Roki Vulović**

**Overview**
Radojka “Roki” Vulović (born October 20, 1959) is a Bosnian Serb folk singer and songwriter whose career became closely associated with Serb nationalist politics during the Yugoslav Wars of the 1990s. Best known for his patriotic and martial ballads supporting the Army of Republika Srpska, Vulović’s music was embraced by many ethnic Serbs as expressions of resistance and national pride, while critics have accused him of propagating divisive wartime propaganda.

**Early Life and Musical Beginnings**
Vulović was born in Sarajevo, then part of the Socialist Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina in the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia. He studied veterinary medicine at the University of Sarajevo but cultivated a parallel interest in music, performing at local cafés and festivals. Influenced by traditional sevdalinka and turbo-folk, he adopted the stage name “Roki” and began recording singles in the early 1980s, achieving modest regional success with romantic ballads and folk dances.

**Rise to Prominence**
By the late 1980s, Vulović had released several studio albums blending folk instrumentation with contemporary pop arrangements. His 1988 album *Ljubav i patriotizam* (“Love and Patriotism”) contained early hints of national themes, but it was the outbreak of war in Bosnia and Herzegovina in 1992 that catapulted him to wider attention. Relocating to Belgrade amid rising interethnic violence, Vulović began collaborating with state‐aligned media and military cultural brigades, performing for troops and at government‐sponsored rallies.

**Wartime Music and Political Alignment**
During the Bosnian War (1992–1995), Vulović’s repertoire shifted decisively toward patriotic anthems. Songs such as “Oj Srbijo, meni dušo” (“Oh Serbia, My Soul”) and “Heroji s Drine” (“Heroes of the Drina”) extolled the deeds of Bosnian Serb fighters under General Ratko Mladić and political leader Radovan Karadžić. His music videos intercut his performances with footage of paratroopers, siege scenes from Sarajevo, and images of battlefields, reinforcing narratives of heroic defense and existential struggle. Vulović openly endorsed the Republika Srpska leadership and was granted honorary rank in the Army’s cultural unit, earning both praise from Serb nationalist circles and condemnation from human‐rights groups.

**Controversies and Criticism**
Vulović’s close association with wartime authorities drew sharp criticism. International observers and Bosniak and Croat organizations accused him of spreading ethnic hatred and glorifying leaders later indicted for war crimes by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY). After the Srebrenica genocide in July 1995, Vulović released “Srebrenički Padre” (“Padre of Srebrenica”), a song praising paramilitary chaplains, leading the European Broadcasting Union to ban its airplay and prompting legal complaints in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

**Post-War Career and Legal Challenges**
Following the Dayton Peace Agreement (1995), Vulović remained active in the former Yugoslav music market, though his overt nationalism limited his audience in Bosnia and Croatia. He toured predominantly in Serbia and Republika Srpska, where he was protected from extradition requests. In 2003, Bosnian prosecutors issued an arrest warrant alleging incitement to ethnic hatred; Vulović remained at large, citing freedom of expression. He continued to release albums—such as *Srpski Vitez* (“Serbian Knight,” 2001) and *Ostani Vojniche* (“Remain a Soldier,” 2005”)—each reinforcing themes of loyalty to the Serb homeland.

**Cultural and Political Impact**
Vulović’s music played a significant role in shaping Serb wartime identity. Academic studies of wartime propaganda note his songs’ use of familiar folk idioms to legitimize military action and foster communal solidarity. His concerts doubled as political gatherings, often featuring speeches by nationalist politicians and displays of flags and insignia. While some fans regard him as a cultural icon preserving Serb heritage under siege, others view him as a propagandist whose work exacerbated ethnic divisions.

**Later Activities and Legacy**
In the 2010s, Vulović moderated his public persona, occasionally performing humanitarian benefit concerts for war veterans and displaced families. He granted interviews distancing himself from extremist politics, emphasizing nostalgia and cultural preservation. Nevertheless, his wartime catalog remains controversial: in Bosnia and Herzegovina, several municipalities prohibit performances of his songs, and streaming platforms have flagged them under hate‐speech policies.

**Conclusion**
Roger Vulović’s career exemplifies the intersection of popular culture and nationalist politics in wartime. His music provided a soundtrack for Bosnian Serb combatants and sympathizers, reinforcing collective narratives of honor and survival. Subject to legal restrictions and moral reproach, Vulović remains a contentious figure whose legacy continues to spark debate over art, propaganda, and accountability in post-conflict societies.
