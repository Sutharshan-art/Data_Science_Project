**United States War Crimes**

**Overview**
Allegations of war crimes committed by United States military personnel and agencies span multiple conflicts from the nineteenth century to the present. These allegations encompass intentional targeting of civilians, mistreatment of prisoners, use of prohibited weapons, and unlawful interrogation practices. While U.S. policy officially condemns and prohibits war crimes under domestic and international law, periodic incidents have prompted investigations, courts-martial, and broader political debate over accountability.

**Legal Framework**
U.S. forces are bound by the Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ), the Geneva Conventions of 1949 (which the United States ratified), and customary international humanitarian law. War crimes are defined as serious violations—such as murder of non‐combatants, torture, taking of hostages, and wilfully causing unnecessary suffering—that contravene the laws and customs of war. The War Crimes Act of 1996 implements international treaties into U.S. law, criminalizing grave breaches of the Geneva Conventions.

**Revolutionary to Civil Wars**
During the Mexican–American War (1846–1848) and the Philippine–American War (1899–1902), U.S. forces employed tactics—such as the burning of villages, summary executions of suspected insurgents, and reconcentration of civilian populations—that modern scholars characterize as breaches of the laws of armed conflict. In the American Civil War (1861–1865), both Union and Confederate forces engaged in harsh reprisals and civilian property destruction, though formal war‐crime jurisprudence was not yet established.

**World War II**
While U.S. units generally adhered to Allied policies, some bombing campaigns and post‐combat actions raised questions under contemporary norms. The incendiary bombing of Japanese cities, including Tokyo, killed large numbers of civilians, and the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945 remain subjects of legal and moral debate. In Europe, isolated incidents—such as unjustified executions of German prisoners—led to courts‐martial, but no comprehensive reckoning akin to the Nuremberg Trials occurred for U.S. personnel.

**Korean War**
During the 1950–1953 Korean War, U.S. forces were accused of summary executions of prisoners and civilians—most notably the No Gun Ri incident in July 1950, when U.S. troops fired on retreating South Korean civilians, killing an estimated 100–200. A U.S. Army investigation in 2001 acknowledged that soldiers had killed non‐combatants but concluded no war‐crime prosecutions would occur, citing lack of evidence implicating specific individuals.

**Vietnam War**
The Vietnam War saw the most extensive U.S. war‐crime allegations to date. The 1968 My Lai massacre, in which U.S. Army troops killed at least 347 unarmed Vietnamese civilians, led to the court‐martial of Lieutenant William Calley. Convicted of murder, Calley's sentence was later commuted. Other incidents—such as the Phoenix Program targeting suspected Viet Cong cadres and reports of widespread use of torture and sexual violence—prompted military inquiries but produced few convictions. The conflict also raised questions over the use of chemical agents (Agent Orange) and area bombing that caused extensive civilian harm.

**Post-Cold War Interventions**
In the 1991 Gulf War, allegations included unlawful bombardment of civilian infrastructure and the killing of retreating Iraqi troops. A 1996 Iraq‐survey commission convened by Congress examined these and other claims—such as attacks on civilian ships in the Persian Gulf—and generally concluded that while errors occurred, there was no evidence of deliberate war‐crime policies.

**War on Terror**
Following the September 11, 2001, attacks, U.S. operations in Afghanistan (2001–2021) and Iraq (2003–2011) generated new controversies:

* **Detention and Interrogation:** The use of “enhanced interrogation techniques” at Guantánamo Bay, Abu Ghraib prison, and CIA black sites—such as waterboarding, sleep deprivation, and stress positions—was widely condemned as torture. Several military personnel and contractors faced courts‐martial for prisoner abuse at Abu Ghraib; however, higher‐level accountability was limited.
* **Civilian Casualties:** Air and ground operations led to significant non‐combatant deaths, notably during raids and airstrikes in Fallujah (2004) and Kandahar. While the U.S. military conducted investigations and, in some cases, disciplinary actions, questions persisted about compliance with proportionality and distinction principles.
* **Targeted Killings and Drones:** The drone campaign against suspected terrorists resulted in civilian casualties, raising legal debates over the classification of combatants and the geographic scope of lawful targeting.

**Accountability and Reform**
Efforts to hold U.S. personnel accountable have been uneven. Lower‐ranking service members more frequently faced courts‐martial for isolated incidents, while systemic policies—such as interrogation directives—often escaped formal prosecution. Congressional inquiries, Department of Defense reviews, and reporting by human‐rights organizations prompted revisions to doctrine: the U.S. Army Field Manual was updated to prohibit unauthorized interrogation methods, and the Military Commission Act of 2006 reformed procedures for detaining and trying suspected terrorists.

**Political and Public Debate**
Allegations of war crimes have spurred domestic political controversies, influencing public support for military engagements and debates over the balance between security and human rights. Landmark publications—such as Seymour Hersh’s exposés—and non‐governmental investigations by groups like Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch kept issues in the public eye. The U.S. government’s responses ranged from defensive legal arguments to policy reforms aimed at reinforcing adherence to the laws of armed conflict.

**Legacy**
U.S. war‐crime allegations reflect the tension between military necessity and humanitarian principles. Over time, evolving international norms, media scrutiny, and legal reforms have sought to constrain abuses and enhance accountability. Incidents from My Lai to Abu Ghraib continue to shape military training, operational planning, and legislative oversight, underscoring the ongoing challenge of ensuring that U.S. armed forces uphold the rule of law in armed conflict.
