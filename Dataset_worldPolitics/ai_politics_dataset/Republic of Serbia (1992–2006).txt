**Republic of Serbia (1992–2006)**

**Overview**
The Republic of Serbia was a constituent state of two successive federal entities—first the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (FRY; 1992–2003) and then the State Union of Serbia and Montenegro (2003–2006). During this period, Serbia underwent war and sanctions in the 1990s, authoritarian rule under President Slobodan Milošević, democratic transition in 2000, and eventual dissolution of the union with Montenegro in 2006.

**Formation and Constitutional Framework**
Following the breakup of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia in 1991–1992, Serbia and Montenegro formed the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia on 27 April 1992. Serbia adopted a new republican constitution in September 1992, affirming its status as a sovereign republic within the FRY. The 1992 Constitution centralized authority in Belgrade, curtailing the autonomous powers of the provinces of Vojvodina and Kosovo, both of which were demoted from full republic status and had their legislatures placed under tighter federal and republican control.

**Milosević Era, Wars, and Sanctions (1992–2000)**
Under the leadership of Slobodan Milošević (President of Serbia, 1989–1997; President of the FRY, 1997–2000), Serbia played a central role in the conflicts in Croatia (1991–1995) and Bosnia and Herzegovina (1992–1995). Its political and military support for Bosnian Serb and Croatian Serb forces drew international condemnation. In 1998–1999, the Kosovo conflict—between Yugoslav/Serbian security forces and the Kosovo Liberation Army—escalated into a humanitarian crisis. NATO’s 78-day air campaign (March–June 1999) compelled Serbian forces to withdraw from Kosovo and led to the establishment of a UN administration (UNMIK) and NATO security presence (KFOR).

Economic and diplomatic sanctions imposed by the United Nations and the European Community from 1992 fractured Serbia’s economy, resulting in hyperinflation, industrial decline, and international isolation. Political dissent was suppressed, media were state-controlled, and opposition figures faced harassment.

**Democratic Opposition and Transition (2000–2003)**
Growing economic hardship and political repression fueled opposition unity. On 5 October 2000, mass protests known as the “Bulldozer Revolution” followed the disputed presidential election results, forcing Milošević’s resignation. A transitional government installed Vojislav Koštunica (Democratic Opposition of Serbia) as FRY President; Zoran Đinđić, leader of the Democratic Party, was appointed Prime Minister of Serbia in January 2001.

The new government embarked on democratization, market reform, and cooperation with the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY). Notable achievements included the return of multi-party local elections, restoration of provincial autonomy in Vojvodina, economic stabilization measures, and the arrest of Milošević in 2001 for extradition to The Hague. However, reform efforts were hampered by entrenched patronage networks and the assassination of Prime Minister Đinđić in March 2003.

**State Union of Serbia and Montenegro (2003–2006)**
Under the Constitutional Charter of February 2003, the FRY was reconstituted as the State Union of Serbia and Montenegro, a looser confederation in which foreign policy and defense were shared competencies while other powers were devolved to the republics. Serbia adopted revisions to its constitution to reflect this new arrangement but retained much of its institutional continuity.

Montenegro’s increasing push for independence culminated in a referendum held on 21 May 2006. With just over 55% voting for separation—meeting the 55% threshold stipulated by the European Union—Montenegro declared independence on 3 June 2006. Serbia proclaimed itself the legal successor state to the union on 5 June 2006, marking the end of the Republic of Serbia’s 14-year role within a federal structure and the emergence of the modern Republic of Serbia.

**Political System and Governance**
Throughout the period, Serbia maintained a parliamentary system with a directly elected president (until 2006) and a unicameral National Assembly. Under Milošević, constitutional amendments augmented presidential powers and undermined judicial independence. After 2000, successive reformist governments sought to strengthen the rule of law, combat corruption, and amend the electoral system to encourage coalition-building.

Kosovo remained an unresolved issue: after 1999, it was governed by the United Nations Interim Administration Mission in Kosovo (UNMIK), and Serbia continued to claim it as an autonomous province under its constitution, a claim unrecognized by international authorities.

**Foreign Relations**
During the 1990s, Serbia’s relations with Western countries were adversarial due to its role in the Yugoslav wars. Cooperation with the ICTY and normalization talks began after 2000, leading to the lifting of most EU sanctions by 2001. Serbia sought to join the Council of Europe (achieved in 2003) and pursued EU and NATO Partnership for Peace programs, while preserving traditional ties with Russia and China.

**Economic and Social Developments**
Sanctions and war disrupted Serbia’s economy; following the democratic transition, reforms focused on privatization, foreign investment, and integration into international financial institutions. Growth resumed in the early 2000s, but unemployment and regional disparities remained high. Social recovery was challenged by the legacy of refugees from Croatia, Bosnia, and later Kosovo, as well as the need to address war crimes and transitional justice.

**Legacy**
The Republic of Serbia (1992–2006) witnessed profound upheaval: from authoritarian rule, conflict, and isolation to democratic renewal and reintegration into Europe. Its evolution set the stage for Serbia’s subsequent EU accession efforts, ongoing negotiations over Kosovo’s status, and the consolidation of democratic institutions in the years after 2006.
