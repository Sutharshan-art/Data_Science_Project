**United States Support for Iraq during the Iran–Iraq War**

**Overview**
During the Iran–Iraq War (September 1980 – August 1988), the United States pursued a policy of strategic balancing that increasingly favored Iraq under President Saddam Hussein. Concerned that Iran’s revolutionary government might prevail and expand its influence, U.S. policy-makers provided diplomatic backing, intelligence sharing, economic assistance, and logistical support to Iraq—while publicly maintaining an official stance of neutrality.

**Historical and Political Context**
Following the 1979 Iranian Revolution and the overthrow of the pro-Western Shah, U.S.–Iranian relations collapsed. The subsequent hostage crisis (November 1979 – January 1981) deepened American mistrust of the Islamic Republic. When Iraq invaded Iran on 22 September 1980, the Carter administration initially expressed regret over the outbreak of hostilities but soon came to view Iraqi resistance as a buffer against Iranian revolutionary export. Under President Ronald Reagan, U.S. policy evolved toward active measures to prevent an Iranian victory without directly intervening on the battlefield.

**Diplomatic and Political Support**
From 1980, Washington provided Iraq with tacit diplomatic support at the United Nations and in bilateral contacts. U.S. officials lobbied Arab and Western governments to recognize Iraq’s territorial gains and to resist calls for an immediate cease-fire that might solidify Iranian revolutionary gains. The U.S. maintained formal relations with Iraq—unlike Iran—and kept its embassy in Baghdad open as a key channel for communication and intelligence exchange.

**Intelligence Sharing and Military Advice**
Throughout the conflict, U.S. intelligence agencies—including the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) and the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA)—monitored Iranian troop movements via signals intercepts and satellite reconnaissance. Declassified accounts indicate that portions of this intelligence were shared with Iraqi counterparts, enabling more effective Iraqi operations along the front. U.S. military attachés in Baghdad provided assessments of Iranian capabilities and occasional tactical advice, though no overt U.S. military personnel participated in combat operations.

**Economic and Financial Assistance**
Recognizing Iraq’s growing financial strains—exacerbated by plunging oil revenues—U.S. diplomats encouraged Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and other Gulf monarchies to extend low-interest loans and to purchase Iraqi oil. These Gulf contributions, in coordination with U.S. diplomatic persuasion, helped sustain Iraq’s war economy. The United States also approved agricultural sales and dual‐use industrial equipment exports to Iraq under the Export Administration Act, subject to restrictions aimed at limiting weapons proliferation.

**Dual-Use and Covert Transfers**
While overt arms transfers to Iraq remained constrained by U.S. arms embargoes in effect from 1984 onward, Iraq acquired significant quantities of military hardware—tanks, artillery, helicopters, and chemical precursors—through third-country intermediaries and licit trade in dual‐use technologies. Western European and Eastern Bloc suppliers formed the bulk of conventional arms flows; however, U.S. exports of machine tools, electronics, and chemical process equipment contributed indirectly to Iraq’s capacity to produce weapons systems, including chemical agents.

**Maritime Security and Reflagging Operations**
Beginning in 1984, both Iran and Iraq targeted oil tankers in the Persian Gulf, endangering vital global energy supplies. In response to repeated Iranian attacks on neutral shipping, the United States launched Operation Earnest Will (1987–1988), reflagging Kuwaiti tankers under the U.S. flag and deploying naval escorts to protect them. While ostensibly aimed at ensuring freedom of navigation, these operations effectively bolstered Iraq’s economic warfare by enabling Gulf states to continue oil exports despite Iranian interdiction efforts.

**Balancing Iran’s Arms for Hostages Effort**
Concurrently, senior Reagan administration figures engaged in covert arms-for-hostages negotiations with Iran—later dubbed the “Iran–Contra” affair. This parallel track involved the clandestine sale of anti-tank missiles and other matériel to Iran, with proceeds funneled to the Nicaraguan Contras. These covert sales were rationalized as a means to secure the release of American hostages in Lebanon and to moderate hard-line factions within Iran. While controversial, the Iran–Contra transactions did not detract from the broader U.S. tilt toward Iraq but rather underscored Washington’s overriding goal of limiting Iranian revolutionary influence.

**Impact and Effectiveness**
U.S. support played a significant role in sustaining Iraq’s capacity to continue fighting into the late 1980s, helping Baghdad to withstand repeated Iranian offensives—including the costly “War of the Cities” and direct attacks on oil infrastructure. By late 1987, the balance of power had shifted decisively in Iraq’s favor, prompting Iranian leadership to accept a UN-brokered cease-fire, which took effect on 20 August 1988. Analysts credit American intelligence sharing, Gulf financial backing, and naval protection of shipping as key contributors to Iraq’s endurance.

**Legacy and Controversy**
The U.S. alignment with Iraq in the Iran–Iraq War has remained controversial. Critics argue that by bolstering Saddam Hussein’s regime, the United States enabled future aggression, including the 1988 chemical-weapon attacks on Halabja and the 1990 invasion of Kuwait. Defenders of the policy maintain that, in the bipolar Cold War context, supporting Iraq was the lesser strategic evil compared to an expansionist revolutionary Iran. The episode has since served as a case study in the complexities of realpolitik and the unintended consequences of proxy support in protracted regional conflicts.
