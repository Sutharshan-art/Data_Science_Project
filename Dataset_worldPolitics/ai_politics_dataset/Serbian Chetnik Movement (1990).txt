**Serbian Chetnik Movement (1990)**

The **Serbian Chetnik Movement** (Serbian: Српски четнички покрет, *Srpski četnički pokret*; SČP), often referred to as the “Šešeljevci,” was an ultranationalist political and paramilitary organization founded in mid-1990 by Vojislav Šešelj. Drawing its name and symbols from the World War II Chetnik movement, the SČP sought to mobilize support for the creation of a “Greater Serbia” by uniting all Serb-inhabited territories. Although barred from formal political party status due to its extremist ideology and paramilitary activities, the Movement rapidly evolved into a significant force during the early stages of the Yugoslav Wars, operating both in the political sphere and on the battlefield.

**Origins and Political Activity**
In June 1990, Vojislav Šešelj announced the formation of the Serbian Chetnik Movement in Belgrade. The Movement’s platform combined monarchist nostalgia, Orthodox Christian symbolism, and militant nationalism. Its public rallies featured traditional Chetnik iconography—black uniforms, the Serbian cross, and portraits of World War II leader Draža Mihailović—alongside nationalist slogans demanding territorial expansion. Because Serbia’s Ministry of Justice refused to register the SČP as a legitimate party, Šešelj ran as an independent in the 1990 Serbian presidential election, gaining notable attention but only a small share of the vote.

Denied legal status, the SČP merged with the People’s Radical Party in early 1991 to form the **Serbian Radical Party** (SRS), with Šešelj as president and former SČP deputies filling key leadership roles. The new party carried forward the Chetnik Movement’s ideology while gaining formal access to Serbia’s electoral system. The Radical Party’s platform emphasized Serbian unity, republicanism, and opposition to Yugoslav federation dissolution without guarantees for Serb minorities.

**Paramilitary Organization and Operations**
Parallel to its political activities, the SČP maintained an armed wing composed of volunteers drawn from Serbian nationalist circles, disaffected veterans, and individuals with criminal backgrounds. This militia operated semi-independently alongside the Yugoslav People’s Army (JNA) and local Serb Territorial Defense units. Early deployments took place in Eastern Slavonia and Western Syrmia during clashes with Croatian forces in 1991. The militia was notorious for its involvement in the Battle of Borovo Selo and subsequent reprisals against Croatian policemen and civilians.

With the outbreak of the Bosnian War in 1992, former SČP fighters—now integrated into SRS paramilitary formations—deployed to the Serb-controlled regions of Bosnia and Herzegovina. They took part in operations around Bijeljina, Zvornik, and Srebrenica, where state- and non-state forces conducted campaigns of ethnic cleansing against Bosniak civilians. Reports from human rights observers and later indictments by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) accused these units of summary executions, forced displacement, and other atrocities.

**Dissolution and Integration**
Under international and domestic pressure to curb paramilitary violence following the Dayton Accords in late 1995, the Milošević government moved to disband numerous irregular units. In April 1994, the SRS officially announced the dissolution of the Serbian Chetnik Movement as an autonomous militia. Many former SČP members were absorbed into the SRS’s broader paramilitary structures or unofficially into state security services, notably the Special Operations Unit of the State Security Service. Despite its formal end, the movement’s ideological legacy persisted within radical nationalist circles and continued to influence Serbian politics through the Radical Party.

**Legacy and Impact**
The Serbian Chetnik Movement’s invocation of World War II Chetnik symbolism marked a revival of right-wing nationalist imagery in Serbia’s post-communist landscape. Its rapid militarization and direct involvement in early wartime atrocities demonstrated how fringe political movements could leverage ideological fervor into brutal paramilitary power. While the formal paramilitary arm ceased to exist by 1996, its veterans and ideology left an enduring imprint on the Serbian Radical Party, which remained a significant political force into the 2000s.

The movement’s history has been central to debates on accountability, national memory, and the reintegration of former combatants into civilian life. In the early 2000s, Serbia’s cooperation with the ICTY led to the indictment and trial of several figures linked to the SČP and SRS militias. These proceedings contributed to a broader process of transitional justice, even as nationalist sentiment and Šešelj’s later acquittal by the ICTY continued to polarize public opinion. Today, the SČP is studied as a case of ultranationalist revival in times of state collapse and war, illustrating the dangers posed by the fusion of extremist ideology with armed force.
