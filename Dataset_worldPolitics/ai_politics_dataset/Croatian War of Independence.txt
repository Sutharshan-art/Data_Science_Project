**Croatian War of Independence**

The Croatian War of Independence was an armed conflict fought from March 1991 to November 1995 between the Republic of Croatia and local Serb forces, supported by the Yugoslav People’s Army (JNA) and various paramilitary groups. It was the first of the Yugoslav Wars to erupt following the breakup of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (SFRY), and resulted in Croatia’s full international recognition and de facto independence.

## Background

In the late 1980s, nationalist sentiments surged across the constituent republics of the SFRY as economic stagnation and political liberalization weakened federal authority. In Croatia, the Croatian Democratic Union (HDZ), led by Franjo Tuđman, won the first multi-party elections in April–May 1990 on a platform emphasizing Croatian sovereignty. Ethnic Serbs, comprising roughly 12 percent of Croatia’s population, feared marginalization under the new government and formed the Serbian Autonomous Regions of Krajina, Banovina and Slavonia, seeking to remain within a Serb-dominated federation.

Tensions escalated as Croatia moved to assert control over its territory and institutions. In early 1991, Croatia declared its intention to establish its own police force and customs border; the federal JNA attempted to disarm the Croatian Territorial Defense forces. Sporadic clashes erupted, notably in the plitvice Lakes area in March 1991, marking the conflict’s beginning.

## Outbreak of Hostilities

On 17 August 1990, Serb insurgents in the Krajina region proclaimed the “Serbian Autonomous Oblast of Krajina,” rejecting Croatian authority. By early 1991, incidents of road blockades, shootings, and skirmishes between Croatian police and Serb militias intensified. When Croatia held a referendum on independence on 19 May 1991—with over 90 percent voting in favor—Serb-majority areas largely boycotted the vote. Croatia formally declared independence on 25 June 1991, triggering a full-scale conflict as the JNA intervened to support Serb separatists and to prevent secession.

## Course of the War

### 1991: Early Fighting and Siege Warfare

Initial JNA offensives in July–August 1991 captured significant territory in eastern and southern Croatia, including the strategic port of Vukovar after a three-month siege that resulted in heavy destruction and civilian casualties. Simultaneously, Serb forces laid siege to Dubrovnik, damaging its renowned Old Town. Croatian defenses, composed largely of hastily organized police and volunteer units, suffered from shortages of heavy weapons and ammunition.

### 1992–1993: Stalemate and Ceasefires

International mediation led to the Vance Plan in late 1991, deploying United Nations Protection Force (UNPROFOR) peacekeepers to establish “United Nations Protected Areas” (UNPAs) in Serb-controlled regions. While full-scale offensives subsided, low-intensity fighting, artillery duels, and occasional raids continued. Croatia struggled economically under UN sanctions, while humanitarian crises emerged in besieged towns.

### 1994–1995: Croatian Counteroffensives

By 1994, Croatia had rearmed substantially through clandestine arms imports and the establishment of its own defense industry. In May 1995, Croatia launched Operation Flash to retake the Western Slavonia pocket, regaining around 220 km² of territory within 48 hours. A larger offensive, Operation Storm, in August 1995, rapidly recaptured the Krajina region, displacing up to 200,000 Serb civilians and effectively ending the Serb rebellion. In September, Operation Mistral 2 secured additional areas in western Bosnia and Herzegovina allied to Croatia.

## International Involvement

Early in the conflict, the European Community (later the European Union) recognized the independence of Croatia on 15 January 1992, lifting the specter of full-scale war among republics. The United Nations imposed an arms embargo on all former Yugoslav republics, which hindered Croatia more than the JNA-backed Serb forces. UNPROFOR’s deployment aimed to stabilize ceasefires but struggled to prevent renewed hostilities. Following the 1995 offensives, the United States brokered the Dayton Peace Accords, primarily addressing Bosnia and Herzegovina, but effectively consolidated Croatia’s territorial gains.

## Humanitarian Impact

The war resulted in an estimated 20,000–25,000 deaths on all sides and over 450,000 refugees and internally displaced persons. Significant war crimes included the Vukovar massacre and the shelling of Dubrovnik. Widespread destruction of infrastructure and cultural heritage occurred, and minority populations on both sides suffered ethnic cleansing. Post-war return and reintegration of displaced persons proved slow and contentious.

## Aftermath and Legacy

With its territorial integrity restored, Croatia embarked on post-conflict reconstruction and joined NATO in 2009 and the European Union in 2013. The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) prosecuted senior political and military leaders for war crimes. The conflict’s legacy endures in Croatia–Serbia relations, regional minority rights debates, and collective memory, shaping political discourse and reconciliation efforts in the Western Balkans.
