**Serbia**

**Overview**
Serbia (Serbian: Србија, *Srbija*) is a landlocked country situated at the crossroads of Central and Southeast Europe, covering an area of 77,474 km² with a population of approximately 7 million. Belgrade is its capital and largest city. Governed as a parliamentary republic, Serbia’s modern political system was established following the dissolution of the State Union of Serbia and Montenegro in 2006. Since 2012, Serbia has been an official candidate for European Union membership and participates in numerous international organizations, including the United Nations, OSCE, and Council of Europe.

**Historical Context**

* **Medieval Kingdoms (7th–15th centuries):** Slavic tribes settled the region in the 7th century. The Serbian Principality emerged in the 8th century, evolving into the Kingdom of Serbia in 1217 under Stefan Nemanjić. The Serbian Empire reached its zenith under Tsar Stefan Dušan (r. 1331–1355). Ottoman conquest began in the late 14th century, with Serbia fully incorporated into the Ottoman Empire by 1459, ending medieval statehood.
* **Ottoman and Habsburg Periods (15th–19th centuries):** Under Ottoman rule, Serb lands were governed as semi-autonomous *sanjaks*. Orthodox Christian Serbs endured heavy taxation and periodic uprisings (e.g., the Banat uprising of 1594). The northern Vojvodina region came under Habsburg control in the late 17th century, becoming a refuge for Serb intellectual and religious life.
* **National Awakening and Independence (19th century):** The First Serbian Uprising (1804) and Second Uprising (1815) led to autonomy within the Ottoman Empire and the establishment of the Principality of Serbia. Full independence was recognized at the Congress of Berlin in 1878. Serbia expanded territory in the Balkan Wars (1912–1913), laying groundwork for the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes after World War I.

**Yugoslavia and World War II**
In 1918 Serbia joined other South Slavic lands to form the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes, later renamed Yugoslavia. During World War II, Axis occupation and the puppet Nedić regime in Serbia provoked both communist-led Partisan resistance and royalist Chetnik insurgency. Post‐war, Serbia became the largest constituent republic of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia under Josip Broz Tito.

**Breakup of Yugoslavia and the 1990s**
After Tito’s death in 1980, rising nationalism and economic decline strained Yugoslavia’s federal structure. In 1991–1992, Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Macedonia seceded, triggering wars. Under President Slobodan Milošević, Serbia—alongside the Republic of Srpska in Bosnia and the Republic of Serbian Krajina in Croatia—was implicated in ethnic cleansing and war crimes, particularly in Bosnia (1992–1995) and Kosovo (1998–1999). International sanctions (1992–1995) and the 1999 NATO bombing campaign devastated Serbia’s economy and infrastructure.

**Democratization and State Union (2000–2006)**
Milošević was ousted in the October 5 revolution of 2000 and extradited to The Hague in 2001. A transitional government initiated democratic reforms and resumed cooperation with the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia. In 2003, the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia was reconstituted as the State Union of Serbia and Montenegro. Following Montenegro’s independence referendum in May 2006, Serbia proclaimed itself the successor state on June 5 2006.

**Political System**
Serbia is a parliamentary republic. The President, directly elected for a five-year term (renewable once), serves as head of state and commander-in-chief of the armed forces. The Prime Minister, nominated by the President and confirmed by the National Assembly, leads government policy. The unicameral National Assembly has 250 deputies elected by proportional representation to four-year terms.

**Major Political Parties**

* **Serbian Progressive Party (SNS):** Center-right, pro-EU, in power since 2012, led by Aleksandar Vučić.
* **Socialist Party of Serbia (SPS):** Successor to Milošević’s party, center-left, coalition partner with SNS.
* **Democratic Party (DS):** Center-left, pro-EU, leading party in the 2000 reform coalition.
* **Other parties:** Alliance for Serbia (coalition of opposition parties), Serbian Radical Party (far-right), and minor ethnic minority parties.

**Foreign Relations and EU Integration**
Serbia maintains a policy of military neutrality but aspires to join the European Union, having opened membership negotiations in 2014. Key accession challenges include cooperation with war-crimes tribunals, rule-of-law reforms, and normalization of relations with Kosovo. Serbia is a founding member of the Central European Free Trade Agreement (CEFTA) and participates in the Berlin Process for Western Balkan integration. Relations with Russia remain close, particularly in energy and cultural ties, while Serbia balances these with its EU ambitions.

**Kosovo Issue**
Kosovo declared independence in February 2008, a move recognized by over 100 UN member states but not by Serbia, which regards Kosovo as its autonomous province. The EU-led Dialogue on Normalization of Relations (2011–present) aims to normalize practical relations without prejudicing final status. The Brussels Agreement (2013) set frameworks for Serb-majority municipalities in northern Kosovo, though implementation remains contested.

**Economy and Society**
Serbia transitioned from state-owned industry to a market economy after 2000. Key sectors include agriculture, manufacturing, and services. GDP growth averaged around 4% annually in the early 2010s but slowed due to global economic conditions and the COVID-19 pandemic. Unemployment remains elevated, particularly among youth. Serbia retains significant regional disparities and continues reforms in public administration, judiciary, and anti-corruption to meet EU standards.

**Defense and Security**
The Serbian Armed Forces consist of army, air force, and river flotilla, maintaining neutrality under the 2009 Defense Strategy. Serbia participates in international peacekeeping under UN and EU mandates and in regional security initiatives such as the Regional Cooperation Council.

**Culture and Identity**
Serbian culture reflects Slavic, Byzantine, Ottoman, and Western influences. The Serbian Orthodox Church plays a central role in national identity. Folk music, epic poetry, and traditional cuisine are prominent, while contemporary arts, literature, and sports (notably tennis and basketball) enjoy international recognition.
