**Iraqi Dinar**

The Iraqi dinar (Arabic: الدينار العراقي‎, sign: د.ع; code: IQD) is the official currency of the Republic of Iraq. Introduced in 1932 to replace the Indian rupee and Gulf rupee systems instituted during the British mandate, the dinar has since served not only as a medium of exchange and store of value but also as a barometer of Iraq’s political stability, economic policy, and interactions with the international community.

### Origins and Early History (1932–1958)

Following the League of Nations–mandated end of British administration, Iraq established its own currency to assert sovereignty. On 15 April 1932, the Iraqi dinar was introduced at par with the Indian rupee (1 dinar = 1 rupee), and divided into 1,000 fils. Initially struck in silver and copper, the dinar was anchored to the sterling area until 1958. Revenue from burgeoning oil exports underpinned a relatively stable exchange rate, while modest inflation kept the currency’s value broadly steady through the Hashemite monarchy and into the initial years of republican rule.

### Republican and Baʿthist Era (1958–1990)

The 1958 revolution that abolished the monarchy ushered in a period of economic nationalism. In 1959, Iraq left the sterling area and pegged the dinar to a basket of currencies to reflect its expanding oil trade. Under Arab nationalist and later Baʿth Party governments, oil revenues financed ambitious development projects, and the Central Bank of Iraq (founded in 1947) maintained currency stability through stringent foreign exchange controls.

However, by the late 1970s and 1980s, Iraq’s fiscal position became strained by the costly Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988). To fund military expenditures, the government engaged in deficit financing, eroding foreign reserves. Though official exchange rates remained unchanged, a burgeoning black market reflected rising inflationary pressures and declining confidence in the dinar.

### Gulf War, Sanctions, and Collapse (1990–2003)

Iraq’s 1990 invasion of Kuwait prompted United Nations Security Council sanctions that sealed off oil exports and most financial transactions. The dinar’s official peg became unsustainable; by 1993, the currency had lost over 90 percent of its value on the black market. The government responded with a series of devaluations and currency reforms, including introducing a new 25-fils coin in 1994 to mollify acute coin shortages.

Chronic inflation and exchange controls drove widespread reliance on U.S. dollars for savings and large transactions. In 1995 and again in 1999, the Central Bank reissued banknotes with higher denominations (up to 25,000 dinars) and different colors to thwart counterfeiters and curb black-market trading. Despite these measures, by 2003 the dinar traded unofficially at roughly 3,000 IQD per USD.

### Post-2003 Reform and Reissue (2003–2005)

The U.S.-led coalition’s invasion in March 2003 and subsequent dismantling of state institutions prompted an urgent need to stabilize the currency. The Coalition Provisional Authority (CPA) and the newly constituted Central Bank of Iraq undertook a comprehensive, three-phase currency exchange:

1. **Phase I (October 2003):** Introduction of “Swiss” dinars—pre-2003 notes overprinted with new designs—to replace old Saddam-era notes and remove them from circulation.
2. **Phase II (December 2003):** First shipment of fully redesigned “Series II” notes (denominations of 50, 250, 1,000, 5,000, and 10,000 dinars), featuring watermarks, security threads, and modern anti-counterfeiting features.
3. **Phase III (October 2004):** Completion of Series II rollout, retiring overprinted notes and consolidating a new currency identity emphasizing Iraq’s cultural heritage and post-Baʿthist national unity.

By late 2004, the exchange stabilized at approximately 1,470 IQD per USD. The reform restored public confidence, allowed for tax revenues and government wages to be paid in domestic currency, and facilitated the reopening of banking channels.

### Monetary Policy and Exchange Regime (2005–Present)

Since 2005, the Central Bank of Iraq has maintained a managed float, intervening occasionally to smooth excessive volatility while allowing market forces to set the rate. Low global oil prices in the late 2010s placed downward pressure on reserves, but strong oil revenues in subsequent years and fiscal consolidation helped stabilize the exchange rate around 1,200–1,300 IQD per USD.

Inflation has moderated, averaging 3 – 5 percent annually since 2015, and the dinar remains fully convertible for current transactions. To deepen financial markets, the Central Bank introduced short-term treasury bills and encouraged the growth of private banking. Efforts to introduce polymer banknotes and broaden digital payments aim to modernize the currency further.

### Cultural and Political Significance

The dinar has long been intertwined with notions of sovereignty and legitimacy. Currency redesigns have symbolized regime change—from monarchy, to Baʿthist rule, and to the post-2003 republic—and have served to remove political iconography. In 2004, immediate past President Saddam Hussein’s image was eliminated entirely, replaced with motifs celebrating Mesopotamian antiquity and contemporary national reconstruction.

Speculation on the dinar’s revaluation has also attracted international attention. Although the currency’s stabilization post-2005 closed black-market exchange channels, some foreign investors have treated the dinar as a speculative asset, anticipating sharp appreciation tied to Iraq’s development. The Central Bank has discouraged such unregulated trading, emphasizing that any sustainable revaluation must reflect structural economic reforms, diversification away from oil dependence, and improved fiscal governance.

### Banknotes and Coinage

Current circulating coins include denominations of 50, 100, and 250 fils, though low-value coins see limited use due to inflation. Banknotes in active circulation are 50, 250, 1,000, 5,000, and 10,000 dinars. The Central Bank periodically reviews demands for higher-denomination notes as cost-efficiency and payment habits evolve.

### Conclusion

The Iraqi dinar’s trajectory mirrors Iraq’s modern political and economic history: its early stability under a sovereign monarchy; strains of war and authoritarian rule; collapse under sanctions; and rebuilding in the post-2003 era. As Iraq continues to navigate reconstruction, fiscal reform, and diversification, the dinar remains both a tool of monetary policy and a symbol of national identity and resilience.
