**Timeline of the Gulf War (1990–1991)**

The Gulf War (August 1990–February 1991) was a conflict precipitated by Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait and resolved through a U.S.-led coalition’s military intervention under United Nations auspices. It unfolded in two distinct phases: Operation Desert Shield, a defensive deployment to protect Saudi Arabia, and Operation Desert Storm, an offensive campaign to liberate Kuwait.

---

## 1990

**August 2**

* **Iraqi Invasion of Kuwait:** At dawn, Iraq’s 60th and 70th Armoured Brigades cross into Kuwait, swiftly overwhelming the Kuwaiti military. Saddam Hussein cites economic grievances and historical claims to justify annexation.

**August 3–6**

* **International Condemnation and UN Action:** The United Nations Security Council adopts Resolution 660, demanding Iraq’s immediate and unconditional withdrawal. Sanctions imposed under Resolution 661 freeze Iraqi assets and prohibit most trade.

**August 7–16**

* **Operation Desert Shield Begins:** President George H. W. Bush orders U.S. forces to Saudi Arabia to deter further Iraqi expansion (Saudi Operation Desert Shield). Allied contributions include forces from the United Kingdom, France, Egypt, Syria, and Gulf Cooperation Council states.

**August–October**

* **Coalition Buildup and Diplomacy:** Coalition forces amass in Saudi Arabia; diplomatic efforts by U.S. Secretary of State James Baker fail to secure a peaceful Iraqi withdrawal. The United Nations issues additional resolutions (e.g., Resolution 665, authorizing naval interdiction of Iraqi shipping).

**November–December**

* **Iraq’s Entrenchment and Threats:** Iraq fortifies positions in Kuwait, lays extensive minefields, and issues threats to Israel and coalition shipping. The U.S. deploys Patriot missile batteries to Israel. Negotiations continue but stall over Iraq’s refusal to withdraw.
* **December 20:** UN Resolution 678 sets a January 15, 1991, deadline for Iraqi withdrawal, authorizing “all necessary means” to enforce it if unmet.

---

## 1991

**January 15–16**

* **Deadline and Final Diplomatic Efforts:** The Iraqi government ignores the UN deadline. President Bush addresses the nation, urging public support for military action. Congress passes a joint resolution authorizing force.

**January 17**

* **Operation Desert Storm—Air Campaign:** At 2:38 a.m. local time, coalition aircraft launch a sustained bombing offensive targeting Iraqi command centers, air defenses, Scud missile sites, and infrastructure across Kuwait and Iraq.

**January 17–February 23**

* **Sustained Air Offensive:** Over five weeks, coalition pilots fly more than 100,000 sorties, enforcing a no-fly zone over northern and southern Iraq (protecting Kurds and Shiites) and degrading Iraqi military capacity. Scud missile attacks against Saudi Arabia and Israel provoke defensive deployments.

**February 24**

* **Ground Offensive (Operation Desert Sabre):** A U.S.-led armored thrust punches through Iraqi lines in the south, while flanking maneuvers by U.K. and French forces encircle Iraqi units. The “left hook” maneuver proves decisive.

**February 26–27**

* **Liberation of Kuwait City:** Coalition forces advance into Kuwait, encountering limited resistance. By February 27, most of Kuwait City is secured. Iraqi forces retreat toward Basra.

**February 28**

* **Ceasefire Declared:** At 9:00 a.m. local time, President Bush announces a “complete cease-fire” after 100 hours of ground combat. Iraqi units remain in southern Iraq under enforced no-fly zones.

**March–April**

* **Postwar Sanctions and Enforcement:** The UN extends the arms embargo and economic sanctions. No major combat operations resume, though coalition maintain forces in the Gulf.

---

## Political and Historical Context

**Iraqi Motivations**

* Iraq, burdened by debt from the Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988) and threatened by Gulf oil overproduction, viewed Kuwait’s overproduction and alleged slant drilling as economic warfare. Saddam sought to consolidate regional hegemony and alleviate financial strain.

**Coalition Dynamics**

* The coalition encompassed 35 nations, notable for its unanimity in the UN and regional participation (e.g., Egypt and Syria). The U.S. leveraged its post–Cold War primacy to assemble a broad, largely voluntary alliance.

**Military Innovations**

* Desert Storm showcased precision-guided munitions, stealth aircraft, and real-time satellite communications, marking a transition to “network-centric” warfare and demonstrating the effectiveness of air power in modern conflicts.

**Domestic Implications**

* The swift victory bolstered President Bush’s approval ratings temporarily. However, economic recession and domestic concerns—exacerbated by increased federal deficits from the war and earlier tax increases—dampened political capital, contributing to Bush’s 1992 electoral defeat.

**Aftermath**

* **Kuwait Reconstruction:** Coalition nations contributed to Kuwait’s postwar reconstruction and environmental remediation after oil well fires.
* **Long-Term Sanctions:** Sanctions remained in place until 2003, affecting Iraqi civilians and leading to ongoing humanitarian debates.
* **Establishment of No-Fly Zones:** Coalition-enforced no-fly zones over northern and southern Iraq aimed to protect Kurds and Shiites, setting precedents for subsequent humanitarian interventions.

---

The Gulf War’s timeline underscores a pivotal moment in late 20th-century geopolitics: the assertion of a post–Cold War international order, the validation of multilateral action under UN authority, and the demonstration of technological advances in warfare. It also foreshadowed future debates over intervention, reconstruction, and the long-term impact of sanctions on civilian populations.
