**National Defence of the Autonomous Province of Western Bosnia** was the military force established to defend the unrecognized entity of the Autonomous Province of Western Bosnia (APWB) during the Bosnian War (1992–1995). Under the political leadership of Fikret Abdić, the APWB sought autonomy from the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina, and its National Defence units played a central role in both local security and broader regional dynamics.

## Historical and Political Background

In the summer of 1993, amid the fragmentation of Bosnian state authority, Fikret Abdić—a prominent businessman and former vice-president of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina—declared the creation of the APWB around the city of Velika Kladuša. He rallied local support by promising economic stability and protection from the violence engulfing Sarajevo and other urban centers. The APWB’s political program emphasized local self-government and distancing from the Bosnian central government, which it accused of mismanaging resources and failing to protect Muslim communities in the northwest.

## Formation and Leadership

Shortly after proclaiming autonomy, Abdić organized a paramilitary force under the banner of National Defence (in Bosnian, **Narodna Odbrana**). Recruitment drew heavily from local farmers, former Yugoslav People’s Army reservists, and supporters of Abdić’s Agrokomerc company. Command structures were closely tied to the APWB’s civilian administration: Fikret Abdić held supreme authority, with his brother Senad Abdić and longtime associate Enver Hadžihasanović occupying senior military-administrative roles. The command was relatively small—numbering between 4,000 and 6,000 troops at its peak—but benefited from intimate knowledge of local terrain and a motivated recruitment base.

## Organizational Structure and Equipment

The National Defence was organized into territorial battalions centered on Velika Kladuša, Cazin, and Bužim. Each battalion maintained light infantry companies supported by improvised artillery (captured or locally manufactured) and a modest armored component comprising outdated APCs and a handful of T-34 tanks. Logistics relied heavily on resources controlled by Agrokomerc, including food supplies, local manufacturing facilities for munitions, and financial backing derived from company profits. Training standards varied, with some fighters receiving formal instruction in Bosnian army camps before defecting to the APWB.

## Military Role and Operations

From its inception in August 1993, the National Defence focused primarily on securing the APWB’s borders against incursions by the Army of the Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina (ARBiH). Clashes were frequent along the “Velika Kladuša corridor,” a narrow strip of territory linking the APWB to Croatian-held areas. In late 1993 and early 1994, ARBiH offensives aimed to dismantle Abdić’s breakaway province; National Defence units mounted a staunch defense, repelling several attacks and even conducting counter-raids that disrupted ARBiH supply lines.

## Alliances and External Support

Politically isolated by Sarajevo, the APWB turned to the Army of Republika Srpska (VRS) and Croatian Defence Council (HVO) for tacit support. While there was no formal alliance, Serb forces occasionally tolerated APWB checkpoints on supply routes, and Croatia refrained from intervening against Abdić to maintain a buffer zone. These pragmatic arrangements underscored the convoluted nature of Bosnian wartime alliances, which transcended simple ethnic or ideological divides in pursuit of local strategic interests.

## Relations with the Bosnian Government

The central Bosnian government branded Abdić’s movement as treasonous. ARBiH operations against the APWB were justified as efforts to preserve territorial integrity and prevent secession. Propaganda on both sides painted the conflict as between loyal patriots and collaborators: the APWB accused Sarajevo of neglect and authoritarianism, while the Bosnian government condemned Abdić’s forces as mercenaries allied with Serb aggressors. The dispute exacerbated divisions within the Bosniak community, leading to fratricidal violence that undercut broader national unity.

## Collapse and Reintegration

In August 1995, during Operation Storm in Croatia, the balance shifted decisively. Croatian and ARBiH forces coordinated strikes to dismantle remaining Serb strongholds and their local allies. The APWB’s corridor to Croatia was severed, cutting off vital supplies. Facing encirclement, National Defence units disintegrated rapidly; many fighters surrendered or fled into Croatia. By the end of August 1995, the APWB was dissolved and reintegrated into the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina. Fikret Abdić fled abroad but later returned to face charges of war crimes and collaboration.

## Legacy and Historical Significance

The National Defence of the APWB remains a controversial chapter in Bosnian history. To supporters in northwestern Bosnia, the force symbolized regional autonomy and self-protection in a time of national crisis. To critics, it represented betrayal that weakened Bosniak unity and facilitated Serb territorial gains. Its existence complicated postwar reconciliation, contributing to lingering mistrust between communities formerly within the APWB and their neighbors.

## Conclusion

The National Defence of the Autonomous Province of Western Bosnia illustrates how localized political ambitions and economic networks can give rise to distinct military formations within larger conflicts. Operating at the intersection of ethnic war, internal dissent, and international intervention, it underscores the fragmented nature of the Bosnian War and the challenges of maintaining cohesive resistance against external aggression. Its rapid rise and fall reflect both the possibilities and perils of seeking autonomy amid broader struggles over statehood, sovereignty, and communal survival.
