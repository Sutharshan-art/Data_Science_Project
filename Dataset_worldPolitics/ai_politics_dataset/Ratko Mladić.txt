**Ratko Mladić**

**Early Life and Military Education**
Ratko Mladić was born on March 12, 1942, in Božanovići, a village in what was then the Independent State of Croatia (now part of Bosnia and Herzegovina). He was the eldest of four children in a Serb family. After attending primary school locally, Mladić enrolled in the Yugoslav People’s Army (JNA) military academy in Belgrade in 1960. He graduated as an infantry officer in 1965 and went on to complete advanced training at the JNA Command and Staff School. His early postings included service in various infantry and armored units, where he rose steadily through the ranks and developed a reputation for strict discipline and aggressive tactics.

**Rise Through the Yugoslav People’s Army**
During the 1970s and 1980s, Mladić held a series of command and staff positions within the JNA. He served as an instructor at the military academy in Belgrade and later commanded a tank brigade in Banja Luka. Promoted to lieutenant colonel in 1977 and colonel by 1985, he was appointed head of the JNA’s training and doctrine department in 1988. By the time of Yugoslavia’s breakup in the early 1990s, Mladić had attained the rank of major general and was regarded as one of the army’s most capable field officers.

**Formation of the Army of Republika Srpska**
In April 1992, following Bosnia and Herzegovina’s declaration of independence, Mladić was appointed commander of the newly established Army of Republika Srpska (VRS), the military arm of the self-proclaimed Bosnian Serb entity. His selection drew on his JNA experience and his perceived loyalty to Serb nationalist leadership, notably Radovan Karadžić and Biljana Plavšić. Mladić swiftly organized the disparate Serb paramilitary groups, reservists, and segments of former JNA units into a cohesive fighting force tasked with securing Serb-held territories in Bosnia.

**Strategic Objectives and Military Campaigns**
Under Mladić’s command, the VRS pursued a multi-pronged strategy aimed at linking Serb-controlled areas in eastern and western Bosnia, establishing secure lines of communication to Serbia, and denying territory to Bosniak and Croat forces. The campaign combined conventional offensives with siege tactics, most notably in Sarajevo, and systematic efforts to displace non-Serb populations. Mladić coordinated closely with political authorities in Pale, where the Republika Srpska presidency was based, ensuring military operations dovetailed with efforts to create an ethnically homogeneous Serb state.

**Siege of Sarajevo**
From April 1992 until February 1996, Sarajevo—Bosnia’s capital and a multiethnic symbol of Yugoslavia—endured the longest siege in modern European history. Mladić’s forces encircled the city, positioned artillery and snipers on surrounding hills, and conducted regular bombardments and sniper fire against civilians. The siege inflicted severe humanitarian hardship, causing thousands of deaths, widespread destruction of infrastructure, and chronic shortages of food, water, and medical supplies. International outcry over the suffering in Sarajevo galvanized UN humanitarian missions but failed to halt VRS operations.

**Srebrenica Massacre**
In July 1995, Mladić directed the assault on the UN-designated “safe area” of Srebrenica in eastern Bosnia. Following the enclave’s fall on July 11, 1995, VRS units separated Bosniak men and boys from women and children and executed an estimated 8,000 males over several days. The killings represented the worst massacre on European soil since World War II and were later classified as genocide by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY). Mladić’s role in planning and overseeing the operation became central to subsequent war crimes prosecutions.

**Indictment and Years as a Fugitive**
In November 1995, the ICTY issued an indictment against Mladić for genocide, crimes against humanity, and violations of the laws of war. Despite being a high-profile fugitive, he remained at large—reportedly sheltered by local authorities in Republika Srpska—and continued to serve as VRS commander until the Dayton Peace Accords were signed in December 1995. Mladić disappeared from public view in early 1996 and evaded capture for more than a decade, living under assumed identities in Serbia and Bosnia.

**Arrest, Trial, and Conviction**
On May 26, 2011, Mladić was arrested near Belgrade by Serbian special forces acting on intelligence cooperation with international authorities. Transferred to The Hague, he stood trial at the ICTY beginning in May 2012. The prosecution presented extensive evidence of his direct involvement in the siege of Sarajevo, the Srebrenica genocide, and other acts of ethnic cleansing. In November 2017, the Tribunal convicted Mladić on ten counts, including genocide, extermination, murder, and persecution, and sentenced him to life imprisonment. His appeal was rejected in June 2021, affirming the original conviction and sentence.

**Legacy and Historical Assessment**
Ratko Mladić remains one of the most controversial figures of the Yugoslav wars. In Bosnia and Herzegovina, he is widely reviled by Bosniak and Croat communities as the principal architect of some of the conflict’s gravest atrocities. Among certain Serb nationalist circles, however, he is still celebrated by some as a defender of Serb interests, although public veneration has been legally curtailed by Bosnian authorities. Mladić’s prosecution and conviction have been cited as pivotal in the development of international criminal law, reinforcing the principle that military commanders can be held individually responsible for war crimes and genocide.
