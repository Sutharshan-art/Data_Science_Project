**Cold War (Doctor Who)**

*"Cold War"* is the eighth episode of the seventh series of the British science fiction television programme *Doctor Who*, first broadcast on **13 April 2013** on **BBC One**. Written by **Mark Gatiss** and directed by **Douglas Mackinnon**, the episode features the **Eleventh Doctor** (portrayed by **Matt Smith**) and companion **Clara Oswald** (played by **Jenna-Louise Coleman**) as they encounter a revived **Ice Warrior**, a classic alien species from the *Doctor Who* franchise, aboard a Soviet submarine during the height of the Cold War in **1983**.

While primarily a science fiction narrative, the episode makes explicit use of **Cold War geopolitical tension**, **nuclear deterrence**, and **military paranoia**, grounding its plot in the real-world fears of the late 20th-century superpower rivalry between the **United States** and the **Soviet Union**.

---

### Plot Summary

Set during **March 1983**, *Cold War* follows the TARDIS mistakenly materializing aboard a sinking Soviet submarine in the **North Pole**. The submarine’s crew has recovered a mysterious creature encased in ice, intending to present it as a triumph to Moscow. However, the thawed being is revealed to be **Skaldak**, a Martian **Ice Warrior** separated from his people for over 5,000 years.

Believing himself to be held captive and dishonored, Skaldak prepares to launch the submarine’s nuclear missiles in a retaliatory strike, potentially sparking a global conflict. The Doctor attempts to mediate, appealing to Skaldak’s sense of honor and restraint. The situation is defused when Skaldak is extracted by his fellow Ice Warriors at the last moment, sparing Earth from annihilation.

---

### Historical and Political Context

Although *Doctor Who* is a fantasy-based science fiction series, *Cold War* roots itself in one of the most perilous real-world political climates of the 20th century. The story is explicitly situated during the **early 1980s**, a period of heightened tension between the **Western bloc** (led by the United States under **President Ronald Reagan**) and the **Eastern bloc** (led by the Soviet Union under **Yuri Andropov**).

Key political elements referenced or alluded to in the episode include:

* **Nuclear deterrence**: The submarine is armed with nuclear warheads, and the threat of their activation becomes a central point of suspense. This reflects the broader doctrine of **Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD)** that defined superpower strategy during the Cold War.
* **Soviet military secrecy**: The submarine setting evokes the clandestine nature of Soviet operations, including real-world parallels such as the **K-129** and **K-219** submarine incidents.
* **Paranoia and mistrust**: The crew's immediate suspicion of the Doctor and Clara, as well as their defensiveness toward Skaldak, reflect Cold War-era fear of infiltration, espionage, and ideological subversion.

Through its science fiction framework, the episode mirrors the existential anxieties of a bipolar world, where misunderstandings and military brinkmanship could lead to global catastrophe.

---

### Production and Development

Mark Gatiss, a long-time *Doctor Who* contributor, intended *Cold War* to be both a **base-under-siege** story and a revival of the classic **Ice Warriors**, who had not appeared in the televised series since 1974. The setting on a cramped nuclear submarine paid homage to films such as *The Hunt for Red October* (1990) and *Crimson Tide* (1995), themselves Cold War thrillers deeply embedded in political narrative.

The episode was filmed on specially constructed submarine sets, with practical effects used to create the claustrophobic tension. Notably, Skaldak is portrayed both in traditional suit form and, in a departure from previous depictions, partially removed from his armor, allowing for new insights into Ice Warrior physiology and psychology.

---

### Reception and Analysis

*Cold War* was well-received by critics and viewers alike, praised for its atmospheric tension, pacing, and intelligent reintroduction of the Ice Warriors. The decision to place the Doctor in a politically charged setting—one grounded in recent history—added a layer of realism to the science fiction narrative.

Thematically, the episode is notable for its anti-militarist tone, a recurring feature in *Doctor Who*. The Doctor consistently advocates for **peaceful resolution**, challenging both the Soviet crew and Skaldak’s notions of honor and vengeance. In doing so, the episode underscores the broader **human cost of ideological warfare**, using science fiction as an allegorical tool for exploring Cold War anxieties.

Academic and critical commentary has noted that *Cold War* functions as a “morality play” within the historical context of **nuclear brinkmanship**, emphasizing restraint, diplomacy, and empathy over violence and retaliation.

---

### Legacy

As part of *Doctor Who*’s long-standing tradition of blending science fiction with political and historical themes, *Cold War* stands out for its direct engagement with **20th-century international relations**. The episode serves as a reminder of the cultural and psychological imprint of the Cold War, while also showcasing the capacity of speculative storytelling to reflect on real-world issues.

It also reinforced the Ice Warriors’ place in the *Doctor Who* mythos, allowing for more nuanced portrayals of formerly one-dimensional adversaries and expanding the show’s narrative toolkit for exploring themes of war, peace, and diplomacy.

---

### Conclusion

*Cold War* (Doctor Who) exemplifies the series’ use of historical settings to explore moral and political dilemmas. By placing extraterrestrial conflict aboard a Soviet nuclear submarine during one of the tensest periods of the Cold War, the episode uses its sci-fi premise to reflect on real-world geopolitical tension, the ethics of deterrence, and the perennial risks of miscommunication and militarism in an era of global stakes.
