**“Not One Inch”**

**“Not One Inch”** refers to a diplomatic assurance allegedly given by senior Western officials, notably U.S. Secretary of State James Baker and President George H. W. Bush, to Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev in 1990–1991, that the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) would not expand “one inch eastward” beyond reunified Germany. The phrase encapsulates enduring controversies over NATO enlargement, the post–Cold War European security order, and Western–Russian relations.

## Historical Context

In late 1989, the collapse of communist regimes across Central and Eastern Europe, culminating in the fall of the Berlin Wall (9 November 1989), created a security reconfiguration opportunity. German reunification became central: West Germany (then a NATO member) sought to incorporate the German Democratic Republic (GDR), while the Soviet Union—under Gorbachev—focused on securing the withdrawal of Soviet forces and ensuring that a unified Germany would not pose a renewed threat.

## Diplomatic Assurances

Between February and April 1990, U.S. Secretary of State James Baker, along with President George H. W. Bush and National Security Advisor Brent Scowcroft, held high‐level discussions with Gorbachev and Soviet Foreign Minister Eduard Shevardnadze. In these exchanges, Baker declared that NATO would not advance “not one inch eastward” from its then‐position, meaning that NATO’s jurisdiction would extend no further east than the former East Germany’s western border.

Key moments include:

* **9 February 1990**: At a meeting in Bonn, Baker told Gorbachev that NATO’s jurisdiction would not expand to the east “not one inch,” a formulation intended to reassure Soviet fears.
* **17 February 1990**: During a Washington summit, President Bush repeated that NATO would not move “one inch eastward” if the Soviet Union agreed to German reunification within NATO.
* **31 May 1990**: In Moscow talks, assurances were reiterated, linking non‐expansion to Soviet concessions.

## Legal Status and Interpretations

These assurances were made verbally and informal, without incorporation into the formal **Two Plus Four Treaty** (Treaty on the Final Settlement with Respect to Germany, signed 12 September 1990). The final treaty guaranteed that unified Germany would remain a NATO member but did not explicitly prohibit future NATO enlargement into Eastern Europe.

Western governments assert that discussions focused narrowly on East Germany’s territory and that no commitment was made regarding other Warsaw Pact countries. Soviet and later Russian officials, including Vladimir Putin, have argued that Western leaders reneged on clear promises, viewing subsequent NATO expansions (1999, 2004, and beyond) as breaches of trust.

## NATO Enlargement

Despite early assurances, NATO initiated its first post–Cold War enlargement in March 1999, admitting Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic. In 2004, it added seven more Eastern European states, followed by Bulgaria, Romania (2004), and the Baltic states—Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania (2004)—Ukraine and Georgia expressed aspirational ties, with limited partnership status. Each expansion triggered renewed Russian objections, framing NATO as an adversarial bloc encroaching on Russia’s historic sphere of influence.

## Political and Strategic Debates

### Western Perspectives

Proponents of enlargement argue it extended stability, democracy, and security guarantees to post‐communist states fearful of Russian domination. They emphasize sovereign rights of states to choose alliances and view NATO’s open‐door policy as codified in Article 10 of the North Atlantic Treaty. Western policymakers assert no formal legal barrier prevented enlargement and that the early assurances covered only unified Germany.

### Russian Perspectives

Russian leaders and analysts maintain that verbal assurances created legitimate expectations that NATO would not move eastward. They contend that NATO’s expansion violated the spirit of the **Jalta–Postdam security order**, undermined European security architecture, and contributed to a resurgent Russian nationalism under Putin. Russia’s 2008 invasion of Georgia and 2014 annexation of Crimea have been partly justified by Russian claims of NATO encroachment.

## Scholarly Assessments

Academic literature offers varied interpretations. Some scholars question the clarity and scope of Baker’s “not one inch” phrase, noting the absence of written commitments and the narrow focus on East Germany. Others argue that even informal assurances can shape trust and international norms. Studies in international relations highlight how miscommunication and divergent strategic cultures fueled reciprocal security dilemmas between NATO and Russia.

## Legacy and Contemporary Relevance

The “not one inch” debate remains central in discussions of European security and the causes of post–2014 Russo‐Western confrontations. It features in political rhetoric, documentary films (e.g., “The Promise of NATO” analyses), and foreign‐policy commentaries. In 2021–2022, as Russia massed troops near Ukraine, Russian officials cited broken promises to justify demands for legally binding NATO non‐expansion, contributing to the diplomatic fallout preceding Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine.

## Conclusion

“No one inch” has become shorthand for contested narratives about the post–Cold War order. While Western actors emphasize legalities and sovereign choice, Russian actors highlight the normative weight of diplomatic assurances. The phrase encapsulates enduring tensions over alliance expansion, national security, and the interpretation of historical commitments—questions that continue to shape Euro‐Atlantic politics and the prospects for a stable, cooperative European security architecture.
