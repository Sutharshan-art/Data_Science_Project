**George Herbert Walker Bush** (June 12, 1924 – November 30, 2018) was an American statesman and Republican Party leader who served as the 41st President of the United States from 1989 to 1993. His long public career spanned military service in World War II, diplomatic posts, two terms as a U.S. Representative, Chairman of the Republican National Committee, U.S. Ambassador to the United Nations and to the People’s Republic of China, Director of Central Intelligence, and two terms as Vice President under Ronald Reagan (1981–1989).

**Early Life and World War II**
Born in Milton, Massachusetts, to U.S. Senator Prescott Bush and Dorothy Walker Bush, Bush grew up in an influential political family. He attended Phillips Academy in Andover, Massachusetts, before enlisting as a naval aviator shortly after his 18th birthday. Flying torpedo bombers in the Pacific theater, he became one of the youngest U.S. naval aviators and was awarded the Distinguished Flying Cross after being shot down in 1944.

**Postwar Education and Business Career**
After the war, Bush graduated from Yale University in 1948 with a degree in economics, where he captained the baseball team. Moving to Texas, he joined the burgeoning oil industry, co-founding the Zapata Petroleum Corporation in 1953. His success in the oil business established his reputation as a self-made entrepreneur and provided a financial base for his entry into politics.

**Entry into Electoral Politics**
In 1964, Bush won a seat in the U.S. House of Representatives from Texas’s 7th district, where he served two terms (1967–1971). He championed moderate Republican positions, supporting civil-rights legislation and increased federal aid for education and infrastructure. After narrowly losing a 1970 Senate race, he was appointed U.S. Ambassador to the United Nations (1971–1973) by President Richard Nixon, and subsequently served as Chief of the U.S. Liaison Office in China (1974–1975), helping to normalize relations with the People’s Republic.

**Central Intelligence and the Republican National Committee**
In 1976, President Gerald Ford named Bush Director of the Central Intelligence Agency. He modernized analytical capabilities and helped oversee covert support for anti-communist movements. Following the 1976 election, Bush chaired the Republican National Committee (1973–1974), rebuilding party finances and infrastructure in the aftermath of Watergate.

**Vice Presidency under Reagan**
Selected by Ronald Reagan as his running mate in 1980, Bush served two terms as Vice President. He undertook diplomatic missions to Africa, Poland, and the Middle East, and led initiatives on drug interdiction and election monitoring in Nicaragua. Despite policy differences—particularly on tax cuts—he remained a loyal partner in advancing supply-side economics and a strong anti-Soviet defense posture.

**Presidential Campaign and Election of 1988**
Running on the slogan “A Kinder, Gentler Nation,” Bush secured the Republican nomination in 1988. He defeated Democrat Michael Dukakis in a campaign that emphasized foreign policy experience and painted Dukakis as soft on crime. Bush won with 426 Electoral College votes and a 53–46 percent popular-vote margin.

**Presidency (1989–1993)**
Bush’s presidency was marked by significant foreign-policy achievements and domestic challenges:

* **End of the Cold War**: Bush skillfully managed U.S. relations with Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, supporting German reunification and the dissolution of the Warsaw Pact without military conflict. He rallied Western and former Communist states in a coordinated response to the collapse of Soviet authority.
* **Operation Desert Storm**: After Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait in August 1990, Bush assembled an unprecedented U.N. coalition and secured congressional approval for military intervention. The liberation of Kuwait in February 1991 garnered widespread domestic and international approval.
* **Domestic Economy and Tax Policy**: Facing a growing budget deficit, Bush famously agreed to raise taxes in his 1990 budget despite his “no new taxes” pledge, seeking bipartisan fiscal responsibility. The decision, coupled with a mild recession (1990–1991), eroded his political standing.
* **Americans with Disabilities Act (1990)**: Bush signed landmark civil-rights legislation prohibiting discrimination against individuals with disabilities and extending accessibility requirements in public accommodations, employment, and transportation.
* **Education and Environment**: He championed the America 2000 education initiative and signed the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990, strengthening regulation of acid rain, urban air pollution, and toxic emissions.

**1992 Election and Transition**
Economic concerns dominated the 1992 campaign. Facing a strong challenge from Democrat Bill Clinton and independent Ross Perot, Bush lost re-election, garnering 168 Electoral College votes to Clinton’s 370. He facilitated a smooth transition and, upon leaving office, maintained high approval ratings for his foreign-policy successes.

**Post-Presidential Activities and Legacy**
After 1993, Bush remained active in public service and philanthropy. He chaired a commission on U.S.–Russian relations, supported aid for wounded veterans through the Wounded Warriors Project, and partnered with Bill Clinton on global humanitarian initiatives, including disaster relief and malaria prevention. His memoirs and numerous public appearances solidified his reputation as a statesman above partisan divides.

Bush’s presidency is remembered for its deft navigation of the Cold War’s end and the Gulf War coalition, as well as for its willingness to compromise on fiscal matters. His decision to raise taxes, though politically costly, is often cited as a demonstration of principle over expedience. As the patriarch of a political family—with son George W. Bush serving as the 43rd president—his career established a legacy of public service spanning nearly seven decades.
